### A.21. Executive summary (max. 750 words, approximately 1.5 pages)
Niger is a landlocked, Least Developed Country in West Africa with one of the highest rates of poverty in the world. The country’s economy, food security, and the livelihoods of its rural communities are extremely vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. Niger’s climate is already hot and dry with major fluctuation in rainfall across years. Increasing temperatures and increasing rainfall variability are having severe impacts on agriculture, which is the main source of income and livelihoods for 87% of Niger’s people. Mean annual rainfall has decreased in recent decades and dry spells have increased, while the onset and length of the rainfall season is variable. At the same time, rainfall intensity is increasing. Intense rainfall events in Niger have caused flooding that led to loss of life and major economic damages amounting to 15,240,615 Euros.

Food insecurity, poverty, and malnutrition are increasing in Niger because of the negative impacts of climate change on the agricultural sector. In rural areas, 3 million people (12% of the population) were food insecure in 2018 and a further 29% of people were at risk. Most agriculture in Niger is rainfed, but given the changing climate and often erratic rainfall, the ongoing expansion of irrigated agriculture is a government priority. Surface water resources are limited and under pressure from climate change and various users, but Niger also has significant groundwater resources that can be utilized sustainably for irrigation to boost agricultural production.

Niger has established Irrigated Agricultural Developments (Aménagements Hydro-Agricoles – AHA) which provide irrigated areas for farmer’s groups with government support. These Irrigated Agriculture Developments (AHAs) are affected by floods and droughts. Flooding damages fields and fills the open irrigation canals with silt, which reduces canal capacity and thus prevents irrigation water from reaching some fields. Intense rainfall events also cause soil erosion. As a result of these problems, only 70 out of 87 AHAs were operational in 2014. Out of a total of 8,704 ha under irrigation only 6,998 ha could be harvested, representing a loss of ~20% and 2,104,728 Euros during the agricultural season of 2015.

The proposed GCF project will address these climate change problems by: i) developing climate-resilient AHAs with solar-powered and water-efficient irrigation systems; ii) supporting the effective operation of AHAs and building the capacity of all stakeholders; and iii) promoting private sector financing for climate-smart agriculture. In this way, the project will enable rural communities and the agricultural sector to adapt to the adverse effects of climate change, as well as mitigating Green House Gas (GHG) emissions by using solar energy.

This project will be implemented in the regions of Tahoua, Agadez, Maradi, Diffa, and Zinder, which are among the most vulnerable to climate change of Niger’s seven regions. In total 1,000 hectares of new AHAs will be developed and 749 hectares of existing AHAs will be rehabilitated to be resilient against the climate change impacts of increasing temperatures, droughts, and floods. The AHAs will be cultivated by beneficiaries (women, men, youth) who will be organized in farmers’ mutual production groups which will constitute a farmer’s cooperative on each AHA. Solar-powered pumps will be installed at the AHAs, instead of the typically used pumps that are powered by diesel or electricity from the fossil-fueled national grid. In addition to irrigation, these pumps will also provide water for livestock and human consumption. The water-efficient irrigation technologies, such as drip irrigation, that will be installed will achieve significant water savings compared to the status quo. A total of 4.6 MW of solar power will be installed across the project sites.

A comprehensive set of interventions will ensure the effective operation of the AHAs, namely enhancing the regulatory environment, establishing strong management structures, and building the capacity of key stakeholders such as farmer’s groups, cooperatives, water user associations, and relevant government staff. The rural communities at each AHA will also be assisted to improve their access to markets and to undertake income-generating activities such as processing of agricultural products. Farmer’s groups, cooperatives, and Irrigation Water Users Associations will receive training and tools to use climate-resilient farming practices and efficient irrigation methods. Agricultural production and climate-resilience will also be enhanced by improving agro-meteorological forecasting and environmental monitoring, and by providing agro-meteorological information to farmers and early warnings to rural communities.

Given the large adaptation needs in Niger’s agricultural sector, it is essential to scale up these climate-smart ways of farming and irrigation. However, the agricultural sector in Niger faces challenges in accessing low-cost and long-term loan financing for climate-smart agriculture. To overcome these challenges, the proposed GCF project will create a favorable environment for such private financing by: i) building the capacity of the private financial sector to promote and scale innovative financing for climate-smart agriculture; and ii) setting up an attractive on-lending mechanism for climate-smart agriculture through local financial institutions in the form of an agricultural loan facility which fund will be provided by the GCF, which could be scaled up at a later stage, in the context of other projects, with the support of regional or international development financial institutions. This proposed Agricultural Loan Facility of 5.890 million Euros will act as a catalyst to crowd in further private and public financing for climate-smart agriculture in Niger.

This GCF project will benefit an estimated number of 121,615 people directly and one million people indirectly. The project’s direct beneficiaries will comprise: i) farming households at the Irrigated Agriculture Developments; ii) people who will benefit from income-generating activities such as processing agricultural products; iii) people who will benefit from capacity building; and iv) people who will receive agricultural products from project sites to support their food security. The indirect beneficiaries of the project will include the entire population of the villages benefiting from this project. The estimated one million people who live in these villages will benefit from awareness-raising activities, dissemination of good practices through guides and radio programs, and dissemination of agro-meteorological information. The project’s impact in terms of food security will include making available at least 6.6 tons of cereal equivalent each year. In terms of mitigation potential, the project will reduce GHG emissions by 11,176.3 tCO2e- per year or 227,551 t CO2e- for 25 years, by developing solar technology instead of fossil-fueled generators and water pumps.

This project is consistent with Niger's National Determined Contribution (NDC), particularly in terms of promoting climate-smart agriculture and considering adaptation, mitigation, and food security, while strengthening development at the grassroots level. The project will also contribute to the achievement of the results associated with the national priorities of the Agriculture Forestry and Other Land Use (AFOLU) sector, of water resources and capacity building of the actors at all levels identified in the NDC. The related benefits of the AFOLU sector are, in fact, constituted by the results of implementation and scaling up of CSA activities, namely strengthening of assisted natural regeneration, recovery of degraded land, improvement of the cereal and forage balance, and of food and nutritional security.

This warming accelerates the evapotranspiration and impacts negatively on the availability of surface water and the recharge of the groundwater resources, particularly the alluvial groundwater. According to Niger's third national communication on climate change, one of the current impacts of warming on surface water resources is a general decline in the flow of Niger and Komadougou Rivers. In the case of groundwater apart from its volume, timing and quality and recharge, the characteristics of the aquifer system is also affected. As evaporation rates increase with temperature rises, the availability of water for groundwater recharge from surface water sources and that left behind from rainfall events will be reduced. The resulting reductions in groundwater recharge, in combination with that related to meteorological droughts, will also be compounded by increased abstraction rates as the dependence of communities on groundwater resources increases because of temperature rises, resulting in increased domestic and agricultural water needs, and surface water sources are depleted. Consequently, the reduction of required volume of water needed for crop growth and livestock, reduce crop harvests and pasture availability, amplifying the impact of droughts or dry spells (Third National Communication, 2016).

## New Rainfall Variability Characterized by Intensive Rainfall and Dry Spells

Rainfall in Niger declined rapidly between 1950 and the mid-1980s and partially recovered during the 1990s and 2000s. An analysis of rainfall changes over the period 1961-2010 shows a significant decrease in rainfall since 1970 and which has continued until beginning of the 1990s, with a long deficit period between 1980 and 1990. The corresponding rainfall deficit is on average around 20%, but may exceed 30% in the west and centre; a clear tendency for isohyets to slide south up to 150 km. In addition, there is a slight increase of rainfall trend that began in 1990 in the Saharan and while the Sahel-Sudan zone shows that the rainfall is relatively constant over the same. Furthermore, observations indicate a decreasing rainfall variability towards the South and an upward trend in temperatures at all stations, with however, a moderate increase on the Niamey and Tahoua stations.

Between 2000 and 2009, the average rainfall in Niger’s crop growing districts was about 8 percent lower than the 1920–69 mean. The recent rainfall increases are probably due to of the northern Atlantic Ocean; as the northern tropical Atlantic has increased in temperature over this period, this has drawn the summer rains further north, increasing rainfall in the Sahel. These changes can be visualized in three ways: as an expansion of the region receiving adequate rainfall for viable agricultural livelihoods, as maps of anticipated changes in rainfall, and as time series plots.

On average, Niger’s climate used to alternate between wet and dry periods every seven years, during the period 1960-87. Since the beginning of the 1990s, a new mode of rainfall variability characterized by a succession of wet and dry years appears to have started in Niger. This evolution has continued in recent years under the effect of climate change. At the same time, there has been an overall decrease in average rainfall. The isohyets, lines on a map indicating mean annual rainfall for a given area, have migrated to the south in Niger as the country has become drier. The 600 mm isohyet which was north of Niamey in the years 1950-1967 is found completely in the south of Dosso after about two decades. The 800 mm isohyet completely disappeared from the Niger map around 1995, i.e., nowhere in Niger receives this amount of annual average rainfall anymore. ## Variability of the Onset and Length of Rainy Season

In Niger, the period 1991-2010 is characterized by relatively delay of rainy season onset, especially at Maradi and Zinder. For Tahoua and Maïné (Djiffa), early onsets of the season are observed but they are often associated with extreme dry spells and are considered as false onset situations. Broadly, the end of the growing season is relatively stable and shows no signs of significant change. Broadly, the lengths of seasons even if they tend to be extended for Tahoua, remain stable for Mainé, Zinder and Maradi.

In terms of climate change impacts on the agricultural season, under the emission scenario RCP4.5, the years with annual rainfall deficit compared to the normal annual rainfall over the period 1961-1990, will be predominant. The potential impacts of climate change related to this situation include a reduction in the length of the agricultural season combined with an increasing frequency of dry days during the agricultural season, resulting in changes to the growing, sowing and harvesting periods in Niger’s crop calendar. The onset of the rain season will be delayed with the majority of rainfall being concentrated in the late rain season, resulting in delayed planting, a shorter growing period and a late harvesting period relative to the current crop calendar.

Farmers in the project intervention area, in light of historical-observed and projected climate risk, are exposed to the increase of temperature, dry spell occurrence, heavy rainfall and disruption of the rainy season onset and are vulnerable to those impacts of climate change considering their actual adaptation techniques (rural exodus; mutual aid - goods and cropping materials or agricultural residues sales; displacement of populations for better land and pasture, the use of local short cycle varieties and farmer-managed natural regeneration of degraded land in Maradi and Zinder). The project intervention area is aligned with the most vulnerable area to climate change identified in the National Adaptation Programme of Action (NAPA), across the regions of Tahoua, Agadez, Maradi, Diffa and Zinder. These farmers, young people, and women are among the most vulnerable in those targeted area.

## Adaptation Needs

Climate change, which can be translated in the project intervention area by the rising of temperatures, the increase of dry spells and heavy rainfalls and variable regional trend of the onset and length of the rainy season, is affecting soil, and agriculture, a relevant sector for ensuring the food security of the population.

In Niger, the various types of soils are subject to degradation due to the adverse effects related to the rising temperatures and the hybrid character of the rainy season. The most visible soil degradation are, among others: (i) the progressive reduction of the plant cover (accentuated by human pressure from livelihood activities); (ii) the formation of crusts at the soil surface following the drying up of the topsoil layers and their induration by the precipitation of iron oxides and hydroxides; (iii) the reduction of rainwater infiltration; (iv) the acceleration of erosion; (v) flooding; (vi) soil depletion and salinization and (vi) the alteration of the balance of soil ecosystems. Those climate hazards induce silting of shallow lands and the reduction of land surface suitable for rice. At medium term, rice farmers need to undertake soil and water conservation actions for maintaining of soil quality and being able to practice efficient irrigation.

Broadly, the human pressure on land results from the expansion of crop fields in Niger, driven by the rapid population growth and the increasing demand for food. In Niger, the cultivated areas have increased by 12.6%, 18.1% and 24.5% respectively in 1975, 2000 and 2013. Particularly in Zinder and Maradi, the land already cultivated in 1975, is now a wall-to-wall homogeneous agricultural landscape where agriculture is still expanding eastward on the short grass Sahelian savannas. In order to combat land degradation due to extensive agriculture which uses unproducible extended areas more than the producible small area in the framework of the current project, since the 1980s, southern Niger smallholder farmers have experimented successfully with the farmer-managed natural regeneration technique which is currently practiced on ~3 million hectares of lands in Maradi and Zinder regions. In the case of Agadez, the reduction of land degradation strategies was focused on the implementation of sustainable land management practices on land identified as suitable for restoration.

Strong greening trends were reported in Tahoua and some studies have even estimated at 100% the increase in vegetation cover from 1982 to 1999. Indeed, marginal vegetation increases in valley bottoms and decreases higher in the landscape and according to some reports, this greening does not necessarily mean more trees planted in the landscape but is rather a consequence of the cropping systems intensification by farmers during the last two decades as a response to increasing population pressure. In other areas of this region, farmers have planted, protected and managed trees to increase woody cover as a response to the droughts of the 1970s and 1980s. Based on those experiences, our project emphasizes the upscaling of sustainable intensification of the cropping systems in irrigation areas (périmètre d'irrigation in French) with the climate resilient practices that have been experimented with success for reducing human pressure on land (farmer-managed natural regeneration, sustainable soil and water management practices…).

Agriculture, the main activity of the Niger's rural population is essentially rain-fed and is therefore highly vulnerable to climate change impacts. The potential impacts of climate change linked to increasing temperatures, increasing dry spells and heavy rain events occurrence, and a spatiotemporal variability of rainy season onset and length are the following: (i) water shortage to meet crop water requirements during crop cycles; (ii) outbreaks of pests such as leaf miner caterpillars of fall armyworm, during dry spells at heading or flowering stage; and (iii) the decrease and/or total loss of crop production.

In terms of pests, leaf miner caterpillar outbreaks are expected to become more frequent with the increasing length of dry spells under climate change conditions as their appearance is stimulated by several consecutive dry days. Fall armyworm outbreaks are also expected to increase in frequency as temperatures rise, resulting in faster insect development, shorter lifecycles and a greater number of pest generations. Consequently, pest management is a growing adaptation need in Niger’s agricultural areas.

Many studies are consistent with predicting yield reductions in the near future under the current changing climate. Millet/Sorghum will decrease by more than 10% in the case of a 2°C increase in temperature and insignificant variations in rainfall by 2050. An increase of 3°C will lead to a decrease in agricultural yields of around 15 to 25%. For rice, the projections under 1.5°C of warming shows a drastically reduction of grain yield by 15% in Maradi region and by 5.5% to 15% in Zinder region. The map on the right hand-side shows the potential relative increase in yield if additional irrigation is applied on present-day harvested areas with climate change, assuming irrigation water is not limiting.

Achieving food security goals in West Africa, depends on the capacity of the agricultural sector to feed the rapidly growing population and to moderate the adverse impacts of climate change. But according to the NAPA, the agricultural production surplus, which covered 86% of food needs until the early 1970s, has become structurally in deficit since the end of the 1980s until the present day due to climate disruptions. Recently, Niger has imported more than 80% of its rice consumption. Severe food insecurity persists and is affecting an increasing number of households. From 2015 to 2018, the population under severe food insecurity more than doubled, from 1.1% in 2015 to 2.8%.

## Climate Change and Food Insecurity

As mentioned above, the adverse effects of climate have extremely negative impacts on production and production factors. In addition to this is the high cost of energy. All this contributes to food insecurity and erodes farmers' incomes. ## Problem Statement

Over recent years, Niger has experienced a significant increase in food insecurity. The food secure population decreased by 3.3%, while those at risk of food insecurity rose from 25.8% to 29.1%. Severe food insecurity more than doubled from 1.1% to 2.6%.

### Climate Change Impact

Forecasts indicate that climate change will exacerbate food insecurity in Niger. The NAPA outlines potential impacts such as reduced agricultural seasons, increased dry days, insufficient water for crops, pest appearances, and decreased agricultural production.

1. **Salack et al, 2008**: Historical data from 1961-1990 showed a reduction in millet growth cycles by 2-3 days by 2020 and 4-5 days by 2050, with a yield reduction of 4.6%.
2. **Statistical Study**: Predicted a 13% drop in millet yields by 2025 due to rising temperatures.
3. **Logistic Regression Model**: Attributed up to 42% of millet yield variability to rainfall.

### Future Projections

Studies by Sarr et al (2007) and Agrhymet (2009) predict continued yield decreases, with a 10% decline in millet/sorghum yields with a 2°C temperature rise by 2050. A 3°C increase could lead to a 15-25% yield decrease.

# Independent Technical Advisory Panel’s assessment of FP176

**Proposal name:** Hydro-agricultural development with smart agriculture practices resilient to climate change in Niger  
**Accredited entity:** Banque Ouest Africaine de Développement (BOAD)  
**Country/(ies):** Niger  
**Project/programme size:** Medium

## I. Assessment of the independent Technical Advisory Panel

### 1.1 Impact potential  
**Scale: High**

1. This funding proposal for a Medium-sized Cross-cutting project, in environmental and social safeguards Category B, is submitted by the Republic of the Niger (otherwise known as Niger), an African State with least developed country (LDC) status. The total cost of the funding proposal is EUR 45.54 million, including a EUR 30.14 million Green Climate Fund (GCF) grant contribution, which forms 66 per cent of the total cost. The remaining EUR 15.4 million, or 34 per cent of the total project cost, is made up of grant contributions from the Government of Niger (EUR 6.16 million), and concessional loan finance from BOAD – the Banque Ouest Africaine de Développement, the Accredited Entity (EUR 9.24 million).

2. The independent Technical Advisory Panel (TAP) assesses the impact potential of this cross-cutting project as HIGH, including both mitigation and adaptation aspects. In the past three decades, climate change has increased the frequency and intensity of drought and flooding in Niger, with the five project regions of Tahoua, Agadez, Maradi, Diffa and Zinder particularly vulnerable to these growing hazards. Many of these regions’ Irrigated Agricultural Developments (Aménagements Hydro-Agricoles or “AHAs”), vital to food security through irrigated staple crops rice, millet, maize and cowpea, are being increasingly damaged by siltation from flooding. Increased temperatures, combined with an overall declining trend in total annual rainfall and a more erratic pattern, are increasing evapotranspiration and causing drought periods, which means it is increasingly important to expand the area under irrigation. The low-emissions, climate-resilient development pathway for this expansion involves using boreholes for sustainable extraction of groundwater and flood-proofed improved irrigation systems for water and energy efficiency, powered by renewable energy technology.

3. The project aims to contribute to the increase of national agricultural production by strengthening the resilience of populations to the adverse effects of climate change, through three components of work:
   - **Component 1** involves the expansion and climate-proofing of the AHAs against siltation (caused by flooding intensified by climate change) in sites with untapped groundwater. This is extracted on a sustainable basis using water-efficient drip irrigation schemes with solar-PV powered pumps (instead of high-emissions diesel-powered pumps). An area of 749 hectares of AHAs will be rehabilitated, and 1,000 new hectares of irrigated plots will be developed. Flood control mechanisms will be put in place, constructing embankments and channels for drainage and aquifer recharge, and structures and revegetation against erosion.
   - **Component 2** involves maximizing the productivity of climate-smart agriculture (CSA) in the irrigated areas of the AHAs, through land governance in which plots are allocated, beneficiaries’ rights are registered and cooperatives are strengthened. Improved climate-resilient farming will be promoted, using agro-climatological information services to farmers, and supply of inputs including drought-resilient seeds. Operation and maintenance and monitoring systems will be set up for irrigation and pumping equipment. Technical assistance will be provided for crop preservation, value addition, warehousing and market access, strengthening value chains for the produce of the AHAs.
   - **Component 3** involves developing capacity to sustain CSA and irrigation, including mainstreaming climate risk management with officials and producers; developing capacity of local financial institutions; and lessons learning and knowledge sharing for scale-up outside the project regions. Output 3.3, managed directly by BOAD as Executing Entity (EE), will enhance access to finance for climate-smart farming and value chain development, through establishing an Agricultural Loan Facility at BOAD. The facility, the details of which still need to be worked out, will on-lend to carefully selected local financial institutions who in turn will make affordable loans to producer and processing cooperatives.

4. **Mitigation impact:** The cross-cutting project is expected to have a modest but significant impact through GHG emissions avoided as a result of the 23 newly established and expanded AHAs being based on appropriately sized solar photo-voltaic (PV) power systems, in contrast to a without-project scenario in which irrigation schemes would have continued to expand using over-capacitated and high-emissions diesel generators for pumping water. It is anticipated that the project interventions will achieve both better energy efficiency and a clean energy path through switching of fuels. This will result in total avoided emissions of 227,551 tons of CO2-equivalent, i.e., 11,176.3 tCO2-equ. per year over the 25-year lifetime of the project. The basis for using CDM methodology ASM-I-F and the calculations are set out in full in Annex 22, together with monitoring and reporting arrangements, which involve equipping 10% of the PV systems with energy meters. Responsibility for monitoring of the energy generation and avoided emissions is taken by the NDA, the National Commission for Environment and Sustainable Development (CNEDD), with collection of data by the EE, ONAHA (the National Office of Hydro Agricultural Development), through its Director of Infrastructure of Materials and Workrooms.

5. The common practice in Niger of using diesel-generated pumps to extract water from boreholes results in high emissions of CO2, NO2 and particulate matter, and causes air pollution. The independent TAP welcomes the shift through the project’s investment to a clean and renewable energy pathway, given the importance for the country of maximizing sustainable groundwater extraction, in line with the Ministry of Agriculture’s Strategy for small-scale irrigation systems (SPIN). This investment, whilst costly, avoids the highly undesirable alternative of locking in a new generation of high-emission infrastructure, since diesel pumps have a lifetime of 20-25 years. The investment simultaneously increases the number of farming households with access to low-emission energy for purposes such as mobile phone charging, charging lighting systems and other portable battery-based appliances. The project also intends to help to remove barriers to scale-up of this low-emission energy, through provision of access to finance, though this is not without its challenges (see Paradigm shift section).

6. The independent TAP notes that, whilst this shift from fossil fuel-based to renewable energy source for powering borehole pumps is wholly positive in terms of mitigation impact, such a shift can pose the risk of potential maladaptation. Once the upfront capital is invested in the solar PV systems, the constraint on pumping that was previously posed by the cost of the diesel fuel is removed, and if this is combined with inadequate water governance mechanisms, there is potential for overextraction of groundwater – see section below on “Risks of maladaptation”, for clarification on how this risk has been adequately addressed by the proponents in the design of this project.

7. **Adaptation impact:** The project will help agricultural communities in the five most vulnerable regions of Niger to adapt to the negative effects of climate change – reducing their vulnerability to flood and drought through investment in efficient, resilient irrigation systems, and enhancing their adaptive capacity through support to climate-smart agriculture (CSA), and strengthening of value chains for CSA produce. Meteorological trends recorded in recent decades in these five regions of Niger on key climate parameters are aligned with future projections, and include increasing average temperatures, later onset of the rainy season, and more dry spells during the rainy season – all of which combine to cause increased evapotranspiration and loss of soil moisture, placing stress on staple crop yields, especially where irrigation is unavailable or inadequate. In addition, the project helps these communities adapt to a growing frequency of intense rainfall events – as shown by trends recorded in recent decades, aligned with future projections – which cause flash floods and soil erosion that combine to cause silting up of irrigation and drainage channels (rendering 20% of the AHA system unusable in the five project regions after the last major floods).

8. The project will rehabilitate existing damaged AHA plots and irrigation schemes and install new ones, using the efficient California and drip irrigation systems in place of the old open-channel irrigation systems which had high evaporation losses (worsening with climate change). These interventions will both increase water availability for irrigation (drought resilience) and ensure that the new schemes are protected against damage and siltation (flood resilience). The newly irrigated areas will be able to have two, and in most cases three, agricultural growing seasons per year, since they will no longer be reliant on rainfall. In addition to enhancing water supply through the new boreholes, the improved efficiency of the new irrigation systems will mean that water loss through evaporation is significantly reduced, allowing an increase in actual water availability for rice cultivation, for example, from 60% to 85% – critical in the face of declining overall rainfall and seasonal shortages. The independent TAP also welcomes the integrated approach taken to drought and flood management – as seen in the construction of wide drainage channels to divert excess water during flash floods away from the AHA plots, and direct them into the downstream seasonal rivers or “koris”, from where the water can seep into the ground, recharging the aquifer.

9. The number of expected direct beneficiaries of the project is 121,615 people, made up as follows: (i) 56,056 people from farming households in the new AHAs; (ii) 41,895 people in the AHAs to be rehabilitated; (iii) 12,320 beneficiaries of the line of credit for private financial sector for scaling up; (iv) 10,920 beneficiaries of income-generating activities (39 groups with an average of 40 participating households each); and (v) 424 officials who will benefit from capacity-building activities. The number of indirect beneficiaries is estimated at 1 million, based on the total population in the villages, communes and regions benefiting from the project, who will purchase and/or consume the expanded produce of the AHAs; participate in awareness-raising activities; and receive agro-meteorological information, inputs on good practices, and radio programming. Through Component 2, the project will also lead to strengthening of institutional and regulatory systems for climate-responsive planning and development. The focus on use of agro-climatological information in crop planning will lead to an increase in generation and use of climate information in decision-making.

10. **Risk of maladaptation:** A significant risk in groundwater-based projects in general is that the introduction of solar PV technology for pumping (removing the former constraint of the cost of diesel), combined with inadequate water governance mechanisms, can allow overextraction leading to a groundwater deficit – when water is pumped out faster than the rate at which the aquifer is naturally replenished. This lowers the water table, eventually rendering the wellpoint unusable, or necessitating deeper “mining” of water that risks contamination by nitrates. As the water table drops, other wells in the area dry up, as do springs and ponds, with harmful effects on biodiversity and human health. In many regions of the world, multiple poorly sited wellpoints have also caused land subsidence, where the soil around the depleted aquifer collapses, compacts and drops, damaging assets and livelihoods.

11. Following questions posed to the Accredited Entity (AE) on why this risk was not highlighted and how it is being managed, the independent TAP was reassured, both on the process followed for selecting sites and determining extraction rates, and on the strong water governance mechanisms in place. The AE highlighted the site selection process outlined in the feasibility study, with thresholds used to ensure that only areas where remote sensing has indicated significant untapped potential were selected. The selection was based on full groundwater analyses for the Iullemeden and Chad Basin Aquifer Systems, allowing for cumulative impacts of other schemes, with the “Water estimation and water balance analysis” figures shown in Annex 28. The independent TAP notes the conclusion in the Feasibility Study that “the project will exert an acceptable level of pressure that is within the aquifer’s recharge capacities, even allowing for an ecological flow reserve”.

12. In response to questions from the independent TAP, the AE also highlighted that pumping will be allowed for no more than 5 hours per day, and that this will be controlled by relay timers in the pumping systems. The new boreholes will all be fitted with piezometers, to enable proactive and ongoing monitoring of groundwater pressure, to feed into wider monitoring of the impact of extraction on the groundwater resource. The project will also establish or strengthen and capacitate Irrigation Water Users Associations (AUEIs), which will be responsible for managing the pumps, and will sign contracts with the EE ONAHA that specify the constraints on pumping, and enforce sanctions if these are exceeded. The AE provided additional detail on the water governance mechanism, outlining the specific roles and responsibilities of each Irrigation Water User Association and of ONAHA, as set out in the operating contracts between the parties.

24. The implementation of the project will create direct jobs in the construction of the plots, sinking of boreholes and construction of flood defences around the perimeters. These jobs will mainly involve local labour and there will also be new indirect jobs around the sites for operations and maintenance, and supply and distribution channels for raw and semi-finished agricultural products. Approximately 12,000 farmers will benefit, not counting the labour for clearing, ploughing, sowing, maintenance, harvesting, etc. The project will enable the development of agricultural marketing chains as well as product processing and conservation activities. Enhanced yields and new income streams will be combined with savings on diesel costs to free up cash for operations and maintenance, and, for some cooperatives with capacity, to take loans to further expand production and processing facilities.

#### 1.3.4. Gender-sensitivity and social inclusion

25. The FP acknowledges the highly constrained socio-economic position of women in rural Niger, and the high rate of youth under- and unemployment, and states that “In the selection of direct beneficiaries, the gender criterion is included to give women, young people and adults the opportunity to participate fully in the project. Jobs will be created for women in sowing, maintenance, harvesting, threshing, marketing, etc. as well as for men. About 50% of direct and indirect beneficiaries will be women”. A thorough Gender Analysis and Action Plan outlines numerical targets to ensure women’s participation in decision-making structures, and their access to technical assistance and finance, but there seems to be a gap between the socio-economic realities and the ambitious targets in the plan, that is not fully addressed by the design of the project activities. For example, the Analysis notes that “Women's fertility rate is one of the highest in Africa, 7.6 children per woman and can reach 9 in regions such as Maradi and Zinder” but no mention is made of mechanisms to support women’s participation, such as cooperative childcare facilities as part of the project interventions.

26. On the challenge that “It is common in rural areas for women to speak little and/or to agree with the views and decisions of men”, the plan suggests a rather vague solution: “To allow or even encourage women to speak up and express their divergent or concordant points of view during the various meetings related to the project.” In response to a question from the independent TAP about the experience with FAO Dimitra clubs as a vehicle for social empowerment and women’s leadership in the project region, the AE responded that “Since 2009, 550 Dimitra listening clubs have been set up in Niger, in 129 villages in the Tillabéry and Dosso regions. Among the Five Regions covered by the project (Agadez, Diffa, Maradi, Tahoua, Zinder), it is reported that listening clubs have been set up in the Maradi region since 2013. The Project can draw inspiration from it as an organizing mechanism in Output 3.2, especially for engaging women and youth.” The independent TAP notes the value of separate forums where women are able to build up confidence and skills to participate in mixed activities and organizational structures that happen in parallel.

27. In response to a request from independent TAP to elaborate on how it will be ensured that “the poorest and most-climate vulnerable smallholder landowners” will have access to AHA plots, the AE highlighted the secure land framework whereby all the land to be developed will be registered in the name of ONAHA, which will sign an operating contract with each beneficiary (man, woman, landowner or not) giving rise to the payment of an annual royalty. Each 0.125 ha plot will be allocated to a farmer (male, female, young) or to a household and its members (headed by a man or a woman). Women living in polygamous unions may, if they wish, be allocated plots under their own names, where the woman and her children will be consider as household. The AHAs system also includes clear mechanisms for land tenure conflict management and dispute resolution.

### 1.4 Needs of the recipient  
**Scale: High**

28. Because of the demonstrably high vulnerability of the country to a range of external shocks and stresses, and the high exposure of beneficiary groups to climate risks, as well as the overall income and development level of Niger, the Needs of the recipient are rated as HIGH. Niger is a Landlocked Least Developed Country (LDC) and is among the Low Income and Deficit Countries (PFRDV). Niger is the tenth poorest country in the world in terms of GDP per capital, and is ranked last in terms of the Human Development Index, with its economy highly dependent on agriculture. Before the COVID pandemic, a quarter of the population was classified as vulnerable to multidimensional poverty, and this is likely to have increased in the last two years.

29. Niger is also one of the hottest and driest countries in the world, with most of its area covered by the southward-encroaching Sahara Desert, and even the Sahel region of the country is subject to serious climate change threats. For rural populations for whom agriculture is the main source of income and livelihoods, the negative effects of climate change are felt most severely. Pressure on natural resources such as water and grazing is also one of the underlying causes of farmer-herder conflicts and violent extremism in the region. From 1983 to 2013, the average temperature increased by 0.35°C per decade. Rainfall has been generally declining with isohyet sliding southwards. There has been a sequence of agricultural droughts since the 1980s, with domestic agricultural production currently structurally in deficit.

30. Despite the ongoing efforts of the Government of Niger and its financial and technical partners to strengthen the resilience of the agriculture and water sectors and enhance climate resilience and food security, greater investment is needed. Niger’s fiscal gap is a barrier to addressing financing needs. Niger has a per-capita gross domestic product (GDP) of USD 565. It had a significant balance of payments deficit on its current account of over USD 1.8 billion in 2020, and has a B3 credit rating by Moody’s. This suggests the country is just able to service foreign debt, and will be in a position to manage the concessional loan being provided by BOAD as co-finance to the project, but relies also on grant finance for the bulk of the project.
