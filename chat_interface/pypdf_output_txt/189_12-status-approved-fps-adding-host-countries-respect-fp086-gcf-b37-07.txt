I. Introduction  
1. The Board, at its twenty -first  meeting, approved through decision B.21/15  funding  
proposal  086  (FP086)  titled “Green Cities Facility” (the “ programme ”) submitted by the 
European Bank for Reconstruction and Development ( “EBRD ” or the “ accredited entity ”). The 
funded activity agreement (FAA) for the programme was executed on 2 July 2019 and became 
effective on 18 September 2019. The accredited entity originally requested EUR 228 million in 
funding, but the B oard approved EUR 87 million (EUR 65 million in reimbursable funds and 
EUR 22 million in non- reimbursable funds).  Board approval (and consequently the FAA) 
provides the option for the accredited entity to request additional commitment from GCF up to 
the originally requested amount. This additional commitment should be requested up to one 
year before the closing date of the programme.  
2. The programme aims to address cities’ climate change challenges while building the 
market case for private -sector investment in sustainable urban infrastructure. The programme  
addresses multiple barriers to climate action through four components that: ( i) deliver policy 
and strategy support to cities to assist them in  prioritiz ing actions; ( ii) facilitate green city 
infrastructure investments; ( iii) build the capacity of key stakeholders; and ( iv) facilitate and 
provide a pathway for cities to access green finance and capital markets. The p rogramme’s  
investments will focus on urban infrastructure in six sectors: low- carbon and climate- resilient 
buildings, water and wastewater, solid waste, urban transport, municipal energy systems 
(district heating/cooling) , and street lighti ng.   
3. Uptake under the p rogramme has been slower than envisioned in the disbursement plan 
of the FAA. The indicative disbursement schedule in the FAA forecast that all funds would be 
disbursed two years after FAA effectiveness. However, currently, only EUR 33 .3 million has 
been disbursed to the programme. Drivers of the slower than expected disbursement are 
coronavirus disease 2019 , the conflict in Ukraine and lessons learned by the accredited entity in 
relation to eligibility criteria for the projects. 
II. Proposed change  
4. The accredited entity has submitted a restructuring proposal (contained in annex I II and 
VI) seeking to add additional countries  to the programme : Bosnia and Herzegovina, Kazakhstan, 
Kyrgyzstan, Morocco, Tajikistan  and Uzbekistan. The restructuring proposal is part of a broader 
set of requests by the accredited entity to accelerate deployment of funds under the programme 
and incorporate lessons learned . The acc redited entity has also request ed to allow for the 
reallocation of non- reimbursable components, amendments to the eligibility criteria, increased 
currency flexibility, extension of programme timelines and inclusion of additional countries.  
5. The Secretariat has evaluated the set of requests and has approved the changes with 
regard to the reallocation of non- reimbursable funds, amendments to the eligibility criteria, 
increased currency flexibility and the extension of programme timelines and determined that 
these changes were  not major. (Further detail s of these approved changes are included in annex 
IV.) The S ecretariat has also evaluated the request for additional countries , is supportive of this 
change and is recommending that the B oard approve this change. Although the addition of the 
countries is a significant change to the scope of the programme, the Secretariat does not 
consider the change to be materially adverse on the  programmatic level and therefor e it does 
not trigger clause 16 of the GCF Policy on Restructuring and Cancellation , specifically sub clause 
16(b) .  
6. In November 2021, EBRD first engaged with the national designated authorities ( NDAs) 
of the programme ’s host countries to inform and consult the NDAs  on the proposed changes for 
the p rogramme. Following the submission of the restructuring proposal on 18 July 2022, EBRD

III. Assessment 
7. The request from the a ccredited entity that will enable it to expand the programme is 
part of a set of proposed changes to allow EBRD to accelerate the deployment of the 
programme , while due diligence on other identified projects continues.  The S ecretariat  is 
supportive of expanding the programme to additional countries as an effort to increase uptake 
under the p rogramme such that the programme can achieve it s target outcomes  and objectives .  
8. The accredited entity, in consultation with the NDAs of the host countries, has sought to 
include the additional countries.  The accredited entity has sought NOLs and consulted with  
NDAs of  all current host countries (see annex II and annex  V). The accredited entity has received 
seven NOLs from the nine existing host countries , and NOLs from all six countries it wishes to 
include in the programme.  
9. In line with previous instances where accredited entities requested host  countries to be 
included in programmes , the Secretariat has prepared this document , which  includes  the NOLs 
from the NDAs from seven of the  nine host countries , and the N OLs from the NDAs of  all six  
additional countries. The Secretariat recommends that the Board take not e of the NOLs and 
approve the request.  
IV. Compliance with GCF policies  
10.  In submitting this document for Board consideration, the Secretariat confirms that all 
relevant requirements contained in  the GCF Policy on Restructuring and Cancellation have been 
complied with  as the changes have  been assessed as non- major and NDA consu ltation has taken 
place .. 
V. Recommendation  
11. In light of the above considerations, the Secretariat recommends that the Board approve  
the inclusion of: Bosnia and Herzegovina, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan , Morocco, Tajikistan , and 
Uzbekistan as host countries for FP 086 .

The EBRD seeks to expand the geographical scope  of countries eligible under the Funded Activity: FP086 “Green Cities Facility ” 
(the “ Facility ”). 
The difficult global context  has profoundly affected cities. Still in the recovery phase of  the lasting impacts of the COVID- 19 
pandemic , cities have been further impacted by the war on Ukraine  and the resulting energy crisis, as well as displaced refugee 
communities fr om neighbouring countries . As extreme weather events increase globally, suppo rting cities , in a context of the 
unavoidable impact of externalities , new priorities emerging and strained budgets , is more critical than ever.  
In this context of increased needs , high uncertainty and new challenges for cities, t he expansion of the geographical scope of 
the Facility will allow EBRD to: 
i) offer GCF -EBRD joint support to a larger group of cities , and  
ii) accelerate the deployment of the Facility by providing timely financial support  to the projects which have 
reached maturity, while providing critical technical assistance for those under preparation.    
The EBRD seeks to expand the geographical scope of the Facility  to six new countries  and their cities .  
• Bosnia and Herzegovina  – Sarajevo, Banja Luka, Brcko, Mostar, Zenica  
• Kazakhstan  – Almaty, Shymkent, Ust -Kamenogorsk  
• Kyrgyz Republic  – Bishkek

Global challenges  
Since February 2022, the war on Ukraine is resulting in a crisis on a scale not seen since the Second World War. Cities in Ukraine 
and neighbouring countries (such as Moldova) are bearing the brunt of this impact through the destruction of infrastructure and, in conflict -free areas, the pressures on infrastructure as a consequence of the influx of refugees.  Furthermore, the implications  of 
the war have reached  other regions – from experiencing  ‘second tier ’ impact as a result of the sanctions, to disruption in 
commodities supply from Russia and Ukraine, resulting in significant oil, gas  and food price increase.  This mainly affect s countries 
with close economic relations to Russia  and Ukraine  (such as Mongolia  and Georgia ). 
This sudden and massive demand on municipal services and infrastructure is exacerbated by the ongoing energy crisis. Prices o f oil, 
gas and coal have all increased dramatically while geopolitical events have emphasised the importance of energy security. Th ese 
challenges have only strengthened the existing imperative to both reduce municipal energy consumption and switch to non-hydrocarbon energy sources. As cities explore measures to address the crisis, it will be important to ensure that these investment s 
are aligned with a low carbon, digital and inclusive future.  
In addition,  the COVID -19 outbreak and cri sis, which had put  significant pressure on city finances , has had a lasting impact on 
priorities as budgets continue to be  strained . Consequently, cities are facing constraints to borrow and invest in climate 
technologies, particularly given their initial premiums . When the pandemic first hit in 2020, 90% of COVID -19 cases were in cities. 
This severely impacted local revenues in 2020 and  continued throughout 2021. Cities are beginning to recover in 2022, but will need 
support to ensure a green recovery.  
The EBRD has long championed sub- sovereign lending to cities. Three -quarters of the Bank’s portfolio in municipal investments is 
in sub- sovereign finance. However, since the COVID -19 pandemic  onset,  this ratio reversed – for example, in 2020, 65% of EBRD 
municipal infrastructure lending was to sovereign entities . Non-sovereign lending highly depends on the credit worthiness of the 
local borrower , and following the pandemic, the borrowing capacity of municipal borrowers has become sever ely constrained. As 
a result, there is a need for additional support for cities through sovereign or sovereign- guaranteed loans. This trend is observed in 
Western Balkans countries and is most marked in Serbia, where prior to the pandemic lending had been almost exclusively on a non-sovereign basis and now the Bank receives request s for municipal investment coming directly from the central government.  
GCF is no stranger to the impact of strain ed city budgets delaying climate action. GCF’s backing has been crucial in an emergency 
relief fund launched to  shield clean energy companies in developing countries from COVID- 19 economic shocks and to keep low -
carbon trajectories on track. Moreover, in the repor t, ‘Scaling up climate finance in the context of COVID -19’, GCF has recommended  
to financial decision -makers ways to align finance with sustainable development, accelerating the transition to a net -zero  and 
climate resilient economy . GCF recognize d that the COVID -19 pandemic stressed government and local authority finances, and the 
report  suggests four interventions including the  alleviation of debt burden, which would enable developing countries to foster a 
green, climate -resilient recovery . Projects where GCF backing has been instrumental include  two “firsts” for the Bank:

by the global repercussions of the war on Ukraine . Considering adding new Host Countries to the Facility  would allow to adjust to 
these different paths to recovery and respond to the identified needs of other countries  in their green transition pathways , 
accelerating the deployment and full utilisation and disbursement of the first contribution approved by GCF Board in October 2018 . 
 
Strategic context  for improving resilience and adaptation 
Regardless of strained budgets, cities increasingly  recognize that resilience and climate adaptation is a priority area for investment . 
The threat of more frequent and more intense climate related events present s a serious challenge for cities around the world, 
particularly in developing countries where cities grow at unp recedented speed. Both extreme weather events and climate 
projections suggest there is a strong case for climate adaptation action in the countries covered under the Facility . Recent events 
attributed to climate change include: the devastating 2014 floods in the Western Balkans and Serbia in particular which triggered a 
wave of donor financing into flood protection schemes around the region. Or the series of exceptionally harsh and cold winter s in 
Mongolia which killed off livestock in large numbers, forcin g rural population to migrate and take up residence in informal 
settlements on the outskirts of large cities.  
CMIP64 climate data, which underpins the latest IPCC Assessment report and which has been synthesised by the World Bank into 
country briefs, is unequivocal: the mountainous Western Balkans are likely to suffer more severe floods and landslides due to  heavy 
preci pitation events. For Eastern Europe (including Moldova), there is a high degree of confidence that the region will be exposed  
to a combination of increasing water stress, wildfires and higher mean temperatures - which is expected to result in pressure  on 
agricultural productivity. For Mongolia and the Central Asian nations, extreme heat events are projected to have strong knock- on 
effects on droughts and flood risk.  
Climate related events  threaten social and economic development, with urban poor, living in substandard housing or informal 
settlements, being the most exposed and vulnerable to hazards. Building resilience and adapting to climate change is an incre asingly 
high priority for cit ies. As a result, beneficiary cities have expressed strong demand for support for adaptation projects.  A first GCF 
investment grant was signed in Georgia in January 2023 for the modernisation of Tbilisi metro and two additional grants have been 
signed in June 2023 for Chisinau River Bic project in Moldova and the Ulaanbaatar Green Affordable Housing project  in Mongolia, 
utilising almost all GCF non -reimbursable funds available under Component 2. T he subsequent commitment envisaged when the 
GCF Bo ard approved the Funding Proposal (decision GCF/B.21/34) will prove instrumental to address these pressing needs.

The pandemic has put significant  pressure on health and social expenditures in a context of decreasing revenues and increasing 
expenditures . These developments led to constrained credit worthiness of local borrowers  (please see sections B .1 and D.1 for more 
details) .  Moreover, the war on Ukraine has had direct and indirect impacts on countries shifting their priorities to immediate 
response . The fiscal impacts have resulted in a change of focus in municipal  investment plans from systematic low -carbon transition 
and climate resilient solutions to infrastructure solutions that provide quick wins and returns .  
Cities needs further  support to integrate climate strategies with their medium- term recovery plans . Concessional finance allow s 
transformational investments , which are typically more costly , by increasing the competitiveness of climate solutions. This way , 
political decision makers can better focus on these solutions and incorporate  GCAP actions into  the recovery plans. The risk being

Risks that have emerged since the design of the funding proposal and have critically impacted the Facility are  the COVID -19

are facing constraints to borrow and invest in climate action and low carbon technologies. As cities are expected to continue

and, in conflict -free areas, the pressures on infrastructure as a consequence of the influx of refugees. Furthermore, the 
implications of the war has reached other regions – from experiencing ‘second tier’ impact as a result of the sanctions, to 
disruption in commodities supply from Russia and U kraine, resulting in significant oil, gas and food price increase. Thus, the war 
is affecting the economic stability within countries with close economic relations to Russia (such as Mongolia and Georgia). For example, according to the World Bank, the war on Ukraine is likely to also adversely impact the Georgian economy through 
reductions in trade, tourism and remittances, as well as elevated commodity prices. Oil and food prices have globally increas ed 
sharply since the beginning of the war due to uncertainty and disrupted supplies from Russia and Ukraine .
