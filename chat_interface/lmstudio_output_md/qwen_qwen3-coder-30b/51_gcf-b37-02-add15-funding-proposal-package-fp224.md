# GREEN CLIMATE FUND FUNDING PROPOSAL V.10.0 | PAGE 3

The Renewstable Barbados project will be the first large-scale hydrogen storage solution in the country. The solar PV power generation with batteries and hydrogen storage will provide clean firm capacity at night, morning and evening peak-hours providing 6 percent of the country’s electricity demand, avoiding 693,000 tCO2e GHG emissions over the life of the project and creating 120 jobs at construction and ten permanent jobs.

The government of Barbados (GoB) has set ambitious decarbonization targets for 2030, including a target of achieving a 100 percent renewable energy matrix. The Barbados’ Nationally Determined Contributions (NDC) targets GHG emissions reduction of 44 percent compared to its business-as-usual scenario by 2030. At the same time, electricity demand is projected to increase to 1,277 GWh in 2030 under a base scenario, implying a 35 percent increase from its 2019 levels. Given the current power generation matrix that is around 95 percent based on fossil fuel generation1, steam generation plants (totaling 40MW) are to be decommissioned as soon as possible and gas turbines (totaling around 100MW) that are not suitable for continued operation are expected to be used only as backup and emergency generation. Barbados has abundant solar resources with high irradiation and capacity factors thanks to its location close to the equator. A 10 MWp solar power plant is already operating, but developers of additional solar plants are facing difficulties in obtaining necessary permits to connect to the grid. The permitting process is slowed by concerns about grid stability, which emerged after the construction of the existing solar plant.

Barbados is subject to hurricanes and other regional climate events. These climate events can cause disruptions to the grid and generation facilities and, consequently, loss of power to various areas in the island, particularly those more distant from the existing generation facilities. Another level of resilience challenge relates to the access to and pricing of fossil fuels that currently are needed for most power generation in Barbados. Geopolitical developments such as the recent Russian invasion of Ukraine highlight the vulnerability that this dependency on imported fuels can pose to countries around the world and particularly to small island economies such as Barbados.

The proposed RenewStable Barbados (RSB) project will use intermittent solar PV generation and combine it with short-term battery storage and long-term green hydrogen storage to provide a clean generation facility that can be dispatched 24 hours a day. The solar plant is oversized (50 MWp), so that it can make available sufficient capacity (13 MW) to dispatch electricity to the grid during the day, while also generating additional electricity to fully charge a Battery Electricity Storage System (BESS). Once charged, the BESS can cover 15 MW of demand capacity during peak hours and produce sufficient green hydrogen through water electrolysis to then convert back to electricity at a lower capacity of 3 MW throughout the night. This design responds to the requirements of the vertically integrated power company Barbados Light and Power Company (BLPC) and is based on the demand profile for the grid and the dispatch profile of thermal power plants that are being decommissioned. The RSB project will enable the decommissioning of a gas and diesel turbine (GT02) that was originally scheduled for 2019, in which case arguably the GHG emission mitigation could be linked to the emissions of this plant in the order of 55,555 tCO2/year. However, a more conservative approach has been used taking into consideration the average emission factor of the grid that results in mitigation of at least 27,719 tCO2e per year, for a total of 693,000 tCO2e over the 25-year lifetime of the RSB project.

# GREEN CLIMATE FUND FUNDING PROPOSAL V.10.0 | PAGE 4

The RSB project is designed to withstand extreme weather and high wind loads during climate events. The RSB project will be located in the southeastern area of the island, rather than in the west where most of the generation is currently located. This location ensures access to power for people on this part of the island in the event that transmission lines are affected by a severe climatic event.

The RSB project is designed to be able to operate in an “island mode” if disconnected from the rest of the generation plants, allowing residents in the vicinity of the project to not only have continued access to electricity, but also to other services that rely on electric power, such as water (pumping stations are close to the RSB site), health services, telecommunications, and other vital services.

The modularity of the project’s design means that each of its components (solar generation, BESS and hydrogen storage) can operate independently improving the resilience and flexibility of operation. For instance, if the solar plant is temporarily out of service, the storage capacity of the BESS and storage system can dispatch at 3 MW for up to 40 hours if fully charged.

Finally, the project generates electricity solely from solar panels. This means that the RSB plant relies on local resources and not on imported fuels thus making the system more resilient to disruptions in the sourcing and logistics associated with importing fossil fuels. The RSB project will also help reduce the fuel cost volatility which is today fully passed through to the end-users’ monthly bills and fix the production cost of this baseload asset for the country over the next 25 years.

The RSB project provides an innovative solution to address the intermittency challenge of non-conventional renewable energy sources such as solar and wind. This innovative solution is based on technologies that are well known including batteries and electrolyzers/fuel cells. Although the price trend is downward as industrialized production is streamlined and competition increases, the price of these technologies today is still relatively high and only makes sense in specific contexts such as island countries where current HFO/diesel-based generation is very expensive.

Retail tariffs in Barbados are very high, at a weighted average rate of approximately USD 0.27 per kilowatt-hour (kWh) well above the LAC average of around USD 0.15 kWh². The GCF concessional financing, in addition to contributing to make possible this innovative decarbonization solution, will be critical in keeping the average end-user tariff below the current levels and bridge the gap between the Barbados tariffs and LAC average as much as possible.

With a fixed equity return and fixed commercial terms from the IFC and other lenders involved in the transaction, the concessionality of GCF will be fully passed through in the Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) tariff formula with BLPC. GCF concessional financing will also be key in enabling a project that can demonstrate an innovative solution to leverage intermittent renewable energy resources, which will provide increase energy security to Barbados, diversification of its power generation sources, and a potential for replicability in Barbados and other small islands and isolated grids.

# B.1. Climate context (max. 1000 words, approximately 2 pages)

## Climate Change Problem

Barbados is among the most vulnerable Caribbean nations to climate change.3 The Barbados electricity grid is centralized and vulnerable to hurricanes. Since 2010, hurricanes Tomas (2010), Ernesto (2012), Harvey (2017), and Elsa (2021) and the tropical storms Matthew, Maria, Kirk and Gonzalo (2020) have impacted the island, causing power outages and enormous damage to the people in Barbados. Unlike its neighboring island countries, Barbados does not benefit from a geothermal potential. Power plants burning local biomass would typically be very land-intensive for this SIDS country. Adding to these challenges with the current energy landscape of Barbados is its high dependence on imported fossil fuels, which accounts for 95 percent of the total energy supply.4 This arrangement leaves Barbados extremely vulnerable to global oil price fluctuations that directly impact the cost of electricity. The country spends about 7 percent of its GDP on importing expensive fossil fuels. Retail tariffs are high at a weighted average rate of approximately USD 0.27 per kilowatt-hour (kWh), almost double the LAC average of around USD 0.15 per kWh, and outages are common (Energy Transition Initiative, 2020). The cost of fuel purchased is recovered by the utility and directly applied to all customers through the Fuel Adjustment Clause which has represented USD 0.21 kWh on average in 2022.5

## Climate Targets and Strategies

The government of Barbados (GoB) developed a National Energy Policy (NEP) for 2019 -2030, targeting 100 percent renewable energy and carbon-neutral island-state transformation by 2030. In 2019, about one-third of the country's GHG emissions came from its energy sector, according to Climate Watch. Barbados' NDC targets GHG emissions reduction of 44 percent compared to its business-as-usual scenario by 2030. In absolute terms, this represents a decrease of 23 percent compared with the baseline year, 2008. The Integrated Resource and Resiliency Plan for Barbados (IRRP) developed by Mott MacDonald projects electricity demand to increase to 1,277 GWh in 2030 under a base scenario, implying a 3 percent increase from its 2019 levels. At the same time, the IRRP recommends that steam generation plants (totaling 40MW) be decommissioned as soon as possible and that gas turbines (totaling around 100MW) are not suitable for continued operation and expected to be used only as backup and emergency generation. The NEP's ambitious targets are then likely to be met by tapping into the country's proven renewable energy potential, including wind, solar PV, waste, and biomass (primarily sugarcane bagasse). To this purpose, the GoB has already supported investment vehicles such as the Energy Smart Fund6 that provides financial and technical support to Renewable Energy (RE) and Energy Efficiency (EE) projects in Barbados through subsidized loans, technical assistance grants, and replacement rebates for air conditioning units and the Public Sector Smart Energy Program7 consisting of (a) retrofitting of public lighting and public buildings with EE technology, and installation of solar PV panels in government buildings, (b) a fleet of government electric vehicles and a prospectus/study for an ocean power plant, and (c) capacity building for the government of Barbados on sustainable energy. These initiatives aim at developing public and private projects focused on climate resilience, energy efficiency, and renewable energy generation, showcasing the increased political interest and will to support renewable energy projects.

## Adaptation Co-benefits

The RSB project will result in higher resilience of the power system in Barbados. By shifting from imported heavy fuel oil and diesel to local, clean solar PV resources, the project is improving the energy security and resilience of the electricity system, as well as reducing the price volatility and increasing reliability of power generation in a more decentralized arrangement. The project will probably not reduce regular grid outages, but rather provide a separate source of base-load power generation that does not depend on logistic infrastructure for fuel.

# GREEN CLIMATE FUND FUNDING PROPOSAL V.10.0 | PAGE 7

Given the vulnerability of Barbados to extreme precipitation events, such as tropical storms and hurricanes, this project will increase the resilience of the population by reducing dependence on fuel imports, whose delivery depends on the fair weather. In addition, the project increases the resilience of all the population of Barbados, and particularly the residents of its southeast region where the project will be located.

During an extreme precipitation event, it is likely that batteries and hydrogen tanks will be charged/filled to capacity and rationed after the event. As long as the batteries or fuel cells are connected to the grid, electricity can be dispatched very quickly; this is particularly important because some thermal plants require a start-up period. The 90MWh hydrogen storage capacity plus the 30 MWh battery storage capacity would allow for a dispatch at a non-peak rate of 3MW capacity of 40 hours, even if the solar plant is temporarily out of service. (This level of damage is unlikely because of the solar plant’s climate-resilient design). Furthermore, the project’s location in southeastern Barbados is strategically located to increase resilience since it provides grid independence to this area, which currently relies on national grid and generation plants located on the west coast.

In the event of a hurricane, where transmission lines from the national grid are damaged and power to this area is cut off, the project can operate in “island” mode (especially because the cable layout within the plant and the substation will be underground so that they are more resilient), guaranteeing energy to the residents of this region. Ensuring energy to this region will also help in maintaining its water supply, given that water pumps near the site can use the electricity from the project to operate.

Also, if other sources of electricity in the country are damaged because of an extreme precipitation event (e.g., a thermal facility gets flooded or port terminal damaged), and the transmission lines are not damaged, then electricity can also be delivered to the areas of the country being serviced by the damaged facility.

The project itself will be Paris Aligned for power resilience, in line with the Climate Risk assessment that was conducted for the project.

## Barriers and risks to climate change

• Barbados electricity grid is susceptible to the impacts of climate change and highly dependent on imported fossil fuels:

Barbados’ macroeconomic vulnerabilities are aggravated by dependency on fossil fuel imports to meet its energy needs. Almost all energy in the island country is imported, leaving a bill of around 7 percent of GDP and exposing Barbados to adverse effects of oil price volatility. Half of fossil fuel imports go directly to the industry and transportation sectors, and the rest is centrally transformed into electricity, resulting in a polluting energy matrix dominated by oil products (92 percent), with non-fossil fuel product participation declining since 2000 to 3 percent.

Unsolicited applications for licenses to generate electricity are allowed under the regulation for up to 20 MW capacity, while two consecutive feed-in-tariff (FiT) programs have allowed and encouraged projects with a size up to 10 MW. Recent additions of solar generation have already created a situation of saturation of certain feeders, exceeding the stabilization capacity of existing spinning reserves. BLPC is reluctant to connect more intermittent generation facilities such as solar or wind, without storage. This situation makes it difficult for Barbados to achieve its goal of carbon neutrality by 2030 and highlights the need for non-intermittent, renewable solutions such as the proposed project.

The plant is expected to provide 13MW during the day and 3MW during the night. With this capacity, the plant will supply 6 percent of the country’s current electricity demand and will be designed to sustain hurricanes. The plant does not require feedstock and can run on island mode if needed. A large-scale hybrid solution with mature technologies and long-term energy storage is the quickest, most competitive, and bankable way to deploy locally produced clean firm power.

## High cost associated with battery storage and green hydrogen equipment:

Green hydrogen is a technology that, although already mature, is quickly becoming cheaper, but early projects need some concessionality while the price of electrolyzers and the cost of renewable energy continues to drop to levels that would make it fully commercial. The level of concessionali ty needed is intended to address relatively higher country and technology risk perception resulting in a higher cost of financing at this early stage. Over time, similar projects are expected to be financed on a purely commercial basis, but GCF funding can help demonstrate technical feasibility in the country context, accelerate implementation and provide a successful example to be replicated in Barbados and other islands.

Equity contributions for the HDF project in Barbados will be provided by private sponsors, and potentially local credit unions. Part of the debt is expected to be financed by development banks and local banks on a commercial basis, further leveraging the concessional funds from GCF.

## Current electricity tariff makes the renewable energy solution less viable:

The proposed solution is competitive when compared with diesel generation, which is the most obvious alternative, but dependent on a fuel that has high and volatile prices, is imported (resulting in lower energy security) and is polluting. The RSB project would provide a natural hedge against a possible rise in international fuel prices, reduce fuel imports and international reserve depletion and improve the country trade balance. Ninety percent of the oil and gas required to operate the plants is imported due to the lack of refining infrastructure. Oil and gas imports impact the competitiveness of Barbados’ economy and costs on average 7 percent of GDP every year.

The project will help fix the production cost of this baseload asset for the country and reduce the fuel costs volatility currently passed to the end-users monthly bills (100 percent of the fuel cost via the Fuel Clause Adjustment). Initial studies indicate that a solar plus batteries only solution would result in slightly lower pricing than the proposed solution given that batteries only last around four hours of discharge, so a few banks of batteries at the nominal capacity would be needed. Other solutions such as biomass and wind are being explored but are at an earlier stage of development. The proposed project could be viable in the absence of concessional support but at a higher tariff for the electricity sold to the grid by the project. However, this would ultimately impact the end consumers that would have to pay a higher electricity bill.

End consumers in Barbados already pay much higher electricity tariff ($0.27/kWh) than the LAC region ($0.15/kWh). The GCF funding is therefore not so much a liquidity solution as a critical concessional source of funding which would help keep tariffs at the same or lower level for the people of Barbados (i.e. maximizing the concessionality in an otherwise set financing plan will directly benefit the tariff). The regulatory framework in Barbados allows for a feed-in-tariff for renewable projects, but only for plants with an installed capacity below 10 MW and aimed mainly to intermittent generation (solar and wind). Thus, it would not apply to the proposed project.

The tariff in the PPA for this project is being negotiated with the Barbados Fair Trading Commission (FTC) and involves a mechanism for the project to recover the investment and return based on the up-front capital investment and other factors through a capacity (availability) payment. The concessional loan of GCF allows for the PPA tariff that BLPC would pay the project to be lower. The impact of this lower tariff is then fully transferred to the end user and shared across all customers in Barbados through the BLPC grid.

Since Barbados is not ODA-eligible, concessional funding for the proposed project is difficult to come by, making GCF support particularly relevant. The concessional funding is needed at this stage given the relatively high cost of electrolyzers and fuel cells. However, the per-unit price of electrolyzers and fuels cells is expected to fall as the scale of their production increases, the same that happened in the solar PV and wind industries decades ago. Therefore, replication of this model in Barbados or elsewhere is expected to be viable with fewer or no subsidies in the future.

# B.2 (a). Theory of change narrative and diagram (max. 1500 words, approximately 3 pages plus diagram)

## Key characteristics and dynamics of the sector or market

The political feasibility of renewable energy in Barbados is unique because the country does not have large petroleum reserves that would provide competing energy from fossil fuels with embedded subsidies. This is a problem that is characteristic of countries in other regions such as North America and the Middle East.

The tropical climate of Barbados makes it an ideal location for wind and solar energy. Barbados averages 8.3 hours of sunshine per day and 5.6 kilowatts of solar irradiation per square meter. However, the intermittent nature of these renewable sources of energy can create instability in the power grid.

Natural disasters in the past and the COVID-19 pandemic have highlighted the need for a more diverse economy in Barbados. # B.3. Project/programme description (max. 2500 words, approximately 5 pages)

The RSB project scope includes the design, financing, construction, operation and maintenance under a PPA scheme of a renewable energy plant in the easternmost end of the island. The project site is in Harrow, Saint Phillip Parish region, 17 km east of Bridgetown, the capital city of Barbados. The project will be based on a 73.4-hectare plot.

The plant consists of a system delivering a stable baseload and dispatchable energy through the following components:  
1. 50 MWp solar power plant, as a primary source of electricity when the sun is shining  
2. 90 MWh hydrogen storage system produced using electricity and a 15 MW electrolysis system during the day  
3. 3 MW hydrogen fuel cell that transforms the stored hydrogen into firm electricity at night and when required.  
4. 15MW/30MWh Li-Ion battery storage system that provides peak power from 7 pm to 9 pm and, in combination with the hydrogen storage, ensures the stability of the plant power service.

## Project Activities

### Component 1: Solar PV Base Load Generation Plant

#### Activity 1: Installation of a new source of decentralized renewable energy generation

The 50 MWp solar photovoltaic (PV) plant would enable the country to produce more energy locally, and lower supply risk or political dependence. Its decentralized power generation is designed to sustain hurricanes and does not require feedstock to run. The solar PV plant is an intermittent renewable source of energy. Its output can partially be predicted in advance based on data analysis. When the solar plant is generating, part of the power output is dispatched to the grid in a stable way, while the balance is split to the electrolyzer and the battery following a linear optimization algorithm. The charging capacity of the combined storage (hydrogen and batteries) maximizes the use of solar PV production.

An Energy Management System (EMS) will be an integral part of the project that will act as the brain of the power plant and manage the optimal energy dispatch between the different subcomponents of the hybrid power plant at any given time. When the solar PV output is not sufficient to deliver the required power, the EMS will command the battery or the fuel cell to provide power.

The plant can run on an island mode if necessary to provide electricity to those affected by any potential disaster and, with the support of GCF concessional funding, would have a competitive set price of electricity over 25 years for renewable and dispatchable baseload power that can also replace baseload assets and peaking gas turbine. The project would thus reduce the fiscal burden of imported fossil fuel and help Barbados achieve its renewable energy target.

Barbados spends approximately USD 350 million on imported energy each year and relies on trade to meet its energy needs. Consequently, a momentary disruption or trade imbalance can lead to uncertainty in access to energy as prices fluctuate on the world market. GOB's NEP for 2019-2030 targeting 100 percent renewable energy is expected to cost around USD 4 billion and yield USD 3.9 billion per year in economic benefits over the 2020s as the country reduces its import of fossil fuels.

Although not a core component of the project, a sheep farming initiative is being planned as a social enterprise. It responds in part to a requirement from the planning conditions to grant the approval to RSB, which is related to the land's current use designation for agribusiness activities. The operation is intended to be outsourced to a sheep farming entity that will be allowed to use the plant site for grazing. This operation will provide vegetation maintenance services which would otherwise need to be performed mechanically, thus reducing the total cost of maintenance for the plant. There is no revenue stream considered from the sheep farm in the financial model.

The project will donate the sheep pens, feed silos, water harvesting facilities, some necessary equipment and manure management facilities, and provide support in development of value-added products and services which may offer supplemental revenue streams to the farm. RSB would enter into a lease agreement with the farm entity (with or without nominal payment) for the use of the land and donated assets.

The project's mixed land use contributes to Barbados' food security goals and helps create the circular economy envisioned in the Roofs to Reefs program, by providing grass and other vegetation for native Black Belly sheep to graze between the solar panels, while enjoying the shade provided by the panels. It is worth noting that the project aims to provide a sustainable solution for both energy production and agricultural use, ensuring that the land is utilized efficiently and effectively.

## Activity 2: Installation of a short-term battery energy storage system (BESS) and a long-term green hydrogen storage system

There are many technologies on the BESS market, but the current leading technology and most suitable for RSB is the Li-ion BESS. For this project, the 15MW/30MWh Li-ion BESS is complementary to hydrogen, being used as a short-term energy storage and for power regulation. Several suppliers on the market offer preassembled enclosures solution for an easy plug-and-play integration that integrates their own firefighting and safety systems in the enclosure. Equipment typically has a lifetime of 15 years assuming one charge/discharge cycle per day, after which a full replacement would be needed. Round-trip efficiency for Li-ion batteries is around 85 percent to 90 percent, and degradation is estimated at 70 percent capacity by the end of the useful life.

The hydrogen storage system is comprised of three major components: electrolyzer, storage tanks, and fuel cell.

### Electrolyzer

Water electrolysis is an electrochemical reaction known since the 19th century. Different technologies of electrolyzer exist on the market: alkaline, proton exchange membrane (PEM) and solid oxide. The main difference between the technologies is the electrolyte used. Two technologies are suitable for the RSB project: alkaline and PEM. The electrolyzer for the project is expected to be a 15 MW electrolyzer operating during daytime when the solar PV plant is generating. Suppliers offer solutions which can be containerized or which are pre-assembled and integrated in a building. Electrolyzers are estimated to have a lifetime of 80,000 hours (around 20 years if operating at 10 hours per day) after which the degradation should result in a 10 to 15 percent inefficiency. The energy consumption of the electrolyzer is around 55 to 60 KWh per Kg of hydrogen produced.

### Hydrogen Tanks

Hydrogen storage will take place in large vessels (50 to 110 m³) at "low-pressure" (around 30 bar). These steel vessels, especially designed for hydrogen storage, are of a technology widely used in the chemical and petrochemical industry. The storage of hydrogen in gaseous form in large vessels is the simpler and safer solution. The total capacity will be equivalent to the hydrogen needed for 90MWh of electricity generation.

### Fuel Cells

As with the electrolyzer, different technologies of fuel cell exist on the market: alkaline, PEM and solid oxide. The only technology suitable for RBS project is PEM. The fuel cell is a strategic brick because of the very few suppliers capable of building a multi-MW fuel cell. HDF Industry will be the supplier of the fuel cell, which is containerized for an easy plug-and-play integration into the plant. HDF has already installed a one MW fuel cell in Martinique that is currently in operation. Fuel cells are estimated to have a lifetime of 30,000 hours (around 7 years if operating at 12 hours per day) after which the degradation should result in a 15 percent inefficiency and a refurbishing of internal parts will be needed. The energy generation of the fuel cell requires around 60 to 65 kg of hydrogen per 1 MWh of electricity generated.

In addition to the main three components of the green hydrogen system, related balance of plant such as water treatment, compressors, pipelines, civil works, etc. will also be needed.

# Key financial and operational risks

- **Technology and performance risks (fuel cell in particular) of this new solution.**

  Mitigant: Although the proposed solution is innovative, it integrates technologies that have already been proven for years (fuel cells and electrolyzers), albeit there being still room for scaling mass manufacturing and reducing production costs. The electrolyzer and fuel cells will be procured from a tier-1 electrolyzer manufacturer and HDF Industry under a Ballard’s exclusivity co-development, respectively, renowned brands in the nascent green hydrogen industry. The fuel-cell technology used is Proton Exchange Membrane (PEM) technology. The electrolyzer technology can either be pressurized alkaline or pressurized PEM, procured through a competitive process. Both electrolyzer technologies compete today.

  IFC has conducted an initial due diligence and management approval to engage in this project at the development stage (pre-investment). From this initial due diligence, IFC has not found an impediment to support the proposed technology or technology provider in Barbados. Although the expectation is that such a proven technology will operate as designed, as a worst-case scenario the risk of the technology failing is mainly concentrated on the hydrogen part.

  IFC’s understanding is that failure to deliver the agreed electricity to the grid will result in the sponsors carrying the burden of lower revenues and penalties, but not in the tariff to the end consumer being increased as the tariff will be set at PPA signing. IFC will conduct additional due diligence prior to financial closing as part of the approval process for debt financing.

- **Cost competitiveness with other sources of power generation.**

  Mitigant: Despite not having access to the exact dispatch profiles of BLPC required to develop an accurate economic assessment and electricity generation price comparison, the team estimates that the project, with the expected concessionality of GCF, competes with a combination of baseload assets and gas turbines (rather than with intermittent solar or wind generation), but entirely carbon-free, domestically-produced, resilient, innovative, and with local return on investment.

  Another alternative to the RSB solution could be a system using solar generation and battery-only storage (instead of a combination of green hydrogen and batteries). Although no such solution is being considered in Barbados, HDF prepared an in-house comparative analysis between these two options and IFC commissioned an independent study (by consultant DNV) to verify these results. The levelized cost of energy (LCOE) for the BESS-only solution is similar to the solution using GH2 (with the GH2 solution varying from 21 percent more expensive to 7 percent cheaper, across the different scenarios evaluated).

  Additionally, it is important to consider that the option with GH2 has several advantages such as:

  a) **Diversification:** not relying on one technology makes the system more flexible. There is also a lower marginal cost of additional MWh when the GH2 component is included.

  b) **Higher autonomy:** the hybrid GH2 & BESS system has the ability to store energy for up to two days of dispatch in case of emergency. This capacity can be increased economically by adding storage tank capacity, which is important for the adaptation/resiliency benefit and that is prohibitively costly for a BESS-only system to replicate.

  c) **Replicability:** being a first mover in the nascent green hydrogen space opens the way for Barbados to replicate the solution in tandem with a cost reduction of the GH2 technology that is expected to be steeper than for the more mature battery technology. It will also provide the opportunity for other potential applications such as industrial decarbonization where GH2 is a promising solution.

  - 3 MW hydrogen fuel cell that transforms the stored hydrogen into firm electricity at night,

# D.2. Paradigm shift potential (max. 500 words, approximately 1 page)

## Potential for scaling up and replication

The proposed project has a very high potential for engendering a paradigm shift given its prospects for scaling up and replicability in Barbados and in other small island economies or in off-grid, remote areas.

Solar PV generation has become one of the lowest cost solutions for renewable energy production. One of the main drawbacks of solar energy relates to its intermittency, being operational only during the day and with significant variations when passing clouds interfere with sun irradiation. The instability that intermittent sources of power generation like wind or solar produce on the grid can severely limit the penetration of renewable energy capacity in the generation mix.

The Project’s proposed solution combines a solar plant with green hydrogen and battery storage to provide a clean, stable, base-load dispatch, thus preventing disturbances to the grid.

## Potential for knowledge sharing and learning

Being one of the first projects in the LAC region - and the world - using green hydrogen as an energy vector to store energy for base-load dispatch, the Project will offer significant opportunity for knowledge sharing and be a critical learning experience for (i) private sector including the Project sponsors who, pending the success of this Project with the support of the GCF, are aiming to build a pipeline around the world, (ii) for IFC as a potential lender and knowledge source for future green hydrogen projects, and (iii) for GCF to demonstrate the viability of the technology and replicability paving the way to support similar projects following a programmatic approach.

## Contribution to the creation of an enabling environment

The Technical Assistance/capacity building component of the Project will supplement the Project by building the capacity of the government of Barbados and relevant actors to support and drive green hydrogen and battery storage projects both in Barbados and beyond. Through capacity building activities, the Project will focus on filling ‘gaps’ to strengthen the enabling environment for private sector investments in green hydrogen and battery solutions, focusing especially on the needs of small island states.

Specifically, the Project will ensure that there is awareness of the up-to-date legislation and that necessary mechanisms are in place bolstering private sector engagement in the energy sector.

## Contribution to the regulatory framework and policies

The Project will provide the opportunity to adjust the regulatory framework (i.e. tariff, PPA, etc.) and safety standards to enable the creation and development of a green hydrogen ecosystem. After a new PPA legislation was put in place in 2015, the Project will be among first IPPs in Barbados, thus contributing to establish a more diversified structure in the electricity sector.

The Project will serve as a proof of concept for a technical solution and business model that can be scaled to contribute to decarbonization of the power generation sector in Barbados and various countries. Beyond this specific application, the proposed solution will be complementary to other uses of hydrogen as a decarbonization enabler and energy vector for hard-to-abate industries such as transport, fertilizers, chemicals, steel, and synthetic fuels.

## Overall contribution to climate-resilient development pathways

The Project is fully aligned with the ambitious objectives of Barbados to increase the share of electricity produced from renewable sources. The Project is fully aligned with the GoB's ambitious objectives to increase the share of electricity produced from renewable sources and the proposed Project is also aligned with key programs being pursued by the government of Barbados, such as the Roofs to Reefs Program.

# D.3. Sustainable development (max. 500 words, approximately 1 page)

## Financing Structure

IFC’s additionality results from providing a financing solution that is not available in the local market. Non-recourse project finance is practically nonexistent in Barbados, and the tenors available in the market are well below the 15 years that IFC expects to offer to the client. In the past three years, only one USD-denominated syndicated loan was issued by a company in Barbados (extractives sector) with a face value of 100 million USD and a one-year maturity. In that same period, there were no USD corporate bond issuances at a corresponding rating of B+/-. This context of limited availability of financing is a challenge for the private sector in Barbados. Private investment, for example, was 15 percent of GDP in 2022, which is a mild improvement from the 13.9 percent average of the previous 5 years but remains below the 17.7 and 17.8 percent registered in LAC and EMDEs¹¹.

# D.4. Needs of recipient (max. 500 words, approximately 1 page)

The Barbados electricity grid is centralized and vulnerable to hurricanes. # F.1. Risk factors and mitigations measures (max. 3 pages)

## Selected Risk Factor 1  
**Category:** Technical and operational  
**Probability:** Medium  
**Impact:** Select  

### Description  
Technology and performance risks (fuel cell in particular) of this new solution  

### Mitigation Measure(s)  
The proposed solution is new and innovative. It integrates technologies that have already been proven for years (BESS, fuel cells and electrolyzers), albeit there is still room for scaling mass manufacturing and reducing production costs. This project's BESS will be lithium ion and is intended to be procured from a top tier manufacturer. The electrolyzers and fuel cells will be procured from a tier-1 supplier and HDF Industry under an exclusive co-development license with Ballard, two renowned brands in the nascent green hydrogen industry, and performance guarantees will be negotiated. The electrolyzer technology can either be pressurized alkaline or pressurized proton exchange membrane (PEM). Both technologies compete today in similar projects (on CEOG, alkaline was used for commercial reasons). The fuel cell technology used is PEM technology. Finally, although these technologies are providing a novel solution, HDF has been operating a 1 MW fuel cell in Martinique since 2019 and reputable international banks and commercial lenders have already invested in a project similar to RSB. Meridiam and SARA provided equity, and BNP Paribas provided debt for HDF's French Guiana CEOG project after conducting technical and contractual due diligence, which uses the same Renewstable solution as RSB.

**Impact:** Medium  

### Description  
Cost competitiveness with other sources of power generation

## I. Assessment of the independent Technical Advisory Panel  

### 1.1 Overview  

1. Renewstable Barbados is an innovative stable energy provision project based on a 50 Megawatt peak (MWp) solar photovoltaic (PV) system combined with a battery energy storage system (BESS) and green hydrogen storage. This combination will allow continuous and stable electricity provision 24/7 with 13 MW during the day and the evening peak between 7 p.m. and 9 p.m., and 3 MW at other times.  

2. The core feature of the project is that the solar PV system will allow for a guaranteed and stable provision of electricity similar to a base-load system. This is achieved through both storage in the BESS to cater for short-term fluctuations during the day and to provide electricity during the evening peak, and through the production and storage of green hydrogen which acts as long-term storage for provision of electricity during the night, or also as reserve power.  

3. Such systems are central to achieving high rates of renewables based on intermittent energy sources, in particular for small island states with limited grid interchange options. The project has opted for a hybrid storage system using lithium-ion (Li-ion) batteries combined with hydrogen (H2) storage for longer periods which is a promising technical solution.  

4. The project is of high relevance for countries, especially small island developing States (SIDS), to achieve high or even 100 per cent renewable electricity production and is one of the first large-scale systems of this kind worldwide.

### 1.3 Paradigm shift potential     Scale: High  

9. Barbados relies currently on over 90 per cent fossil fuel-based electricity generation.  
The country has the intention to shift to 100 per cent renewables by 2030. No large-scale  
storage and base-load plant from renewables currently exists in Barbados.  

10. The major paradigm shift is not so much towards renewable energy, although this alone  
is already a major change compared to the current situation. The real paradigm shift is the  
change from solar PV being an intermittent source of energy that potentially creates grid  
stability problems, to stable base-load energy provision. The proposed energy storage solution  
adopts a hybrid, combination of a BESS with H2 storage. It is the first of its kind in the Caribbean  
and one of the only plants worldwide at this scale. A large investment will have to be made in RE  
base-load provision and storage to achieve the RE targets of countries. The project plays a  
critical role in assessing and choosing optimal technology solutions to enable this paradigm  
shift.  

11. The approach of combining H2 and a BESS is one possible technological solution.  
Another option, already implemented in various countries, is to use only a BESS. This system is  
more energy efficient as producing H2 and then generating electricity from the H2, results in  
larger energy losses than using batteries. At current costs, a hybrid approach is also more  
expensive compared to adopting only the BESS. However, a hybrid system has other advantages  
related to flexibility and reduced impact of battery degradation over time. Trialling different  
technological approaches in energy storage is considered a positive contribution to identifying  
and choosing optimal storage and base-load systems relative to country requirements and  
situations in the future. However, information, capacity-building and training should be  
technology-neutral for storage solutions and should not be exclusive or uniquely prefer the  
proposed hybrid solution.  

12. The project benefits critical experience in green hydrogen production and storage.  
Green hydrogen can play an important role to decarbonize short- and medium-haul maritime  
transport, while electro-fuels (e-fuels) such as e-liquefied natural gas or e-methanol can also  
decarbonize long-haul maritime and aviation services that are critical for islands. However, the  
independent TAP believes it is important that hydrogen is promoted only in areas where it has  
an environmental, technical, and financial advantage compared to other solutions. In the case of  
Barbados, battery electric vehicles which have a factor of three to four times higher efficiency in  
energy usage compared to H2 vehicles, are better suited for all road transport modes, while H2  
or e-fuels based on green H2 are an option for maritime shipping and aviation. The independent  
TAP wants to avoid GCF grant resources being used for an indiscriminate promotion of  
hydrogen technology.  

13. Knowledge-sharing is critical for a paradigm shift. The project includes measures for  
capacity-building and development of skills. Information, know-how and capacity-building  
should however be technology neutral and should not be steered by the investor with vested  
interests in the H2 economy.  

14. The project is private sector with a private company taking the investment risk. Private  
sector capital is utilized for renewable energy storage solutions. This is considered as highly  
positive and with a high replicability potential by showcasing that renewable energy base-load  
and storage provision can be delivered in a cost-effective manner with private capital.  

15. Overall, the independent TAP considers that the project has the potential to attract more  
private companies and capital to invest in storage and base-load RE provision. Renewable  
energy storage and base-load provision are critical, and the proposed system creates a  
paradigm shift. The independent TAP therefore considers that the paradigm shift potential of  
the project is high.

## 1.4 Sustainable development potential      Scale: Low to Medium  

16. The project is aligned with several Sustainable Development Goals: SDG 3 (good health and well-being); SDG 7 (affordable and clean energy); SDG 8 (decent work and economic growth); and SDG 13 (climate action).  

17. The project has a positive impact on reducing local air pollution and noise due to reducing fossil electricity generation. 18. The project has a positive economic impact by using locally available resources and by reducing the need for import of fossil fuels. This increases the sustainability of the economy of Barbados. No impact on job creation is expected as the project will potentially create a similar number of jobs to those lost in fossil-based electricity generation. This is also true for the planned sheep-farming initiative which replaces jobs of former agricultural activities on the land (sugarcane farming).  

19. The major potential negative impacts of the project are associated with end-of-life hazardous products (solar PV systems and batteries). These will be taken care of through extended producer responsibilities ensuring the used products are shipped to the country of origin or to a closer recycling facility. Freshwater availability for H2 production is secured and is at a very low level compared to current usage and water availability rates. The land used for establishment of the solar PV system is currently used for sugarcane farming which is a declining crop industry in Barbados. A sheep-farming initiative is being planned as a social enterprise, thus replacing a declining agribusiness activity with one that is more attractive financially. This avoids potential negative land-use change impacts.  

20. Overall, the sustainable development potential of the project is considered low to medium. It can make important contributions in the area of reducing dependency on fossil fuel imports while the major focus and impact of the project is clearly on mitigating climate change.  

## 1.5 Needs of the recipient     Scale: Medium  

21. The Barbados electricity grid is vulnerable to hurricanes. Since 2010, hurricanes Tomas (2010), Ernesto (2012), Harvey (2017) and Elsa (2021) and the tropical storms Matthew, Maria, Kirk, and Gonzalo (2020) have impacted the island, causing power outages and enormous damage to the people of Barbados. The independent TAP therefore considers the needs of the recipient as medium.

### 1.7 Efficiency and effectiveness             Scale: Medium  

27. An alternative viable storage solution for Barbados would be based on BESS alone. A battery-based solution with current prices is expected to have a lower overall cost due to the higher efficiency of batteries relative to usage of hydrogen. This is confirmed by an external Det Norske Veritas (DNV) study (annex 28). # Key Gender Terms and Concepts

## Gender analysis or assessment
Identifies, analyses and informs action to address inequalities that arise from the different roles of women and men, or the unequal power relations between them, and the consequences of these inequalities on their lives, their health and well-being. The way power is distributed in most societies means that women have less access to and control over resources, and are less likely to take part in decision-making.

## Gender and energy nexus
Women and girls disproportionately bear the burden of energy poverty because of gender norms and traditions, which hinder equal access to modern energy services. Women and men have different energy needs and capacities to access energy services and appliances. This is a consequence of societal gender norms, and differences in responsibilities and opportunities. In this context, gender-blind approaches further institutionalise and perpetuate existing inequalities. This is why we need to create a gender-responsive energy sector that enables women and men to equally benefit from energy services and supports their socio-economic potential.

## Gender-inclusive
This term refers to initiatives, activities or programmes that are open to both women and men but are designed to overcome any barriers to the full participation that one of the two sexes may experience. In most cases, it targets the increased participation of women and girls. Implementing gender-inclusive programmes is a powerful way to promote gender equality and eradicate gender bias.

## Gender-neutral
Something that is not specific to women or men. This can be policies, investments, projects or business models, whereby it can be assumed that there is no different effect on women or men. Often, something is mistaken to be gender-neutral when it is "gender blind". This means that the policy, programme or business model did not take into account the ways in which it would affect women and men differently. In the end, gender-neutral most commonly means that the needs of men are assumed to be the needs of everyone, which leads to women's needs being left out.

## Gender-based violence (GBV)
An umbrella term for any harmful act that is perpetrated against a person's will and that is based on socially ascribed (gender) differences between females and males. The nature and extent of specific types of GBV vary across cultures, countries and regions. Examples include sexual violence, including sexual exploitation/abuse and forced prostitution, domestic violence, trafficking, forced/early marriage, harmful traditional practices such as female genital mutilation, honour killings and widow inheritance.

## Gender mainstreaming
Mainstreaming a gender perspective means assessing the implications for women and men of any planned action as a way to make women's as well as men's concerns and experiences an integral part of all planning and decision-making processes.

## Introduction
This gender assessment provides a summary overview of the gender equality situation in Barbados specifically within the context of the renewable energy sector. The report summarizes the principal findings regarding the gender and social impact assessment of the Renewstable Barbados project within the fourteen (14) neighboring and surrounding communities located nearest to the project site at Harrow Plantation, in the Parish of Saint Philip, Barbados (Please refer to Annex Table 5). The aim of the gender assessment is to identify the social and gender considerations specific to the project and devise ways of streamlining gender into the project proposal.

The Renewstable® Barbados Project (referred to herein as "the Project" or "RSB") is an innovative and unique large-scale green energy project which combines the use of solar, hydrogen and lithium-ion battery technologies to construct and operate a hybrid solar photovoltaic (PV) energy facility with hydrogen storage. RSB will deliver non-intermittent, carbon-free, and renewable electrical power to the national grid and will also accommodate a Blackbelly sheep farming facility within the solar power plant and surrounding green areas.

The Project will be located at Harrow Plantation, in the Parish of Saint Philip, Barbados, which is situated on the southeastern end of the island. Harrow Plantation consists of approximately 123.0 hectares (ha) of privately-owned land, of which approximately 73.2 ha will be leased by RSB for the purpose of carrying out the Project. The RSB leasehold area (referred to herein as "the Project Property") will encompass the following proposed project components and activities:

- Approximately 59.1 ha will be used for the solar PV power plant, including associated rights-of-way (RoWs) and various restrictions, such as drainage reserves. Most of this land (approximately 57.4 ha) will also support solar grazing (i.e., the practice of grazing livestock, in this case Blackbelly sheep, between and beneath the solar panels of the power plant).
- Approximately 1.6 ha will be used to implement energy storage and management systems in support of the solar PV power plant. These systems will be contained within an area referred to as the "Hydrogen Power Centre" (HyPCe).
- Approximately 2.3 ha will be used for buildings and facilities associated with the on-site Blackbelly sheep farm as well as general office and storage facilities.
- Approximately 10.3 ha will be left as green space (i.e., undeveloped land that is covered with grass) to be used as a fodder pasture for grass harvesting and baling.

The approximate geographic coordinates of the centre of the Project Property are as follows: 13.127548, -59.472712. The land within the Project Property is existing agricultural land that is currently used for sugarcane farming augmented by rotational crops (e.g., cotton). Cultivation of existing sugarcane fields and rotational crops within the Project Property will continue until the start of Project construction.

On June 16th, 2023, the project received its planning approval and in maintaining the agricultural integrity of the land has incorporated as a dual usage facility a Blackbelly Sheep Farm as key component of the project development. This facility will include on-site Blackbelly sheep farming as well as general office and storage facilities.

## Barbados Country-Specific Gender Context
Barbados is globally recognized as a leading voice for small island developing states (SIDS), specifically in championing climate, climate finance, and environmental issues. The country has also been consistently ranked among the top countries in governance and social development indicators, and amongst the countries with lowest corruption indexes.

In 2021, the country formally became a Republic, becoming the fifth Caribbean Community (CARICOM) country to do so. The country's current population is estimated to be 281,976 people. According to the World Population Prospects, the country's annual population growth is around 0.18%. In 2021, there were 3,040 births and the population of males totalled 139,000 while females stood at 148,000. The female population at that time was 51.57% compared to 48.43% males.

Barbados' female population exceeded that of men by approximately 9,000 females and at the time (i.e., circa 2021) Barbados ranked 30th out of 201 countries/territories in terms of female to male ratio.

Since the start of the 20th century, Barbados has transitioned from a largely agrarian sugar-producing nation-state to a diversified service sector-based economy—where tourism, international business and finance are among the country's main economic drivers. However, with this transition, the traditional gender stereotypes that cast women and femininity as essentially different from and inferior to men and masculinity still remain intact.

According to the Barbados Country Gender Assessment, which was carried out by the Caribbean Development Bank in 2016, these gender binaries continue to affect the economic options and behaviours of women and men across the public/private and professional/domestic spheres. In recent years, Barbados has however made considerable progress in these areas, specifically in:

1. Its efforts to enhance women's economic participation and opportunities within society
2. Educational achievement
3. Reproduction, health and survival
4. Political empowerment

In the most recent Global Gender Gap Index (GGGI), Barbados scored 0.769, ranking it 31st out of 146 countries. The GGGI showed that the country ranked highest, at 4th globally, in economic participation and opportunity demonstrating the enhanced gender parity achieved in this area.

In the educational attainment sphere, Barbados also performed reasonably, ranking 65th globally but 3 scores up from its 2022, 67th ranking in this area. A major contributor being the country's high literacy and secondary school enrolment rates, which both ranked 1sts.

## Overview
Though the country has benefitted from stellar female political leadership in the last five (5) years that has been internationally recognized, in terms of political empowerment Barbados only ranked 58th. Altogether, when conceptualizing Barbados global gender performance against the larger grouping of Latin America and Caribbean states, Barbados' ranks 5th overall with the only other English speaking Caribbean state ranking higher being Jamaica at 3rd. This demonstrates the strides the country has made in advancing gender equality and women's empowerment.

## Bureau of Gender Affairs (BGA)
The Bureau of Gender Affairs (BGA), a department within the Ministry of People Empowerment and Elderly Affairs, is the entity responsible for integrating gender into all development plans and policies and to achieve gender equity and equality. However, budgets to support gender mainstreaming efforts are limited and are not available in any ministry except the host ministry.

## Environmental Policies
Notwithstanding this, Barbados has put in place numerous environmental and energy-related policies including (among others) the 2012 Barbados National Climate Change Policy and the Barbados National Energy Policy 2019-2030. The BGA has not been systematically involved in environmental bodies such as the National Climate Change Committee. This means there has been a dearth of gender mainstreaming and the consideration of gender specific dimensions within many of the country's national development policies and plans.

## Gender Policy Framework
Barbados has ratified several key international and regional human rights instruments related to gender equality, namely the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW) and the Inter-American Convention on the Prevention, Punishment and Eradication of Violence against Women. It has also ratified the eight core International Labour Organisation (ILO) Conventions.

There is no National Gender Policy currently in place. The BGA, the national agency responsible for gender mainstreaming, areas of work have been shaped by the Beijing Platform for Action and Commonwealth Plan of Action for Gender Equality 2005-2015 and include poverty alleviation but not broader engagement in economic development. Achievements include the development of legislation and support mechanisms relating to gender-based violence and delivery of gender training. Links to some NGOs and the Institute for Gender and Development Studies at the UWI Cave Hill Campus are strong. However, there are few links with the business community and agencies concerned with economic development.

### National Groundwater Protection Zoning Policy, 1963  
(revised in 1973 and 2010)  
and Green Paper on the 2020 Water Protection and Land Use Zoning Policy  

The National Groundwater Protection Zoning Policy delineates the island into five zones. Public water supply wells are located in the most restricted Zone 1 areas (Stantec 2022).  

The Green Paper (i.e., a tentative government consultation document of policy proposals for debate and discussion, which was produced by the Ministry of Energy and Water Resources, the Government of Barbados, and the Barbados Water Authority [BWA]) proposes that the groundwater protection zones introduced in the National Groundwater Protection Zoning Policy be re-configured for the following purposes (Stantec 2022):  

- To boost groundwater water quality protection due to the presence of more recalcitrant persistent pollutants.  
- To release more lands in the current Zone 1 protection areas for development.  

More specifically, the Green Paper states the following (Stantec 2022):  

- The existing National Groundwater Protection Zoning Policy is over 50 years old and does not protect against persistent chemical pollutants.  
- The existing Zones 1 to 5 are proposed to be replaced with Zones A to E, with Zone A termed a strict exclusion zone smaller than the existing Zone 1.  
- Only agriculture Class Two will be permitted within Zone A; this type of agriculture includes “horticulture, fruit growing, seed growing, use of lands for farmers markets, bursary grounds, woodland or forestry”.  
- Solar PV farms and wind farms – deemed to have low environmental impact – may also be allowed in Zone A areas under specific permitting conditions.  

As described in Appendix B, the Project is currently located within a Zone 3 groundwater protection zone as per the existing Policy. If the Policy is revised as proposed in the Green Paper, the Project will be located within a Zone D groundwater protection zone.

# 10.3 Gender integration in Barbados’ updated NDC

# 16 Conclusion

Although, Barbados has and continues to put in place policy measures which aim to bridge the gender gap between females and males, the energy sector, and the renewable energy sector more specifically, is still very much a male dominated field. Men are stil l among those most represented in the technical related areas of the sector, they (men) hold the highest percentage of the decision-making and senior-level positions and by extension are among those highest paid in comparison to their female counterparts.
