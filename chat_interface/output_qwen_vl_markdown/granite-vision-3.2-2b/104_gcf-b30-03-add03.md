1. Introduction: Relevance of Gender Mainstreaming for the Project
=======================================================

This Gender Assessment has been prepared as an input for the design of the Green Climate Fund (GCF) in order to build greater resilience to hydro-meteorological hazards in Timor-Leste. The Project intends to enhance climate resilience and disaster preparedness capacities at national, sub-national and community level in Timor-Leste through the development of a Gender Assessment and Action Plan (GAPP).

The GAPP is intended to be used as a tool for identifying gender gaps and opportunities in the context of climate change adaptation. It will also serve as a basis for developing strategies and policies that address these gaps and opportunities, with a focus on promoting gender equality and women's empowerment.

The GAPP is based on the Gender Assessment Index (GAI), which is a direct measure of the gender gap in achievement of the three Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) in Timor-Leste: SDG 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy), SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure), and SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities). The GAI is calculated by comparing the percentage of women who have access to affordable and clean energy, participate in industry and innovation, and hold leadership positions in decision-making bodies.

The GAPP will also consider other factors that contribute to gender equality and women's empowerment, such as education, health, economic opportunities, and cultural norms and values. The assessment will be conducted using a combination of quantitative and qualitative methods, including surveys, interviews, focus group discussions, and case studies.

The GAPP is expected to provide valuable insights into the gender gap in achievement of the SDGs in Timor-Leste and inform the development of strategies and policies that address this gap. It will also contribute to the promotion of gender equality and women's empowerment by highlighting the importance of considering gender perspectives in climate change adaptation and mitigation efforts.

3.3 Education

Since independence, education has been a major priority for Timor-Leste.7 The country has succeeded in achieving gender parity in primary and secondary school enrolment (84% for girls vs 92% for boys) at the national level, but low enrollment rates above primary level reflect high rates of repetition and dropout, including due to language barriers and economic reasons.7

At the tertiary level, although notable improvements have been made in narrowing the gap, in 2015, just 38% of people who had studied at university were female, a figure that had risen to 46.6% in 2015.7 However, the overall proportion of the population with tertiary education is still low (19.5%).

Amongst adults, women are much more likely to have missed out on schooling. 84% of women aged 25 and over are illiterate, compared with 67% of men and 50% of women aged 15 years and older who are literate (the latter having low adult literacy). 83% of men and 52% of women aged 15 years and older are illiterate.

7.4 Tailor preparedness and response actions to the differential needs and capacities of vulnerable groups

Women are too often categorised merely as victims, which overlooks the fact that women can be powerful actors in their own lives and communities. They have different but valuable knowledge, skills, experience and coping mechanisms that can be instrumental in addressing or managing climate-related risks. The Project will need to ensure that women are recognised as active participants in decision-making processes and that they are given the opportunity to contribute to the development of disaster preparedness and response plans.

• Develop public awareness and education campaigns tailored to the specific needs and concerns of women, including their roles in household management, access to financial resources, and participation in decision-making processes.

• Include gender differentiated variables in hazard and risk maps and use these to develop disaster preparedness and response plans.

• Analyse gender-differentiated responses to previous disasters and incorporate gender sensitive approaches into the development of new disaster preparedness and response plans.

• Engage with women's groups to co-develop and implement capacity building interventions, including early warning systems (EWS) in parallel with a top-down EWS to reinforce the value of traditional knowledge as well as a back-up system to an automated EWS.

• Ensure that forecast-based financing actions are gender-responsive and that women and men have equal access to financial resources, including loans, grants and insurance products.

7.5 Promote equitable access, decision-making and access to resources

Equitable decision-making at all levels supports the development of adaptive capacity and climate resilience in both rural and urban areas. Evidence shows that women's inclusion is a position of power within communities. Active engagement of women and marginalised individuals in disaster risk reduction activities can lead to more effective and sustainable solutions.

• Human capital - Provide education and training of women to increase their employability and participation in salaried and non-vulnerable employment.

• Social capital - Create and strengthen local women's networks and groups and promote equal representation in local organisations and community meetings.

• Physical capital - Ensure equal access to information, transport and communication assets, including land ownership and active engagement in decision making for natural resource management.

• Financial capital - Promote women's livelihoods through securing access to water, irrigation and agricultural production.

7.6 Minimise the risk of Gender-Based Violence The risk of increasing gender-based violence (GBV) can be minimised through adopting a gender- sensitive and responsive approach to the development of early warning systems (EWS). # Flood Warning Systems for Women and Children

## Introduction

Flood warning systems are crucial tools for protecting lives and property during flood events. However, women and children often face unique challenges in accessing and understanding these systems. This report aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the current state of flood warning systems for women and children, highlighting their strengths and weaknesses, as well as identifying areas for improvement.

## Current State of Flood Warning Systems for Women and Children

### Strengths

1. **Early Warning**: Many flood warning systems provide early warnings, allowing people to take necessary precautions before the floodwaters arrive.
2. **Accessibility**: Some systems are designed with accessibility in mind, making them more usable for women and children with disabilities.
3. **Multilingual Support**: Some systems offer multilingual support, ensuring that all members of the community can understand the warnings.
4. **Community Engagement**: Many systems involve local communities in the planning and implementation process, which can help ensure that the warnings are relevant and effective.

### Weaknesses

1. **Lack of Awareness**: Despite the importance of flood warning systems, many women and children remain unaware of their existence or how to access them.
2. **Limited Resources**: Many communities lack the resources needed to implement and maintain these systems, including funding for technology and infrastructure.
3. **Cultural Barriers**: Cultural barriers can make it difficult for some women and children to understand and follow the warnings provided by flood warning systems.
4. **Gender Inequality**: Women and girls are often more vulnerable to floods than men, and they may face additional challenges in accessing and understanding these systems.

## Recommendations for Improving Flood Warning Systems for Women and Children

1. **Increase Awareness**: Increase awareness of the importance of flood warning systems among women and children through community outreach and education campaigns.
2. **Improve Accessibility**: Improve the accessibility of these systems by ensuring that they are designed with the needs of women and children in mind, including those with disabilities.
3. **Provide Multilingual Support**: Provide multilingual support to ensure that all members of the community can understand the warnings.
4. **Involve Women and Children in Planning**: Involve women and children in the planning and implementation process to ensure that the warnings are relevant and effective.
5. **Increase Funding**: Increase funding for flood warning systems to ensure that they are well-maintained and up-to-date.
6. **Address Cultural Barriers**: Address cultural barriers by providing training and support to help women and children understand the warnings provided by these systems.
7. **Use Leaders as Champions**: Use leaders, such as community elders or religious leaders, as champions to promote the use of flood warning systems among women and children.
8. **Provide Person-to-Person Contact**: Provide person-to-person contact in information dissemination to ensure that women and children have access to accurate and up-to-date information about floods.
9. **Use Technology**: Use technology, such as mobile phones or social media, to disseminate warnings and provide real-time updates during a flood event.
10. **Monitor and Evaluate**: Monitor and evaluate the effectiveness of these systems to ensure that they are meeting their intended goals and identify areas for improvement.

## Conclusion

Flood warning systems are crucial tools for protecting lives and property during flood events, but women and children often face unique challenges in accessing and understanding these systems. By increasing awareness, improving accessibility, providing multilingual support, involving women and children in planning, increasing funding, addressing cultural barriers, using leaders as champions, providing person-to-person contact, using technology, monitoring and evaluating, and implementing the recommendations outlined above, we can improve the effectiveness of flood warning systems for women and children.
