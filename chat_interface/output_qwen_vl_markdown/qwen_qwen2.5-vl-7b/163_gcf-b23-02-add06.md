# Detailed Project / Programme Description

## C1. Bifocal Contact

### Geographic Information/Context

The Republic of the Marshall Islands (RMI) is a small island developing state (SIDS) consisting of 29 coral atolls and 5 major islands, with a total land area of approximately 70 km². The country has an exclusive economic zone that extends over two million km² of ocean. There are 24 inhabited atolls and islands, which are mostly remote and lie merely 2 m above sea level on average. There are no rivers, streams or lakes in RMI and the number of small surface pools is very low.

The RMI is particularly vulnerable to climate change. With its climate influenced by large ocean-atmosphere interactions, it has a high exposure to extreme weather events such as droughts, storms, sea level rise, and temperature extremes. The infrastructure concentrated in small low lying islands and atolls largely of which is only 1 km² away from sea-level changes in wealth patterns or extreme events have significant and profound effects on settlement, living conditions, and the economy.

The hydro-geological framework of the country significantly contributes to its high vulnerability to natural disasters and climate change. The RMI lies within the Pacific Ring of Fire where the core activity is tectonic plate movement which is a fact that it is heavily influenced by storms, high tides, sea level rise, El Niño, reduced annual rainfall and temperature extremes are some of the effects.

### Climate Change Impacts

1. **Droughts** and storm waves are the main extreme events that impact RMI. Historical data show a decreasing trend of rainfall in the country over time. The most significant droughts have occurred during the El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO). The atolls and islands located 10ºN and further north receive less than 1,250 mm (50 inches), while the atolls and islands located farther south of 7ºN receive more than 2,500 mm (100 inches) of rain annually.

2. Increasing sea level rise and decreasing rainfall characteristics** RMI's vulnerability to climate change relative to freshwater resources is likely to exacerbate the risks of freshwater shortages in RMI, challenging the ability of the Marshall Islands to access safe freshwater resources from now on.

### Population/Incomes

As of 2017, RMI has an estimated population of over 55,000 spread across its 29 atolls. Nearly 73% of the population lives in Majuro (approximately 40,000) and Ebon (approximately 27,000) and Ebeye (approximately 11,000). RMI is a high human development and lower middle-income country with a 2016 per capita income of USD $656, yet the country has one of the highest levels of inequality in the world. Approximately 37% of population lives below the national poverty line.

### Economic Structure

Given its small size and sparsely distributed land and population size, RMI's economy is small and fragile. Since its independence from the United States in 1986, RMI has been a member of the Compact of Free Association (Compact), the current Compact is scheduled to expire in 2023. The remaining income of the Marshall Islands comes from the sale of phosphate, fishing rights, tourism, and remittances. The economy sector and small scale handicrafts and mostly subsistence agriculture. Industry is limited to the processing of coconut products and fish.

### Climate Change Adaptation

Due to the limited land and significant distances between islands and atolls, the cost of economic activity is high and economic development is slow in achieving its full potential. The upgrading of government services are high and constrained by logistical challenges. The total amount of investment required for drought mitigation measures was estimated as USD 10 million. The total cost of responding to the severe drought in 2016-2017 was reported to be approximately USD 3.5 million with total post-disaster recovery costs estimated at USD 4.8 million.

The Government of RMI has received USD 9.5 million from the Green Climate Fund (GCF) for climate change adaptation projects in RMI. With limited climate finance, the Marshall Islands are working to scale up its efforts.

## The most significant influence on mean sea-level variability over all of the Pacific region is due to ENSO (NIWA)

Sea level is projected to continue to rise in RMI based on modeling of three emission scenarios. Estimated increase ranges from 3 m (E1980) to 24 m (E2050). Increases in sea level, and hence increases in storm surge, will have a significant impact on coastal communities.

As both wave run-up and overwashing of the coastal berm or coastal defenses by waves can be extremely sensitive to small changes in water levels, it is important that RMI consider the potential impacts of sea-level rise. ## Water sources, management and related issues

### Rainwater harvesting systems

For approximately 90% (and potentially 20% by RMI population live), the main source of potable water is rainwater accumulated from household and community rainwater harvesting systems. Whereas these systems provide a significant contribution to domestic water supply in dry seasons, they are often inadequate during prolonged droughts or when there is a threshold, the harvesting and/or storage capacity is insufficient to meet even basic needs.

### Water quality

Water quality is generally good for most of RMI. The majority of households have rainwater harvesting systems typically with one tank of either 5,432 L or 56,871 L capacity. For a household of 4 people (average size), at a water consumption rate of 100 liters per person per day, the average household would be supplied for 3 days (4,542 L) to 49 days (56,871 L) under conditions of little or no rain; if the RWH systems (gutters and downpipes) are not maintained properly, this period could be reduced by up to half. However, a community survey done as part of the project development phase found that approximately 20% (particularly in gutters and downpipes) were in poor condition (refer to Annex III Efficiency Study).

### Access to Water and Sanitation in Rural Areas of RMIs

#### Key Points:

- **Limited access**: Only one out of five (20%) households have access to safe water.

- **Outer Atolls**: Incomplete toilet and hand washing facilities for communities.
- **Inner Atolls**: Poor access to water and sanitation services, especially when considering WASH and equity of access. Focusing primarily on HH systems using rainwater or from wells, while in schools and public buildings, the use of shared toilets is common.

#### Challenges:

1. **Limited Water Resources**:
   - Due to RMI's unique geography, other options for safe freshwater resources are limited.
2. **Groundwater Quality**:
   - Groundwater from the freshwater lenses underlying the atolls and islands provides a secondary source of water but is affected by seawater intrusion, land subsidence, and heavy storms; groundwater quality is compromised, especially during droughts.

3. **Stationary Infrastructure**:
   - Some stationary infrastructure (water reserve omissions) in systems within community centers and small models are not easily deployed due to the requirement for deployment during extreme drought conditions.
4. **Deployment Challenges**:
   - Deployment of WASH initiatives as well as reporting surveys to enable the impact of the project on sanitation and health to be monitored is challenging, especially when considering the limited access to water resources in outer islands.

#### Overall Project Addressed:

- **Country-specific challenges**: Droughts severely impact the availability of potable water for communities living in the RMIs.
- **Limited governance structures**: There are limited national water governance and coordination mechanisms or accountability frameworks to support water resilience. Therefore, capacity building and the development of robust governance structures at local levels will be key.

#### Conclusion:

The RMI continues to be poorly equipped to deal with drought outside of the major population centers at Majuro and Eajebey, and periodic social disruptions from drought can be expected to continue into the foreseeable future.

### Groundwater

62. Despite these past and ongoing initiatives, existing rainwater catchment systems and tanks are insufficient for providing water security, particularly in the face of climate change. With projected climate-change-induced increases in the variability of rainfall, the current RWSM will be inadequate to meet future water demand and to maintain sufficient water quality.

63. Groundwater serves as an important source of non-potable water. Fresh groundwater in the outer atolls exists as fresh lenses which float atop deeper, saline groundwater. The availability of fresh groundwater depends on island size and rainfall, and diminishes during droughts. Groundwater quality ranges from moderate to extremely saline (depending on depth). In some cases, seawater intrusion has occurred due to over-extraction or contamination of groundwater in most locations. Based on testing performed by EPA at a few of the atolls (Ehul, Jaluit and Nandik) during 2015-2016, the average salinity was found to be approximately 370 mg/l, which is considered moderately saline (standard EPA TPS = 500 mg/l).

64. Groundwater is extracted from shallow wells owned by households or communities. The water is accessed by borers (or in some cases hand pumps) which often restrict access by the mobility challenged people within the community. People tend to extract groundwater during dry seasons and store it for use as drinking water, cooking water, and sometimes as a backup source for drinking during droughts. Excessive use and extraction of groundwater, which occur during the dry season and drought times, have resulted in salinity intrusion and increase the salinity of groundwater resources.

65. Some communities are using shallow wells to extract seawater from the ocean floor. This is done by drilling down and thereby at risk from surface contamination. King tides and inundation of seawater of the atolls can also be adversely impacted by this practice, as well as by the extraction of saltwater from the ocean floor. The majority of the current groundwater water well design may too low to adequately protect it from future subsidence and salinity intrusion.

# F.1 Economic and Financial Analysis

## Economic analysis

The economic analysis of the proposal was conducted during the design phase, focusing on one major output, namely Output 1: Implementation of optimal mix of interventions to ensure water security in outer atolls and islands (Drought Resilience). As Output 2 (Optimization of alternative water sources to reduce reliance on harvested rainwater) and Output 3 (Throughput optimization of existing infrastructure) are not yet implemented, they were not included in the economic analysis. The focus is on ensuring water security for 20 laps in the Marshall Islands in times of climate change; the benefits streams from these three outputs will be captured under the umbrella of Drought Resilience and Climate Change Adaptability.

### Addressing Climate Vulnerabilities in the Water Sector in RMI

This has four key benefit streams. Firstly, mobile and three other statutory revenue-owning (RO) units do not have to be further maintained, replaced, or steered for a particular purpose. Secondly, there will be no need to deploy mobile RO units during droughts, thus avoiding costly emergency operations to provide the absolute minimum of drinking water to outer islands/ atolls via mobile RO units and bottled water. Thirdly, there will be no need to purchase additional bottled water in times of climate change-related water for WASH and resultant health treatment costs will be avoided. Lastly, loss of productive labour will be caused by increased droughts.

### Economic benefits

The economic benefits that are valued in the economic analysis of the project are based on: 1) avoided costs of providing drinking water to outer islands/ atolls via mobile RO units and bottled water during droughts (and other dry days during times of drought); 2) avoided drought emergency response costs, including mobile RO unit deployment costs; and 3) avoided socio-economic drought-related costs and losses.

### Water security in the context of climate change

While the economic analysis considers all interventions required to ensure water security during baseline and climate-induced droughts, the economic analysis considers these jointly. This is due to the fact that the benefits from the project are not dependent on whether a drought event is classified as a baseline or climate change induced drought. For example, if the interventions targeted at the baseline drought were insufficient to meet the needs of the baseline drought and the climate change-induced drought, then the project would not materialize, as the interventions would be used to meet the needs for the baseline drought instead. Some droughts may be classified as a baseline event or a climate change induced event regardless whether this drought is a baseline drought, or a climate change-induced drought. This would complicate the allocation of benefits. Seeing the targeted water security for RMI as both a holistic and mutually dependent cause, these benefits are considered jointly in the economic analysis.

### Benefits during droughts

The benefits of this project occur when water security is ensured during a drought, when compared to the status quo (baseline). The baseline scenario is defined by the current situation. Figure 12.3 on page 58 of the FP illustrates the current (baseline) occurrence of drought over a period of 29 days and 60 days, distinguishing between outer islands/ atolls and inner islands/ atolls. For example, in the outer islands/ atolls, a drought is defined as a 30-day drought in the southern atolls and a 60-day drought in the northern atolls. The baseline scenario assumes that there will be no water security for these communities during this period of time; thus, there is no benefit for an atoll/ island community arises when the project ensures water security for a community during a drought period, which goes beyond the water security provided by their existing water storage (status quo). For example, if the baseline scenario assumes that there will be no water security for 20 days in the outer islands/ atolls, then the project provides greater water security for the remaining 20 days; this community is per definition a beneficiary of this project. ## Summary of the Secretariat’s assessment

### Project background

The project supports the Government of the Marshall Islands in its efforts to adapt to increasing climate risks, including more frequent and extreme droughts, and sea level rise.

#### The Marshall Islands is particularly vulnerable to climate change:

- Its climate is influenced by El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO) events, which are associated with tropical cyclones.
- Its populations and infrastructure are concentrated in small low-lying islands (atolls of the largest of which is only 16 square kilometers).
- Any rise in sea level, changes to rainfall patterns, or increased frequency of extreme weather events will impact settlements, living conditions, and the economy.

#### The combination of climate change vulnerability significantly contributes to its high vulnerability to natural disasters and climate change. Droughts and storm waves are the main extreme events that impact the Marshall Islands.
- Historical data show a decreasing trend of rainfall intensity in recent years.
- Increasing sea level rise and decreasing rainfall characterize the Marshall Islands’ vulnerability, which is expected to worsen over time.

#### The project will address these challenges by improving water security for 24 inhabited atolls and islands. These projects are likely to exacerbate the risks of freshwater shortages in the Marshall Islands, challenging the ability of the Marshallese people to have access to safe, fresh water all year round.
- Overpopulation on low-lying islands is expected to increase due to rising sea levels and storm surges; groundwater quality will be compromised over more frequently. Desalination units are costly to operate and maintain, and there may not be sufficient water application (at scale) to meet the needs of populated urban islands such as Majuro and Ebeye.
- The primary focus of the project will be the improvement and expansion of rainwater harvesting systems on 24 inhabited atolls and islands. These systems are expected to provide up to 10,000 liters per day for 77 rural communities on 23 of the outer islands and atolls of the Marshall Islands.
- Rainwater harvesting systems will be improved on 2,529 households buildings; additional rainwater harvesting systems will be constructed in 48 new households. A total of 16 new community rainwater harvesting systems will be constructed. In addition, the project will provide water treatment systems (downstream and upstream) to 2,000 water wells, safeguarding water quality in the communities.

#### Climate objective

Adaptation
- The Marshall Islands consists of 24 inhabited atolls and islands, which are mostly remote and low, on average, just 1.2 meters (m) above sea level.
- Climate change projections for the Marshall Islands indicate that droughts are to become longer, more severe and more frequent. Existing water sources and insufficient rainwater storage means that freshwater shortages will be exacerbated by climate change.
- Adaptation for human health and well-being, and food and water security: by providing access to a resilient supply of good quality water; this project will directly improve water security and positively impact human health and well-being.

# Women and Culture

## Marshall Islands is based on a system of exogamous matrilineal clans: traditionally, people lived in extended families of three or more generations. Each person belongs to his or her mother's lineage or lineage and has rights to use landline and other property. These lineages group own most of the land in the islands, which can only be "owned" by citizens. Most Marshall Islands have land rights on several atolls or islands.

For thousands of years, the people of the Marshall Islands survived on thin layers of land in a vast universe of ocean. The sea provided food and resources for survival, and the land provided fresh water resources. The importance of land to Marshall Islanders is not only about having a piece of land; it is considered a source of life.

The RMI is a matrilineal society, with women belonging to their mother's lineage and having rights to use land and other property. Women have traditionally been the primary caregivers in the home and hold a position of great importance and influence in society. ## Health Services

The vast majority of health services in the RMI are provided by the government, with only a small number of private providers. There are two hospitals and 56 health centers in the Islands. ## Water, Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH)

The concept of WASH groups together water, sanitation, and hygiene because the impact of deficiencies strongly overlaps. These issues need to be addressed together to achieve sustainable development outcomes, especially for women and children. Historically, sanitation and hygiene have received less attention than water supply in terms of policy formulation and implementation.

The concept of "institutional WASH" arose through global recognition that focusing on water, sanitation and hygiene issues at household and community level only is not enough to achieve the kind of sustainable development outcomes required by the MDGs. Institutional WASH considers the availability of WASH facilities:

- At all schools, for both students and teachers, and especially for girls, given that WASH affects women more than men.
- At all health centres given the vital need to prevent infection and spread of diseases.
- At all places where there is the need to ensure people are able to work in a healthy and safe environment.

## Water and Sanitation

Global studies reveal that over half of schools built in developing countries do not have adequate water and sanitation facilities. As indicated in this report, when water, toilets and hand-washing facilities are not available at school, students are more likely to miss school due to illness or the need for personal hygiene needs and tending to personal hygiene needs. In addition to attendance issues, studies - including this assessment - have shown that WASH is a key factor in improving learning outcomes in schools and student health, learning outcomes, gender equity, poverty, personal self-worth and dignity.

With respect to current sanitation conditions in schools in the outer slums and slums of the RMI, the key findings are:

- The number of functional toilets and urinals do not meet national standards;
- There are no dedicated or easily accessible toilets for students with disabilities;
- Toilet facilities are often shared by boys and girls, which is a violation of girls' menstruation requirements;
- No separate number of hand-washing facilities; seawater used for hand-washing;
- Soap is generally not available at schools for student use;
- Teaching of WASH principles, in particular hygiene practices, at the school is not widespread;
- There is no budget provided to schools to support for WASH requirements;
- There are no specific roles or responsibilities assigned to staff including who is responsible for ensuring repairs and providing soap and other consumables.

## Children

Using the four sets of rights set out in the Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC) as a guideline for assessing children's vulnerability to climate change, it is apparent that children’s rights are not being fully realized given PDNA 2016 and community consultation findings:

- **Survival:** Health issues caused by water and sanitation concerns including unsafe drinking water, waterborne diseases, and nutrition issues stemming from insufficient or poor-quality food.
- **Development:** Decreases in school attendance and educational attainment due to weather-related absences and the impact of climate change on access to safe water and natural disasters.
- **Protection:** Protection issues related to overcrowded housing, very high levels of violence against women and high urban migration;
- **Participation:** Children becoming more aware of potential impacts of climate change on their lives but are not fully involved in decision-making processes or in determining or participation in adaptation initiatives.

Beyond the identification of vulnerable populations, a “child-centred approach” to disaster risk reduction fully engages children and young people moving them into adaptation processes including needs identification and strategy development.


## Women

Women in the Marshall Islands and other Pacific countries generally bear the double burden of productive and reproductive activities – all of which are significantly impacted by climate change. Reproductive responsibilities include childbearing, breastfeeding, preparing food, cooking meals, caring for children and the elderly, cleaning etc. As droughts and storms intensify, these resources become scarce and women have to travel further distances in search of water, fuel and other resources for their families. In fulfilling these duties, women may not have enough time to engage in income-generating activities or education. The increased burden on women can have serious social and financial implications for women and lead to exacerbating gender inequality in households and communities.

### Reduced food supply
Many families in the RMI, especially in the outer islands, produce a significant proportion of their own foods. # CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

The project design process identified several key water insecurity factors that prevent women, men and children from accessing safe drinking water year round including:

1. Lack of adequate household and community-based water supply and sanitation systems in terms of their efficiency (i.e. maintenance and sizing issues) as well as volume;
2. Limited access to clean water for cooking and cleaning purposes which are likely to be contaminated from both organic contaminants and high salinity especially during periods of drought;
3. Stationary desalination systems that are limited in location and/or ineffective due to the high cost and skills required for operation and maintenance.
4. Communities relying on groundwater sources that depend on rain water. These systems fail during drought periods and may also contaminate groundwater resources;
5. Increased vulnerability of women and children to waterborne diseases;
6. Limited capacity to pay for high operating costs for solutions provided in urban centres, and
7. Limited participation of women and other vulnerable groups in decision-making processes regarding water supply.

From a GESI perspective, the inadequate access to safe and clean water resources and sanitation facilities has resulted in the following consequences:

1. Food insecurity, caused by decreased yields of good quality subsistence crops; loss of livestock due to lack of water for drinking and cleaning purposes; increased time spent in gathering and preparing family meals and results in malnutrition which is having a significant impact on children;
2. Health problems, leading to increased prevalence of conjunctivitis (pink eye), diarrhoea, dysentery, malaria, typhoid fever, cholera, scabies, skin diseases, and other waterborne diseases caused by unhygienic diets during droughts. For pregnant women, young children and other vulnerable groups, the risk of contracting these diseases is increased;
3. Losses of livelihoods, due to reduced yield from cash crops; associated agricultural products (such as livestock) are also affected. The 2016 drought in Maji Moto region resulted in a decline in production of 50% of all crops and livestock. In the same year, the Ministry of Agriculture reported that the sector by the 2016 droughts was agriculture which sustained a decline in gross production of 102%. The drought also had an impact on the dairy sub-sector where the number of dairy cows increased from 35% to 48%, and the number of cattle decreased from 79% to 68%;

Decreased school attendance, with students missing up to three days per week during the 2016 drought as high in Maji Moto region nearly reaching 21%; in the rate increased by an average of 35% compared to the previous year. ## Water treatment

### Boiling:
- 46.5% of all respondents indicated that they never boil water from tanks before drinking.
- About 17% of all respondents indicated that they always boil water from rainwater tanks prior to consumption (primarily respondents from Ronging, Majene and Rantepao). About 25% said "sometimes" do, with about the same number of respondents indicating that they always boil tank water prior to drinking.

### Filtration:
- In response to questions on filtering water for drinking purposes, about 51% of all respondents indicated they never filter water from rain tanks; about 30% said "sometimes", and 21% said "always". The majority of respondents who said "never" were primarily respondents from Malealepa Airai, Tarawa, Aina, Wolk, Jeng Meit and Lekpe (J.). With regard to filtering rainwater for drinking purposes, about 45% indicated that they never filter water; about 30% said "sometimes", and 16.7% said "always" (mainly people from Malealepa Airai, Jeng, Meit, Lekpe, Eabdon, Lib, Jalan (T.), Tarawa, Aina, Wolk, Jeng Meit and Lekpe). ### Cleaning Agents:
- In response to questions about whether people add any type of disinfectant to rain tank water prior to drinking, about 44% said "never", 40% said "sometimes" and 26% said "always". The majority of respondents who said "never" were primarily from Malealepa Airai, Tarawa, Aina, Wolk, Jeng Meit and Lekpe (J.).
