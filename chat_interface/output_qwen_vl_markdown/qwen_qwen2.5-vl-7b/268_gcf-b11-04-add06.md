# Energy Efficiency Green Bond in Latin America and the Caribbean

## Executive Summary

Energy Efficiency (EE) is considered one of the instruments with the greatest potential to lower production costs and increase business productivity while simultaneously reducing Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emissions. This EE potential has fueled the creation of private initiatives along the EE value chain in which companies can access financing for energy efficiency projects, including green bonds as a means of investing in EE. However, there are significant barriers to developing the EE market in Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC), where the majority of the population lives below the poverty line.

Local Financial Institutions (LFIs) are very conservative when lending to private sector companies and have historically been reluctant to lend for EE projects due to a lack of experience with EE financing, a preference for short term, collateral-based lending schemes against a company's balance sheet, which are not always available in LAC. This combination of limited knowledge and risk appetite leads to inadequate financing terms for these private sector initiatives in EE.

There is nevertheless an alternative source of financing for EE projects: Capital markets provide a platform for companies to issue green bonds as a means of raising funds for energy efficiency projects or debt securities* in the form of green bonds*. In the last five years, the green bond markets have expanded globally and now include a wide range of products such as Asset-Backed Securities (ABS), a type of green bond in which a pool of green projects are packaged, issued, and backed by the cash flows generated from these projects. The issuance of green bonds in capital markets has the aim of accessing more adequate financing terms and conditions for EE projects.

The purpose of the DBF's Energy Efficiency Green Bond in Latin America and the Caribbean Programme (the "Programme") is to provide an alternative financing mechanism for EE projects through the issuance of green bonds, which will be used by companies to finance energy efficiency measures in a user's facility or premises. This will contribute to the implementation of energy efficiency improvements and on the meeting of the other agreed performance criteria* (IEA, EER Market Report 2015).

EE projects include investments in efficient machinery and lighting, co-generation, renovation and retrofitting of existing buildings, insulation, and the substitution of fossil fuel energy with biomass production or natural gas, and installation of renewable energy facilities.

* Debt securities are negotiable instruments serving as evidence of a debt. They include bills, bonds, negotiable certificates of deposit (CDs), notes, and other similar instruments that are issued by governments, corporations, or traded in the financial markets. Bonds are securities that give the holder the unconditional right to fixed payments at specified intervals and the return of principal on maturity. CDs are time deposits earning interest at a rate determined by the issuer. Notes are debt securities with longer maturities than bonds.

* Green bonds are defined as debt securities issued for financing projects or activities, which can be applied (either by specifying use of Proceeds, Direct Project Exposure, or Special Treatment) towards new and existing EE Green Projects - defined here as projects that have the primary objective to achieve energy efficiency improvements and/or environmental sustainability purposes. ### A.2. Background and Diagnosis

The challenge of Climate Change (CC) and EE in the United Nations Framework Convention for Climate Change (UNFCCC) suggests that to maintain the rise in global temperature below 2 degrees Celsius, countries will need to reduce their GHG emissions by at least 50% from current levels and reach net zero global GHG by 2070. Given that the major sources of GHG emissions have traditionally been energy (67%), industry (18%) and land use change and forestry (14%), it is clear that EE will play a key role in the broad-based impact. In that sense, the UNFCCC foresees that all of the US$ 432 billion invested annually to the year 2050 should be directed towards energy efficiency and renewable energy, including solar, wind, hydro among others.7 For LAC, the International Energy Agency (IEA) suggests that the share of EE in total energy consumption could increase from its current level of around 18% to 34%. Renewable production would therefore need to expand by 50% within the next ten years. Given the high costs associated with renewable energy and the fact that EE is a low-cost option, it is clear that investing in Energy Efficiency (EE) is thus considered to be the most competitive and cost-efficient way to satisfy increasing energy demand. LAC countries could reduce energy consumption by 10% over the next ten years through EE measures.8 The IEA estimates that the total investment required for EE technologies and equipment (estimated cost of US$6 billion - US$35 billion) to build gas-powered open cycle power plants is around US$247 billion, which is more than double the estimated investment in some countries LAC have embarked on the path to develop EE programs, private sector initiatives in this regard are also being considered.

## B. Expected Performance against GCF Investment Criteria

### B.1 Impact potential

The Programme will be a direct contribution to the shift to low-emission, sustainable development pathways in the participating countries. By overcoming substantive barriers to ER financing, new and additional private sector investments are expected to flow into the LAC region as a result of the Programme developed by the Programme and its demonstration effect will provide the foundation for expanded financing from other sources.

- The Programme is expected to finance ER projects with a size below 30 MW. The expected energy savings reflect the threshold for ER projects to be financed under the Programme. Savings from larger projects are not included in this estimate.
- Most of the investments will be in long-lived assets, meaning that the Programme will contribute to locking-in low-carbon infrastructure.

The GHG emission reductions from the underlying projects are expected to be 17,000 tCO2e per million US$ invested. The average project size is around 5 MW. The aggregate expected emission reductions of the total financing are around 13.2 million tCO2e[3].

### B.2 Paradigm Shift Potential

Potential for scaling up and replication. The proposed IIBP Programme intends to replicate and scale up a proven model for ER project finance in the LAC region, with the aim of demonstrating its viability and scaling it up to a regional level. The two step solution (Accumulation and Mobilization Steps) in the Mexico pilot project was recognized by the G-20 Climate Finance Study Group in Turkey (September 2015) as an innovative approach for ER project finance.

The proposed Programme will learn on the lessons learned during the structuring of its first phase namely, (i) how to design a financial structure that is attractive and scalable from the perspective of institutional investors, (ii) target different maturities in the debt markets depending on market readiness, and (iii) how to create an appropriate risk profile for ER project finance by making investments in a new asset class. The Programme is therefore designed to be replicated worldwide, with IIBP as the lead institution, and will provide a platform for other actors and will help closing the financing gap for medium and small scale ER projects up to 30 MW.

B.3. Sustainable Development Potential

In addition to climate change benefits, EE investments promoted through the proposed financing will bring about many economic and environmental co-benefits. The Programme will contribute to strengthening the capacity of recipient countries in developing comprehensive EE strategies for reducing consumption and maintenance costs, enhancing productivity and competitiveness.

The Programme will target countries with significant potential for EE investment opportunities within the domestic private sector (including LPG), increasing the access to finance through capital markets securities for green bonds and other financial instruments. The Programme will also support the development of EE market efficiency practices that will reduce energy consumption per unit of output, hence alleviating the host country's dependence on fossil fuels.

Alternatively, if EE investments of at least 15% against projects' baseline will displace fossil energy production, they will contribute environmental benefits.


B.4. Needs of the Recipient

The Programme will target countries with a significant potential for enhancing EE that prioritize mobilizing private sector investment in EE and have strong policy support.

The Programme will target countries of different sizes, with different economic circumstances including income levels, and with different framework conditions. The countries initially targeted (Mexico, Colombia, Peru, Chile, Argentina) are all upper-middle-income countries with a high level of EE markets and local capital markets. The needs will also differ among the different sectors targeted (such as industry, buildings, transport).

The market and institutional barriers leading to shortage of available adequate financing for EE projects at all levels in these countries are similar. These include: lack of experience in structuring and approach to financing of EE projects, reflecting lack of technical knowledge, perception of high risk of exposure, and the need to provide guarantees or collateral to secure access to traditional bank loans. Green ABS are also a new asset class for all the countries in the region; local investors are unfamiliar with the performance of the underlying EE projects, which can be mitigated by additional external credit enhancement.

While the country-specific interventions will be tailored to the circumstances and specific needs, there are common elements that need to be addressed:

- Economic needs for measures that can scale up investments in EE, leading to climate benefits, and project sustainability.
- Financial needs for instruments that offer adequate financing terms (lower, collateralized) and pricing for EE investments.
- Institutional needs to structure and finance securitized green bonds and the underlying EE investment projects.

## G. Programme Structure

The Programme Structure of the Programme is to provide an alternative financing mechanism for EE projects in LAC through the issuance of green ABS. A second objective is to contribute to the development of LAC capital markets, by introducing green ABS under the highest standards by following the Green Bond Principles and the International Capital Market Association responsible investment guidelines among local institutional investors.

### Description of Facilities

The GEF will have two Green ABS Facilities:

- The first facility (Phase I<sup>®</sup>) is a pilot project in Mexico: "Capital Markets Solution for Financing Energy Efficiency Projects in LAC" under the approval of the GEF 10th Executive Directors' Meeting on May 2014. This Phase seeks to support the ESO model and reduce its financing gap for small/medium sized EE projects (less than $3-MW) in Mexico.

- The second facility is a "Green ABS Facility for Energy Efficiency Green Bond in Latin America and the Caribbean" and aims at i) bringing the pilot project in Mexico to scale by replicating the financial structure of the pilot project, ii) providing access to capital markets for energy efficiency projects sector in several capital markets of the LAC region. This second facility is expected to be approved under the GEF 10th Executive Directors' Meeting on May 2014 and will finance EE projects with a size up to $30 MW. This robust Phase is expected to address the financing gap for EE projects in the private sector by promoting alternative sources of finance, and develop a new asset class in capital markets (both domestic and international).

Description of the Structure of the Programme. # C.3. Programme Risks and Mitigating Factors

## E) Project Performance Risk

There is a potential risk that the EE projects will not achieve the expected energy savings. Mitigating factor: This risk is transferred to the Project Developer. If the savings are not achieved, the project developer would be required to pay back the amount of technology provider fees and/or any other costs incurred by the technology providers. The performance based financing contracts align the ESCO incentives with the actual savings generated through the payment of the debt and are contingent on the projects generating the projected savings.

### Implementation Risk

Unlike large RE projects, EE projects have no construction risk, and EE project implementation is usually a matter of installing equipment. However, there may be risks that sub-contract third parties to implement the EE project, which includes the contractor's surety bonds and insurance. Mitigating factor: The ESCO will be required to provide performance incentives are fully aligned (since they have the primary goal to seek the best contractors who work with the best technology providers). This risk is mitigated by the fact that the ESCOs are responsible for the EE projects and the starting date for project implementation.

### Operation Risk

There may also be risks related to the ability to continue the project's O&M. Mitigating factor: In such cases, it will be fundamental to identify other ESCOs that could serve as replacements. Back-up servicing agreements may need to be signed in such cases even.

## Market Risk

The market risk is associated with the timing of the public offerings and placements of green bonds. In the case of the Phase I pilot in Mexico, the market is expected to be in its initial stages of development. Mitigating factor: The comfort period for investors will be short if there are inadequate pricing or broader market conditions for their placements. Mitigating factor: The comfort period for investors will be short if there are inadequate pricing or broader market conditions for their placements.

## Currency Risk

The IIB can provide financing (both Loans and PPGs) in local currency, hence eliminating Foreign Exchange (FX) risk on its financing. However, the GCF financing shall be provided in US Dollars. Mitigating factor: The GCF financing will be provided to the LAC countries through a trust fund to be established under local law are expected to provide financing in local currency in both Accumulation and Disbursement accounts. This would help mitigate FX risk on the GCF financing. However, entering into hedging agreements or instruments which would help mitigate the FX losses. The GCF will rely on the IIB's experience in providing financing in local currencies as well as the contributions provided by the participation of different LAC countries could help reduce FX exposure.
