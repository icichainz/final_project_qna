# A2. Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

Brazil’s energy-related emissions have increased 130 percent from 1990-2012, against a Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) of a 10 percent reduction by 2030. With extensive urbanization (over 85 percent of Brazil's population lives in cities), the energy sector is expected to be one of the largest contributors to emissions growth. The project aims to address this challenge and contribute to Brazil’s NDC, while also supporting the country’s efforts to meet its commitments under the Paris Agreement.

The project will focus on two key areas: (i) improving the efficiency of street lighting in urban centers; and (ii) promoting energy-efficient projects in industry. These interventions are expected to reduce emissions by 17.4 million tons of CO2e, equivalent to taking over 300,000 cars off the road.

The project has two major components:

- **A. Energy efficiency debt facility**: With a strong window lender and an industrial EE window, the facility will be able to leverage a non-reimbursable GCF of $195 million, a non-reimbursable GCF grant of $40 million that will serve as the equity reserve, a CEF of about $180 million from its own resources and private local debt (sourced by CEF). It is envisioned that this structure will crowd in about $400 million in investments from local banks and other financial institutions. The World Bank will provide up to 50 percent of the total loan amount, with a maximum of $200 million in loans (equivalent to 50 percent of their potential losses). In addition, the World Bank will offer a $200 million credit enhancement facility (CEF) for the CEF (a so-called “credit enhancement loan”), which will support CEF to have made the good partial risk guarantees offered to the syndicated private lenders.

- **B. Technical assistance grant**: To finance initial facility starting costs, including technical studies, operational costs, preparation of high-quality Public Private Partnership (PPP) street lighting sub-projects, and training of CEF teams in origination, assessment and monitoring of energy efficiency sub-projects in street lighting and industrial EE projects.

The project is expected to have a total cost of $405 million. The GCF has provided with an SGP Recipient executed grant of $4 million and a World Bank $1 million contribution (Bank-backed).

FinairZEEC is expected to finance about 36 public street lighting and 14 portfolios of industrial EE projects, with an estimated lifetime emissions reduction of 17.4 million tons of CO2e.* See Annex 2, Annex 3.1, Annexes 3.2 and Annex 14 for more details.

**B.1 Description of Financial Elements of the Project / Programme**

FinTechREAZEE is an innovative financial structure that has been financing some of the major barriers to financing EE interventions in Brazil. After a decade of rapid growth and social progress starting in 2003, Brazil has entered a period of economic stagnation and political uncertainty. The global financial crisis (GFC) initially triggered the slowdown, political uncertainty in the wake of the Lava Jato corruption scandal and the impeachment of former Brazilian President Dilma Rousseff have driven investor confidence to new lows. Total private investments have taken notice and are now looking for opportunities that can provide a return on their investment while also making a positive impact at the federal, state and municipal levels.

At the federal level, these constraints have sharply reduced the transfers from the government to the Brazilian Development Bank (BNDES), which served as major financier for municipalities and businesses alike.

#### Overview of Facility Structure

FinTechREAZEE EE facility is a facility that will lend to EE projects in the public street lighting and industrial sectors. The objective is to develop new financial products for EE investment in Brazil that will facilitate investments at scale in these sectors, by de-risking cash flows of energy efficiency projects and making them more bankable.

Once the concept is proven with the public street lighting sector, it can be scaled up with government support over time and allow the concept to be rolled out to EE investments in other sectors (buildings, transport, etc.).

Market surveys conducted for the Feasibility Studies indicate that there are very few financiers taking place in these sectors. Some of the most important constraints associated with this lack of financing in Brazil is high transaction costs associated with project development, lack of balance sheet financing options for cities and industries, and limited de-risking risk in EE cash flows limiting the availability of financing, lack of standardization of business models, amongst others.

The FinTechREAZEE EE facility will address these constraints by providing a new financial product to the market, creating new asset classes, aggregating investments into a diversified portfolio, and providing access to finance for private sector sponsors of EE projects, and implementing off balance sheet financing approaches for cities and industries.

In the case of street lighting, the facility will primarily lend to Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs) set up by-the private sector, which will be granted concessions by the municipalities to modernize and operate the street lighting system on a Public Private Partnership (PPP) basis. ## Credit Enhancement

CEF will be able to offer credit enhancement (CE) to the private lenders who would enter in an lender framework agreement to stipulate the maximum amount to be committed at facility level. The process to agree on the credit risk assessment for the sub-projects and the eligibility criteria of the sub-projects, among others. CEF will provide a CE up to 50% of the total project cost (including any additional costs incurred by the private lenders) as adjusted basis to projects in street lighting and industrial sectors as per the framework agreement. The WB team will work with the private sector lenders to ensure that they are able to meet the eligibility criteria for CEF CE, which would contribute to the distribution of the financial market, in agreement with WB Operational Policy OP 10.00 for Investment project financing with mainstreamed financing.

## Liquidity Reserve and Credit

Given the barriers to investment for the private sector described above, the EE Facility will be designed to offer credit enhancements to the private sector lenders to participate in the syndication supporting the underlying projects, and enough liquidity reserves to allow the facility to be sustainable over time. Credit enhancement of the facility is offered through a CEF CE up to 50% of total project cost (including any additional costs incurred by the private lenders) as adjusted basis to projects in the facility (2 steps) right on COP17 (the levy on the electricity tariff collected by municipalities (3) a grant-funded liquidity reserve (4) an equity investment in the syndicate. The CEF CE will be provided up to 50% of any additional losses incurred by the private lenders in the facility offered by CEF and backstopped by the World Bank.

### Liquidity Reserve at the facility level

A CEF grant (partially complemented by a CTF grant or other climate funds) will be used to establish a liquidity reserve account within the EE Facility to improve its credit rating in order to attract private investors. The provision of liquidity at the EE Facility level addresses several risks at sub-project level, such as potential material delays in project implementation due to unforeseen circumstances (e.g., weather conditions, strikes, etc.), and others that may result in delay our default payments from concessionaires/Industries to CEF. The Liquidity Reserve also provides flexibility for CEF to take on risk, given that they are taking less via their committed funds, than the COP17 levy collected by municipalities. The liquidity reserve offered at facility level is expected to be particularly important, given the fact that CEF will have to manage the risks associated with harmonizing the timing of identifying projects and making investments in them.

### Credit Enhancement Product

CEF will offer a credit enhancement to private sector lenders/investors that will be backstopped by the World Bank's IFI contingent loan. If the Facility cannot make payments in full to the syndicated lenders/investors after the other credit enhancement lines of defense have been exhausted, CEF will cover up to 50 percent of the total project cost (including any additional costs incurred by private sector lenders) as adjusted basis to projects in the facility. This will cover payment shortfalls as well as losses. The credit enhancement, backstopped by the IFI contingent loan, is expected to be particularly important given that it allows CEF to take on risk and provide a higher level of CE.

The credit enhancements are intended to improve the profile of investments in EE, enabling it to attract investors that would otherwise not be interested in investing at any price (due to the fact the Facility is investing in underlying projects and markets with no track record). The enhancements are not expected to have a substantial impact on the financing cost of these investors, but will allow CEF to take on risk and provide a higher level of CE.

## Foreign exchange

The EE Facility will make loans denominated in BRL. The CEF and co-lenders tiers will be denominated in USD. To limit the FX risk associated with the IIP contingent loan and GCF loans, CEF has requested the World Bank to perform a currency swap on its behalf upon disbursement of these loans. This swap would allow CEF to convert BRL into USD at the time of disbursement. The converted amount will be liquid at shorter and medium-term maturities (up to 10 years); beyond 10 years, a conversion can be performed on a case-by-case basis with the World Bank. In addition, the World Bank has agreed that an interest rate swap conversion can be performed. An additional feature of this IIP contingent loan is that the repayment schedule can be adjusted at the time of disbursement, allowing CEF to better match its borrowing terms to the market conditions at that time.

As an example, as for market conditions in August 2017, the net cost estimated in BRL of the IBRD IIP contingent loan and the GCF loan would be:

| IBRD IIP contingent loan | 15-year tenor, fixed interest rate of Libor (lim) + 0.90% in USD, would have a final costs in BRL after the swap of approximately 9.11%. |
| --- | --- |
| GCF loan | 20-year tenor, fixed interest rate of 0.75% in USD, would have a final cost in BRL after the swap of approximately 8.46%. |

It is important to note that the cost of the FX swap means that the impact of the benefit of concessionality of GCF loan is reduced once it is converted into BRL. As a result, the project would benefit from higher concessional rates compared to the 0.75% interest rate assumed above.

## Managing Risk

The first line of defense in managing risk of the facility will be to ensure a strong pipeline of high-quality sub-projects that will borrow from the facility, as well as strong capacity of CEF to conduct the due diligence of these projects.

IBRD provides, at the option of the borrower, specific instruments for managing foreign exchange risk (FX) for both the IIP contingent loan and the GCF loan. For IBRD loans, the borrower may opt for a currency conversion, which will be part of the Loan agreement; while in the case of hedgegs (IBRD IIP contingent loan or GCF loans), the conditions are that the client must execute a currency swap with IBRD at the time of disbursement into the local currency. Therefore, when the client requests local currency loans, IBRD must execute a swap transaction with a foreign exchange bank to convert BRL into USD. The cost of this hedge is variable and depends on market rates quoted in Reais, and payments will be made in US $ at the current exchange rates. The cost of the hedge varies per market conditions.

### Public Street Lighting

The World Bank estimates that Brazil has more than 18.5 million points of light, with a service penetration of approximately 95.5 percent of residential areas. Public street lighting represents about 3-4 percent of total electricity consumption in Brazil and close to 5 percent of peak consumption. ## Industrial Energy Efficiency

The potential for efficiency in the industrial sector is enormous. A study from the American Council for an Energy-Efficient Economy (ACEEE) analyzed the performance of the 16 largest economies. Brazil was ranked #15 (last out of all industrial energy users). The Brazilian Association of Large Consumers of Industrial Electricity and Consumer Livestock (ABRACE) have carried out several studies on EE in large industrial plants, including those related to heat recovery, steam generation, and thermal processes (potential savings ranging from 8 to 40 percent of current consumption depending on the sector). The World Bank has also identified significant opportunities for EE in Brazil, particularly in relation to energy generation and use, with specific results on the potential related to energy efficiency in the industry.

While a large share of Brazilian industrial plants is in urban industrial districts, cities often leave industrial districts out of their citywide plans for emission reductions, largely because they are outside of their direct purview. More than 150 projects with high return and short payback periods have already been identified among large industrial energy users. Projects encompass a variety of sizes and technologies, such as automation in factories, regenerative burning in cement plants, and heat recovery from steam pumps. The average payback period for EE investments is very low (paybacks of 1-3 years), and internal rates of return ranging from 30 to 90 percent. More capital intensive projects, such as cogeneration and combined cycle power generation, have lower returns but still range between 25 and 50 percent. Potentials for EE should also be significant in medium and small industries and commercial establishments.

Under the Baseline Scenario, the Feasibility Study analysis estimates that an "average" city would eventually fully convert its inventory to LEDs; however, due to its financing and budget constraints, the process will take place over a long period of time. At the end of 13 years, the Baseline Scenario assumes that 30 percent of the existing inventory has been replaced by LEDs.

The Project Scenarios, as described above, involves the establishment of a PPP that will finance the upfront cost of modernizing and replacing entire inventory of public street lights with LEDs. By bringing in private sector financing through the FonBRAZEEC facilities, the project is able to overcome one of the key barriers for implementing an LED modernization project: lack of sufficient revenue to fund the upfront costs. The Project Scenario also aims at overcoming other barriers, including improving municipal capacity and reducing the transaction costs involved in the preparation of projects through standardized approaches, and identifying investors willing to invest in these projects through FonBRAZEEC facilities.

### 1. Public Private Partnerships (PPPs) for Street Lighting

A PPP in street lighting is a form of administrative concession. It requires the granting authority (i.e., the municipality to carry out the project) to enter into an agreement with the private sector before the modernization of street lighting services.

### E.6.4 Application of best practices

## Public Street Lighting

### Identification of the Baseline Scenario

The analysis assumes that, without this project, municipalities would slowly replace burned-out lamps in the existing fleet with new ones. Given the municipal constraints on financing (under the Fiscal Responsibility Law, described in detail in the Lighting Brazilian Cities report), cities would be limited to using only 10% of their revenue for public street lighting and would have to cover the remaining costs through O&M costs.

For many cities, the source of revenue to fund public street lighting is known as COSIP, a tax levied on end-users via their electricity bill.

#### Baseline Scenario

The Baseline Scenario assumes that the city has a COSIP surplus of approximately 10 percent of their total expenditures, after paying electricity and O&M costs for street lighting. In other words, COSIP collection will be R$ 20 million. R$ 18 million of which will be used to cover the current expenditures (including replacement of burned-out lamps), leaving a surplus of R$ 2 million.

With this surplus, the city will fund its annual system expansion investments, as well as modernization projects in the following years. The Baseline Scenario assumes that the city will have a COSIP surplus for at least five more years.

The Baseline assumes the city will use 25% of its total surplus to fund the modernization with LEDs, equal to R$ 0.5 million per year.

In practice, the measures taken under the Baseline Scenario, the city would eventually fully convert its inventory to LED technology due to financing and budget constraints; this process will take place over a long period of time. By the end of 13 years, the Baseline Scenario assumes that 30 percent of the existing inventory is replaced by LEDs.

### Project Scenario

The Project Scenario, as described above, involves the establishment of a PPA which will finance the upfront cost of modernizing the entire fleet of public street lights with LEDs. By bringing in private sector financing through the FinBRAZEE facilities, the project is able to overcome one of the key barriers for implementing a LED modernization project: upfront financing costs under constrained budget and balance sheet constraints.

The Project Scenario assumes that the PPA will provide the necessary funding to cover the technical capacity and reduce the transaction costs involved in the preparation of projects through standardized application, and identifying investors willing to invest in these projects through a dedicated facility.

### Energy efficient street lighting

The World Bank has prepared and is publishing an in-depth report on the market for energy efficient investments in public street lighting in Brazil. Lighting Brazil Cities: Business Models for Energy Efficient Public Street Lighting (August of 2014, WB Report No. 13697-MT), hereinafter referred to as Annex 12, provides more detail on the technology, barriers to investment, market analysis, and investment needs.

In brief, public lighting services represent an important energy consumption source for cities. In fact, in Brazil, public lighting represents approximately 5% of total electricity consumption. The cost of maintaining public lighting already represents the second most expensive item of most municipalities' budget, surpassed only by payroll expenditures. Therefore, energy efficiency projects in public lighting sector can play an important role for carbon emission reduction and cost savings.

Within global scope, participant countries of the 21st Conference of Parties (COP-21) held in November 2015, submitted their contribution proposals to combat climate change. Among other objectives, Brazil committed itself to increase energy efficiency by 43% until 2030 and to reduce its carbon emissions intensity per unit of GDP by 37%. The goal could be obtained by implementing energy efficient technologies in the Brazilian municipal street lighting sector.

Lamps using the new technology LEDs or Light-Emitting Diodes emerge as a feasible technical and commercial option for investments in energy efficiency in the public lighting sector. LED lamps are 40 to 60 percent more efficient in terms of energy consumption than traditional light bulbs, and they last up to five times longer. The adoption of LEDs results in operational and maintenance costs and the capacity to integrate efficient lamps infrastructure with smart monitoring systems, which can be used for energy savings and other purposes. Furthermore, the higher quality of LEDs has the positive effect of reducing crime and increasing citizens' perception of safety.

In 2012, an international non-governmental organization, The Climate Group, was one of the first organizations to conduct studies in cities with public lighting projects with LEDs technology to confirm the performance and acceptance of this new technology. The study showed that the adoption of LED lamps can reduce energy consumption by up to 75% levels of energy savings ranging from 50 to 70 percent reaching up to 80 percent, when combined with smart systems of management and control. In addition, the use of LEDs in public lighting has a positive impact on the environment in wide range of socioeconomic benefits, such as improvement in lighting quality, improve security and increased local economy. Furthermore, the adoption of smart control systems allowed greater flexibility in terms of lighting options with increased focus on people.

### Urban EEE Industries

EEE technologies vary by industrial subsectors but typical energy-saving measures include:

- **Energy systems:** Upgrading boilers and switching fuels, using electric-driven systems, including compressed air systems.
- **Process technology:** Upgrading and replacing equipment, machinery, and facilities.
- **Waste heat and waste use:** Use of waste heat (of hot/warm gases, liquids and solids) and burning combustible waste materials.

Energy intensive industrial sectors include cement, steel, textile, pulp and paper, food processing, bricks, and ceramics. Many of these industries are present in Brazilian urban industrial districts. Potential EE measures for cement industry include the use of energy efficient kilns, heat recovery systems, and improved control systems to reduce waste fuel and waste heat recovery. The cement industry is one of the energy-intensive sectors, producing a huge volume of heat exhaust at a temperature up to 150°C. Therefore, the key measure to improve EE is the utilization of heat exhaust for steam generation or other purposes.

## H.2. Arrangements for Monitoring, Reporting and Evaluation

Besides the arrangements laid out in the AMA, the project implementation involves (a) monitoring of performance indicators in Section H: periodic progress reports; and (b) midterm review of implementation progress. CEF will be responsible for the preparation of the progress report and the midterm review report to be submitted to the World Bank and reporting on projects impacts and results. For the sub-project level activities, each borrower should enter in an acceptance of conditions form with the World Bank and submit a quarterly progress report to CEF. The latter will prepare a report to report to CEF and the World Bank. The World Bank will follow its standard monitoring and reporting guidelines, which will include semi-annual reports from the beneficiaries to the PMU established within CEF. CEF will aggregate this information and submit it to the World Bank, who will then share the information with the GCF as per the agreed reporting requirements.

It is noted that the end targets of the project are best estimates as it is not known upfront what subprojects will be financed and the targets may be revised and finalized during implementation. By the end of the project, CEF will prepare a report with CEF assessment completed; assessment and preparation for the project completion report (PCR) which will review the performance of the project, assess effectiveness and efficiency of project implementation, the achievement of the development objective, and provide relevant lessons learned.
