# B.1 Climate rationale and context (max. 1000 words, approximately 2 pages)

## Socio-economic context

One of the main economic activities generating foreign exchange in Guatemala is agriculture, which depends greatly on access to water resources from precipitation (Poma, Toral, Griffin, Castañeda, and Anchukaitis, 2017). Guatemala is the second most vulnerable country to climate change in Latin America and the 11th worldwide. This vulnerability is due to its high population density in areas of low rainfall, a large proportion of the population living below the poverty line (INEGI, 2002; CEPA, 2005; EMAT, 2017) and the limited resilience of affected communities to face these impacts. Guatemala is one of the countries with the highest percentage of indigenous people in Latin America: indigenous peoples are the majority of whom live in poverty conditions (INEGI). Poverty among the indigenous population is at 31% compared to 15% for non-indigenous populations. The country has a high proportion of children living in extreme poverty, most of which are children from indigenous communities. Poverty and food security are growing challenges in Guatemala. According to the World Bank, 40% of Guatemalan households live below the poverty line (World Bank, 2017). In Guatemala, subsistence agriculture comprises 53% of production and is responsible for the production of 67% of the country's food.

The direct beneficiaries of the proposed project are infra-subistence and subsistence farmers living in regions highly exposed to aridity due to climate-induced droughts and heat waves. The project will be implemented five departments located in the north, northwest, and west of Guatemala: Alta Verapaz, Petén, Quetzaltenango, Huehuetenango, and Zacapa.

There is a clear link between climate change and rural livelihoods, since the expected climate impacts would result in lower yields of staple crops (maize and beans), on which the vast majority of the prioritized population depend for their subsistence. The projected changes in temperature and precipitation will also have an impact on the production of a wide range of other crops, such as coffee and sugar cane. These changes are expected to not only affect yields, but also the quality of the products. As such, scenarios are set to have a severe impact on the livelihoods of the most vulnerable small farmers.

### Historical climate trends

Guatemala experiences a bimodal seasonal rainfall pattern, with a rainy boreal summer season and a dry boreal winter season (Poma et al., 2017). The rainy season usually begins in May with two periods: June and September and a period of decreased rainfall called "cursicio" or midsummer drought (MDS) between July and August. The MDS can last on average for about one month, but it has been observed to last up to three months. In the dry season, which is also a drier period, changes that are critical for determining agricultural calendars across Guatemala.

Climate in Guatemala has already experienced a change in temperature in most of the territory, affecting crop production and livestock rearing (Poma et al., 2017). The analysis of historical climate data shows an increase in average annual temperatures since 1951. This is consistent with other studies using different datasets. For assessing the historic trends of both temperature and precipitation the CRU dataset for the periods 1961-1990, 1981-2010 and 1971-2000 were used (Poma et al., 2017). The analysis was complemented with additional data by FAO – as indicated in Section C1 and C2 of the Feasibility Study. A summary is presented below.

#### Temperature

Analysis shows that the average annual temperature increased with 0.6°C since 1951 in the Mayan lowland region and in the Dry Corridor. More importantly, in these time periods, the inter-annual distribution of changes in temperature was more variable than precipitation, with up to 0.8°C in specific months important for the agricultural production (January, June and December).

## Climate change projections

### In order to integrate a metric that would allow for temperature changes (associated to soil moisture loss via evapotranspiration) and future rainfall estimates, FAO decided to work with aridity. Aridity is a metric that combines atmospheric demand for humidity via temperature and available precipitation. Aridity integrates these two variables into one index which can be used as an indicator of the water balance in any given area (available water vs evapotranspiration). Because monthly temperature and annual precipitation are available as products from climate models, aridity provides a simple metric that is easy to use for integration potential for evapotranspiration (since models do not produce other variables for more complex PET formulas).

### The Penman model cannot be used towards the future, as the climate models used in this study do not produce the necessary information on soil moisture and vegetation cover. Instead, the FAO-56 model is used, which only demands monthly temperature and annual precipitation, reducing the uncertainties associated to more complex, but more intricate variables towards the future like wind. A simple model with less assumptions adding to uncertainty is preferred.

### FAO followed WMO guidance and used the CRU observation-dated population for grant. This dataset is a common product for this type of analysis and is currently being used worldwide to cross-validate and verify multi-model ensemble for new forecasts. FAO confirmed that the length of this data set used is the only that allows formulating the two climatologies (1960-1989 and 1990-2014).

## Barriers to Adaptation and Mitigation Planning

### Financial barriers
- **Relatively high cost of upfront investments**: Small-scale farmers in rural Guatemala generate insufficient cash flow from their farms, limiting their own resilience. It is therefore difficult for them to afford the up-front costs of many climate-resilience investments (new seed varieties, water storage infrastructure, irrigation systems, etc.). Similarly, farmers cannot easily access credit or other forms of financial assistance that would help them make these investments.
- **Limited access to collateral**: Small-scale farmers are not directly revenue-generating and they do not generate appropriate collateral for commercial borrowing. As a result, they have limited access to loans from banks and microfinance institutions.

### Social barriers
- **Women's disempowerment regarding access to resources and information**: Women are usually the demographic group that stays working at their farms (men often migrate to the city and earn more for better jobs), yet they lack decision-making power. The gender gap in this project area is presented in the Gender Assessment (See Part A of Annex 6).
- **Language barriers**: To better understand the needs of the communities, a Spanish-speaking facilitator is required.
- **Limited access to information and resources**: Information on climate change adaptation and mitigation requirements of the government institutions to access available support systems such as the RASB network, among others. The project will address this by providing training in Spanish. Critical information, including guidelines and tools such as PINPAC and PROGROSSE, is available solely in Spanish.

### Institutional barriers
- **Weak local and national governance of water resource management and climate change**: Institutions have limited capacity to lead multi-stakeholder collaboration processes on climate-resilient agriculture and water resource management. The project will address this by providing training for the communities and government institutions that lack the necessary human resources required to meet the additional challenges posed by CC, and to bring together stakeholders in a collaborative process.

### GCF grant support is required to overcome the barriers to climate resilience among vulnerable farmers in Guatemala.

The project seeks the minimum possible level of concessionality within the context of Guatemala's economic situation. Although the Government of Guatemala (GoG) is taking actions to implement climate change related food security policies, it does not have sufficient resources to address the challenges posed by climate change and its associated financial remuneration. Public revenues are insufficient to achieve meaningful reductions in climate change vulnerability, to improve access to water for agriculture, or to provide adequate financing for adaptation measures. The most pressing issues in Guatemala is the lowest in the Central American region. In the case of Guatemala, public revenues in 2017 were USD $8 billion and public debt USD $18 billion. The debt service as a percentage of revenue is 22%. The three main sources of public revenue are taxes on income, property, and sales. These taxes are insufficient to produce and sell enough goods and services to pay off its debts without incurring more debt. Guatemala spent 30% of GDP on social spending in 2017. This includes education, health, pensions, and social security. The model assumes scenarios in which the climate change burden will reach between 38% and 64% of GDP in 2050, depending on the scenario. The GCF is not able to provide sufficient funding for this project because this financing gap uses internal resources. Due to the nature and extent of the climate change risks, the government is not able to build resilience of farmers at scale without grant support from the GCF.

5. Gender equality strategy

96. The project’s strategy for gender equality focuses on making the proposed interventions inclusive, sustainable and resilient, while promoting gender equality to provide an efficient and timely response to the challenges faced by different population groups against the prevalence and incidence of extreme weather events such as drought and prolonged heat.

97. This strategy defines the RELIVE framework for action on gender issues, and focuses mainly on supporting the most vulnerable populations to climate change, understood as those groups whose livelihoods are directly dependent on natural resources, such as fishing or agriculture being highly dependent on the weather, mainly rain. In addition, those who practice it are mostly women, young people and indigenous peoples, who have rates of acute poverty and limited access to productive assets such as land and inputs for agricultural production, technical assistance and financing.

98. On the other hand, the strategy contributes to reducing gender inequalities in climate-related frequent disasters by promoting the participation of women, young people and indigenous groups. The strengthening of the producer association processes and the development of new leadership in the communities, contributes to improve governance in the territories. The objective is to create the conditions for the full participation of women in all stages of the project, ensuring their full participation in the processes of improving their technical and associative capacities and their livelihoods, and making a special effort to include women in all phases of the project.

99. The following are the integral strategic actions:

a. Hire the project staff following inclusive selection processes, setting a hiring quota of at least 35% women and 25% indigenous. These percentages include the recruitment of young people.
b. Reduce the existing gender gap between men and women by including a minimum 35% participation of women in technical institutional strengthening, capacity building in the territory, promotion of leadership and association processes, and restoration and conservation of natural resources, among others, that contribute to the improvement of territorial governance.
c. Ensure the incorporation of gender issues and social inclusion in the capacity development processes carried out by some MAGA units that participate in the project, incorporating women into these institutions in support of the awareness and development processes of capabilities.
d. Ensure that social control and citizenship engagement processes guarantee the representation of vulnerable groups in decision-making: at least 35% of women, 25% of indigenous people, and young people.

e. Establish links with the service for victims of violence against women (Article 4 of the Political Constitution of Guatemala, Article 2 of the Equal Remuneration Convention of 1951, the United Nations Declaration on Indigenous Rights and Article 66 of the Political Constitution of Mexico), the National Commission for Women (CONAMUJERES), the FAD Policy on Indigenous and Tribal Peoples, and Article 151 of the Labor

Code, with project actions in the territories in order to create greater capacities in the municipalities and communities intervened, that can contribute to the reduction of gender inequalities, violence against women and social exclusion.

f. Monitor compliance with qualitative and quantitative indicators through measurement instruments that allow evaluating gender equity and social inclusion through project actions. These instruments must have information disaggregated by gender, age groups and ethnicities.

g. Establish mechanisms to guarantee access, control and equal ownership among men and women, young and indigenous of tangible goods (inputs, technology) and intangible goods (knowledge, skills, experience) in decision-making and leadership provided by the project in order to guarantee the improvement of the livelihoods of the populations in the intervention territories.

h. Support local governments, as well as municipal offices for women affairs and representatives of SEPRED, in the implementation of gender equality policies as a key action to correct existing inequalities in the territory.

k. Document the progress in the activities and results of gender equality and social inclusion in the intervention territories of the project through press, television or local radio, events in the municipality, etc.
