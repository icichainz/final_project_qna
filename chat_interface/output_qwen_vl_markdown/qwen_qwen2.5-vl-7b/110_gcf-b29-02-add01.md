## Selection of Target Atolls

Under the context of climate change and vulnerabilities, it is required to build national land resilience in order to conserve it and maintain a safe, secure and comfortable life for the local residents and to allow them to hand it over to the next generation. In order to achieve this, it is reasonable and realistic to consider the most vulnerable areas as target areas for climate change adaptation projects. The selection of target areas should be as much as possible, and supplementing the required or insufficient function with the implementation of climate and coastal resilience measures in these areas will help to reduce the vulnerability of the local residents and islands: Item 1) impact of climate hazard and vulnerability; item 2) project effects, and item 3) similarity of coastal conditions and coastal problems with other inhabited islands, and validity as model cases. From the results of the above-mentioned criteria, the Atolls of Laamu North and South in the Maldives and the Atoll of Laamu Atol were selected as the target islands for the proposed GCF financed projects. Also, Meedhoo Island in the Atoll of Laamu North was selected as a sub-set of activity financed by the Government of the Maldives (GMD).

## 3. Theory of Change

### Goal
Project interventions aim to shift the paradigm away from current common understanding and action on coastal resilience in the Maldives by modification of natural beaches otherwise using hard structure measures without sufficient climate data and consideration for impact due to artificial modification towards a new paradigm to maintain the natural resiliency of sand beach and coral reef that is the foundation of the Maldives. This will be achieved through the following four inter-related Project Results: 1) establishment of an integrated coastal zone management (ICZM) and to implement the coastal conservation measures at 10 priority islands; 2) strengthening multi-hazard early warning systems for coastal communities and infrastructure exposed to coastal erosion; 3) strengthened multi hazard early warning system for coastal communities and infrastructure exposed to coastal erosion; 4) improved resilience of coastal, coral reef and land use. The Project Results also aim at creating enabling conditions for scaling up and replicating the Project impact beyond the immediate target area. The Project Results are based on the pillars of Ministry of Environment (MoE) in the Government of Maldives and JICA Japan International.

### Key Barriers
To better reflect this transformation, the key barriers to adaptation: 1) lack of regulatory system to pursue coexistence and co-prosperity of development and coastal conservation considering climate change impact; 2) insufficient knowledge and expertise on the proper implementation of soft (nature based) measures for coastal zone and beach protection and long-term sustainability management; to alleviate the loss of natural resilience of sand beach and coral reef that is the foundation of the Maldives. This approach should ensure the maximum mitigation of maladaptation cases experienced by the country.

The reason why beach nourishment method is proposed at above presented three sites as climate adaptation measures on coastal erosion is as follows:

1) Only the method to solve fundamentally for coastal erosion, which are "hard structure measures such as revetment, seawall, groin, detached breakwater, etc." and "soft measures" such as beach nourishment. Based on the characteristics of sandy beaches, they can be easily eroded by wave action and current transport. This means hard structure measures is just protect land side and not to improve the decrease of sand supply. To solve coastal erosion problem fundamentally, improvement or recovery of sand supply should be considered.

2) Multi-functions of sandy beach

Beach nourishment method has functions not only "protection" (which is only function for structure measures), but also "beach user" and "environment". Selection of method (hard or soft) is mainly depending on 1) what kind of beach (e.g. natural vs artificial), 2) what is the main erosion responsible, especially if it is wave action or longshore transport, 3) how is morphology changing over time (long-term vs short-term), and 4) originally sandy beach and still exists, for 2) residential area (for high consideration on beach use and protection of environment), and 3) artificial beach (e.g. reclaimed land), are the main factors to select beach nourishment method.

Natural beaches have been formed through several thousand years; however, once natural beach take place to artificial beach with hard structure measures, coastal protection using hard structure measures will be maintained continuously instead of natural beaches which has been maintained naturally.

3) Similarity of formation, flexibility, and adaptability of sandy beach

Importance of sandy beach is that it can absorb energy from wave action. This is a different phenomenon such as big Tsunami which causes disaster destructively. Combination of coral reef and natural sandy beach can be formed through several thousand years in the same area (e.g., sand volume increases due to increase of wave action). Such flexibility is one of strong advantages of sandy beach, and which is not obtained by hard structure measures.

4) Applicability to coral reef coast

Mildly sloping coral reef coast is significantly smaller to that than for general sandy beach which is facing to offshore waves directly. This means, sand movement and discharge are more difficult in the former case. Therefore, it will be more easy and cost effective for sand replenishment as maintenance work.

5) Transferability of experience from developed countries for coastal measures

As coastal conservation/protection measures in developed countries, beach nourishment as soft measures have become mainstream as coastal protection/conservation measures, based on lessons learned from pilot projects.

6) Experience of similar Projects by JICA

JICA has been implementing several similar projects applying beach nourishment from pilot scale to large-scale in tropical islands with coral reef coasts. One of large-scale projects is "Sai Bok Beach Project" which was implemented for 10 years and maintained the beach for more than 20 years with no sufficient periodical sand replenishment. As the other project, "Technical cooperation project on coastal conservation in Mauritius" is the one success project to scale up the beach nourishment method by strong ownership of Mauritian Governments and communities after the pilot-scale Project.

In other words, a "soft measure" is to protect coasts by not only hard structures but also by enhancement of nature protection function such as beach nourishment (including combination of nourishment and supplementary structures), artificial sand dune creation, sand engine (sand motor), cost reduction measures, etc. These measures are applied in order to reduce coastal vulnerability such as land use control (set-back, securing of buffer zone), ecosystem system, etc. Some of these measures can be categorized as temporary seawalls/revetments, artificial reefs (same as submerged breakwaters) and beach nourishment.

As beach nourishment method, there are mainly two methods, which are "dynamically stable beach" and "statically stable beach". The former is just to replenish sand on beaches without any coastal structures. This method has been commonly applied in the United States, Australia and some of European countries. On the other hand, "statically stable beach" is a combination of nourishment with artificial structures such as groins, headlands, breakwaters, etc. in order to reduce sand discharge after initial beach nourishment.

Private sectors not only in the Maldives but also in other countries have commonly applied "statically stable beach". This method has been used for many years and is still being used today. In contrast, public sector has not been common with this method as it requires more investment than just to dump by-product sand which was obtained from port (navigation channel) construction. This is almost same method as that for "dynamically stable beach", just filling sand without any structures.

### 4. The Gender Context in the Maldives

Compared to most of South Asia, Maldivian women face little discrimination in basic aspects of life such as education, health, and survival; however, gender inequality still exists. Specifically, the Maldives exhibits a classic case of a relatively prosperous country where gender inequalities in basic well-being are largely due to social norms and cultural practices rather than economic factors. The traditional woman’s roles within and outside the home, persist and may be expanding.

Furthermore, poverty and climate change are interrelated. The poorest and most disadvantaged groups are more likely to live in areas that are vulnerable to climate change impacts such as agriculture and fishing which makes them particularly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. In rural areas, these difficulties are compounded by limited access to education, health care, and formal employment.

In urban areas, women face challenges related to mobility, and limited access to resources, services, and decision-making. All of these inequalities combine to diminish their adaptive capacity.


#### 4.1 Gender Inequality Index

Several indices have been developed to quantify the concept of gender inequality. The United Nations Development Programme uses the Gender Inequality Index (GII) and Gender Development Index (GGDI).

The GII presents a composite measure of gender inequality using three dimensions: reproductive health, empowerment, and the labor market. The GII can be interpreted as the loss in human development due to inequalities between males and females across these three dimensions.

The Maldives had a GII of 0.360 in 2018 and ranks 81 out of 162 countries assessed.

## 4.6 Labor Force
