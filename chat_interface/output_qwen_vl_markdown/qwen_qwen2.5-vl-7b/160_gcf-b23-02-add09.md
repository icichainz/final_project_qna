## Project Description & Objectives

The ESCAPE-Tarapaca project ("ESCAPE" or "the Project") is an innovative and unique large scale power project which combines Chile's natural resources with proven generation technology in order to provide clean renewable energy supply 24 hours a day, seven days a week. The Project will be implemented by the Tarapaca Region Government and consists of a 300 MW pumped storage hydroelectric ("PSH") plant using the Pacific Ocean as its lower reservoir and an existing natural reservoir (the Lluta River) as its upper reservoir. The project is expected to provide 1,200 GWh per year.

The Project will set a precedent by providing a reliable baseload solution at a price competitive with thermal technologies, within the context of an electricity grid heavily reliant on high emission energy sources (<45% thermoelectric) and unreliably hydropower (<30% hydropower). The Project is aimed to meet Chile's target of 70% clean energy by 2050. The Project will also provide a reliable baseload solution for the Tarapaca Region, which has no other viable replacement to thermal technology (decarbonizing the current national grid up to 95%). In particular, the Project will stimulate a paradigm shift in the Chilean electric market, as the grid transitions from high polluting conventional energy

## Barriers to Entry & Need for GCF Support

The Project initiated development in 2011 and has successfully completed key pre-construction milestones including engineering design, environmental impact assessment, legal engagement and execution of construction contracts, among others. The initial equity for development of this start-up Project was provided by a group of individual Chilean investors who hold the view that the Chilean regulatory environment is not conducive to investment in renewable energy projects because they do not have the financial capacity to implement the Project—and for this reason, the Project requires incorporation of at least one strategic investor. The Project strongly believes that GCF’s participation will facilitate entry of such a investor(s).

### Barrier 1: Energy policy and regulatory challenges

As presented above, the Chilean regulatory environment is restrictive for investment in the multi-electric system and social benefits which can be provided by bulk renewable energy storage. These benefits, in addition to the provision of energy and firm capacity, include reduced transmission congestion, reduction of CO2 emissions, decarbonizing of the grid with the replacement of coal facilities, reduced reliance on variable wind power, and catalyzation of additional renewable investments, among others, among others. These benefits and services are a critical component in the Project’s business case and will be provided by bulk energy storage only if implemented through this funding proposal.

It should be noted that the Chilean regulators promote “technological neutrality” with due direct subsidies, tax credits or other incentives for new technologies or energy sources, although in fact, the regulator's failure to incorporate the social costs and benefits (such as the cost of CO2 emissions) in the cost of production effectively provides indirect subsidies to incumbent generation. The Chilean regulatory environment has been very restrictive towards local and renewable resources since it has faced opposition from incumbent generators, since the bulk energy storage component would implement a new technology that is not yet proven on a large scale. This is further complicated by the lack of sub-optimal system operation (i.e. infeasibly to quickly respond to energy ramps and transmission congestion during certain hours) as a result of significant injections of variable renewable energy, both challenges that bulk energy storage would help resolve.

### Barrier 2: Securing long-term long-energy agreement

The principal barrier to the successful implementation of this Project is the absence of a stable source of revenue and cash flow which would adequately compensate for the Project for the economic and social benefits it can provide and in which, as well, the Chilean regulatory environment has been very restrictive. The Project requires execution of long-term energy agreement(s) to ensure stable future cash flows and enable the Project to secure equity and debt funding. # C.S. Market Overview (if applicable)

Since the privatization of the electric sector market model in 1992, Chile has had one of the most stable and transparent regulatory frameworks in Latin America. The market model is based on free market principles, including marginal cost pricing, which allows for a competitive environment with limited state intervention to ensure stability and competition. The regulatory fundamentals remain in place today and the limited adjustments over the last 19 years have been made to adapt to new technologies and changing market conditions.

The Chilean government has implemented several policies aimed at promoting renewable energy development in the Chilean power sector, which have targeted lowering electricity prices for consumers via public auctions for distributed generation and the opening of the power fuel source by providing investment in renewables, although no economic incentives are provided.

The retail sale of electric power is split into two customer segments based on peak demand: regulated and unregulated customers. Regulated customers include those with a demand over 5 MW. These customers can purchase up to 0.5 MW or 5.0 MWh out of the unregulated market limit. Customers with a demand over 5 MW are strictly in the unregulated market, which is subject to competitive bidding and price controls. The Chilean power grid is organized into three independent systems: the SEN (SENA), the SERN, and the SECH. The largest regulated system is the SEN, which encompasses most of the country from Araucania in the north to the island of Chiloé in the south.

The Chilean electric power grid is organized into three independent systems. The largest grid, the National Interconnected System (SENA) extends for 31,000 km, encompassing most of the country from Araucania in the north to the island of Chiloé in the south. The SEN has a total installed capacity of approximately 24,000 MW and is divided into three central grids: the SENA, the SERN, and the SECH.

The SEN is dominated mainly by four electric generation companies: Enel, AES Gener, Engie and Colbún. Enel owns the largest stake of generating capacity with 29% of the total installed capacity, followed by AES Gener (17%), Colbún (14%), and Engie (13%). The SERN is dominated mainly by two electric generation companies: ENAP and Colbún. The total installed capacity as of December 2017, Hydropower plants represent 30% of the system, considering all the dam basins in Chile. The rest of the generating capacity is principally comprised of renewables plants, including solar, wind, biomass, and run-of-river plants of less than 20 MW.

Historically, coal has been a dominant source of power generation in Chile, and is expected to continue to hold a dominant share (currently 39% of total generation), considering that many of the coal facilities are relatively new, having been commissioned since 1995. The government has announced plans to phase out coal-fired plants by 2040 with the aim of increasing competitiveness of other technologies (i.e., non-hydro renewables) and gradual decarbonization of the mix. It should be noted that Chile is currently in a transition period, where it is expected that the government will continue to discontinue new build coal plants in Chile and as such, the Ministry of Energy initiated a working group to analyze and design a strategy for the phase-out of coal-fired power generation. The Ministry of Energy has stated that this strategy will require the incorporation of flexible projects such as bulk energy storage. In fact, in January 2019, the report prepared by Chile’s National Energy Regulator recognized the strategic role that storage can play in the process of decarbonization of the energy matrix.

#### 1) Early Equity Investment

The Project has been funded to date by a group of individual private investors, principally Chilean, who were attracted by the Project's unique vision that bulk energy storage is key for developing a 100% renewable electric system and transforming Chile into a global leader in this field. These barriers present large scale, unique and ground-breaking investment deals, such as the Project, from moving forward. The Chilean electricity market, which is currently dominated by state-owned companies, is characterized by a lack of competition, high costs, and low competitiveness and includes the participation of established power companies, making execution of a PPA increasingly challenging for new entrants. Furthermore, the Chilean government has recently introduced several barriers to entry in order to protect existing players from competition. These barriers have prevented the Project from obtaining a solid PPA and in turn, also prohibited incorporation of strategic investors with the required financial capacity and experience into the project.

Given the Project's solid economics, which make it possible to offer an energy price below the average variable cost of existing coal units in future PPA tender processes, we are confident that a PPA will be obtained along with the early stage equity investment. However, if the PPA process is unsuccessful and/or the equity investors are unable to continue funding cash contributions for guarantees (security bond) which are required to participate in PPA tender processes and ensure the Project's financial viability, the Project will not be able to proceed beyond development phase. If this financing process is not successful, the GCF funds will be reimbursed. In addition, GCF's participation as an "anchor" equity investor would provide a strong signal of confidence from the international community that the Project has solid economics and can attract other investors in future PPA tender processes.

Without the GCF support, the Project's short to medium-term capital needs are likely unfunded, risking discontinuation of development. Project's Design and Technological Solution

The Project’s innovative design resolves the intermittency problem inherent to solar and wind technologies by effectively integrating Chile’s abundant sunlight and seawater resources to ensure electricity availability 24/7. The Project design fundamentally differs from conventional pumped storage hydropower (PSH) plants, which use a reservoir of water in a hydroelectric pumped storage capacity to provide coverage during night-time hours. The commercial integration of the two technologies allows the Project to continuously provide reliable power.

EdF’s two principal generation technologies, photovoltaic solar and pumped-storage hydro, are both proven technological solutions that have been successfully implemented by EdF. Both projects have been operating for over 20 years and over 230 GWs are in operation around the world. PSH technology using reversible turbines has been operating since the 1970s and over 14 GWs of installed capacity.

The PV plant consists of a 561 MW AC PV solar park which will be constructed in phases. The solar plant equipment will consist of solar panels, inverters, underground and aerial cables, in addition to meteorological stations, a control room, O&M office and warehouse. The PV plant will utilize a single-axis tracking system in order to maximize energy output.

The PSH plant installations include reversible pumping equipment located in an underground powerhouse. When the Project is generating power during peak hours, seawater from the ocean is pumped into the reservoir via a pipeline to the upper reservoir on top of the coastal cliff, and during dark-hours, the equipment generates electricity with the water stored in the reservoir, which is released and returned to the coast. There will be a single underground turbine shaft for both pumping and generating modes. The turbines and substation will be installed in the underground powerhouse cavern created by the excavation of the existing tunnel. The PSH plant installations include a 65 km transmission line from the site of tunnelling totals approximately 5.5 km. The PSH plant installations will also include a 65 km transmission line from the PSH plant to the existing substation.

- **Enhancement of Enabling Environment**: Implementation of this technology at the La Serena power plant, along with the implementation of PSR technology using seawater will provide experience, knowledge and technical know-how that can be shared with other countries both locally and worldwide.

# Independent Technical Advisory Panel's assessment of FP115

## General background

The Chilean electricity market was deregulated in 1982, thereby becoming the first such market in the region to undergo this type of market segment unbundling. This was achieved by separating generation and transmission functions from distribution and sales activities.

The deregulation of the electricity market resulted in one driven strictly by prices and competitive bidding. The Chilean electricity market is characterized as a "technological neutrality" market, which means that competition is enhanced by lower market clearing prices, thus fostering the competitive development of new technologies. However, it has been argued that this assertion is not true because the non-inclusion of social costs and benefits, such as carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions, in the cost of production, effectively provides indirect subsidies to the development of traditional fossil fuel-based power plants.

The Chilean electricity market is dominated by thermal power plants coupled with inadequate electricity storage capacity on the Chilean grid. There is currently 52 MW of storage capacity in a grid with a capacity of approximately 24,000 MW that is usually needed to incorporate renewable energy sources (RES) into the system.

The development of RES has been slow and limited by the current structure of the framework used to decide which alternatives are technically feasible. What has been referred to as an "Argentinean Gas Crisis" happened in 2006 when Argentina, without any warning, cut off natural gas supplies to Chile. This led to a series of high electricity prices, power shortages, which lasted several years; and the construction of well over 3,000 MW of coal power plants.

The Chilean Government has set as one of its main goals the development of this grid in the future. Key ones that are of importance to the goals and objectives of this financial proposal include: the aggressive adoption of investment in wind energy; the reduction of electricity prices; a reduction reliance on imported fuels (e.g., natural gas imported from Argentina); and an reduced reliance on coal-fired power plants.

Apart from the latter mentioned above (i.e. indirect support for fossil-fuel plant development) due to the current structure of the framework used to decide which alternatives are technically feasible, there is no other direct or indirect support provided by the Chilean Government for the development of wind energy in Chile.

## Adaptation impacts

### (a) The climate change vulnerability of Chile’s energy sector can be summarised as follows:

- **(i)** The past seven years in a row have been catalogued as part of the ten driest hydrological periods on record, with water levels falling to historically low levels and reservoirs being drained down for significant periods, making it impossible to import coal for electricity generation due to the lack of sufficient river water supply.
- **(ii)** Natural gas is also imported from Argentina. A case in point is Chile’s recent history occurred in 2006 when Argentina cut off its natural gas supply to Chile, resulting in a system shock and leading to extremely high power prices and even shortages. In response to this vulnerability, the government has been diversifying energy sources and carbonization of the Chilean grid – approximately 3,000 MW of coal-fired power plants were established during this period.
- **(iii)** The Chilean hydroelectric sector is currently experiencing a drought that means that 30 per cent of the system’s installed grid capacity, corresponding to dam and run-of-river hydropower projects, are not available. This represents an increase in the availability of more than 20 per cent in available hydroelectric energy, when comparing the last five years to the last ten years. In the last five years, for instance, more than 5,790 GWh were generated by hydroelectric power plants.
- **(iv)** Chile’s portable water supply is also impacted by climate change, especially for communities in rural areas and indigenous populations. # Gender Assessment

## Espejo de Tarapacá Project


### 1. Introduction

This gender assessment provides an overview of the gender equality situation in Chile and summarizes the principal findings regarding the gender situation in the community located closest to the Project, the community of Calera de San Marcos (Tarapacá Region – Chile).

Espejo de Tarapacá Project ("EPT" or "the Project") is an innovative and large-scale power project that will generate electricity using concentrated solar power technology in order to provide a renewable “solar plus storage” baseline solution for the Chilean market. The objective of the Project is to develop a 100 MW photovoltaic (PV) plant with a 250 MW/400 MWh pumped hydroelectric storage (PHS) plant using the Pacific Ocean as its lower reservoir and an existing natural lake as its upper reservoir, and (a) a 60+ MW photovoltaic solar (+ PV) plant with single axis tracking.

The Project’s PHS plant is located approximately 100 km south of Iquique, adjacent to the Calera San Marcos community. The Calera San Marcos community has been identified as the most vulnerable community with a local economy dependent almost exclusively on sea-related activities, largely dominated by fishing and aquaculture. The community has been in conflict with the Project developer with San Marcos several years before submitting the environmental permit request to authorities, in order to address community concerns in early development. Collaboration agreements have been signed between the Project developer and the Calera San Marcos community beneficially affected by the Project.

Project development and completion with these affected groups is important to note that the Project will not generate physical resettlement of local communities in any of its components or stages.


### 2. Gender Analysis

#### 2.1 Chilean Background

Chile, particularly within Latin America, has been recognized for its robust environmental and social governance framework, which includes gender equality as a key component. The country’s current population is approximately 17.6 million people. According to the latest census (2017), annual population growth is around 0.93%, as compared to 2.5% in 1980. At present, about 68% of the total population is 15-49 years old and approximately 50% are women. The sex ratio (number of females per 100 males) for Chile is 49%. The majority of the population, 87%, lives in urban areas and nearly 40% of Chileans live below the poverty line.

In most of the previous century, Chile was strongly influenced by traditional gender roles and norms designating women responsible for maintaining the home and childcare. Males maintained the traditional role as breadwinners and decision-makers (see Ley Nacional de la Mujer). In 2013, one of every three women in Chile have been affected by some type of domestic violence. The government has taken steps to reduce poverty, sustain growth, and promote democratization, the role of women within the Chilean

# INTRODUCTION

## CONTEXT

San Marcos is located in the Atacama Desert, 110 kilometres south of the city of Iquique. The inhabitants originally from other regions of the country, founded San Marcos in 1972.

Approximately 300 people live there, and they rely mainly on the sea for their livelihoods.

In 2012, Valhalla arrived in San Marcos with the idea of developing a project that could offer an alternative to hydropower for the country. The project, called Espejo de Tarapacá, would involve installing a Pumped Storage Plant that would use the tides and ocean currents to lift seawater to the upper part of the coastal cliff.

This water would be stored in natural concavities located on the cliffs. When there is no solar energy available, the water would fall from these tunnels and return to the sea, generating hydropower.

Valhalla chose to develop Espejo de Tarapacá in San Marcos because it has an ideal geographical location with numerous natural concavities at the top that permit the storage of sea water without the need to build a dam.

The development of this Project has occurred within a pre-existing context of tension and multiple interests, including tourism and industrial operations. Historically, the relationships between these actors have been transactional in nature, based on the use of monetary compensations to mitigate conflicts during the environmental permitting and construction phases.

This paradigm of engagement has not permitted an adequate consideration of the environmental and social impacts of this type of initiative, and it has hindered the construction of a shared vision for the future plan of long-term development. When Valhalla arrived in San Marcos, this dynamic had already played out in other fishing villages nearby, and local residents had detailed knowledge of these experiences.

This document describes the interaction between the community of San Marcos, a fishing village in northern Chile, and Valhalla, a new Chilean energy company.

THE BEGINNINGS


CONVERSATIONS AND INITIAL REACTIONS


The founders of Valhalla, two young Chileans motivated to contribute renewable energy to their country, arrived in San Marcos for the first time in June 2012. During this visit, they conducted some initial feasibility activities to analyze the possibility of building a dam on the coastal cliff where the water could be stored and rented a boat to study the depth of the bay.


During these few months, they began conversations with representatives of existing community organizations, particularly the Fisher- Men's Union and the Women's Council. Amidst a generalized context of mistrust, the community leaders showed openness to talking with the Valhalla team about the idea of generating renewable energy.


During this period, the continuous initial questions about the project began to emerge: Are you going to bring energy to the village? Who are you? What company do you represent? Why did you come here? The community's isolation in those days. San Marcos still wasn't connected to the electricity grid; its inhabitants had no other means of lighting, and depended on trucks, coming from Iquique, for their water supply.


Some inhabitants of San Marcos also said they doubted that the project would actually be implemented because they already came with the idea of building wind energy projects that never came to fruition. In public, several community representatives feared the harm that the project could cause to their main source of income: fishing. This fear was influenced by the experience of nearby communities with coal-fired power plants and mining operations, which had generated accusations of environmental damage to the marine ecosystem.

### Commitment

The company committed to developing a project that is compatible with the San Marcos village and the marine resources on which it depends.

### Principles

1. **Recognize the community's concerns** and work to address them: The relationship between the community and the company must transparently recognize the community's concerns, but this does not fall into the trap of making that conversation with possible contributions towards the project.
2. **Involve the community in the studies**: The fishermen must know, participate and safeguard their interests during all phases and other aspects of the project's baseline studies.
3. **Transparency**: The community must have access to information on the themes and know the main characteristics of the project.
4. **Be present and accessible**: Generating trust requires time: The company must be present in the village and have a day-to-day interaction that promotes mutual trust.

# REACHING AGREEMENTS

As the dialogue process moved forward, in March 2014 the company and the community began considering a framework agreement that would establish Espejo de Tabarapacá as a contribution to San Marco's local development.

The community and the company's perspectives did not oppose the idea of an agreement; they emphasized that it was necessary to know the terms of reference for the project before starting conversations about subjects such as social investments in the village. For the company, this was essential because making sense of the project wasn't compatible with their marine-related economic activity.

## ANALYSING POSSIBLE IMPACTS AND ELEMENTS OF DESIGN

During the following months, the community and the company analyzed the results of the different studies that would form part of the Environmental Impact Study. They also held conversations that influenced the design of the project and its elements of design. For example, the company decided to install a membrane in the reservoir in order to address concerns about the absorption of metals and salt into the reservoir water, which they feared would generate impacts when this water was returned to the sea.

The company also decided to include a desalinization plant in the project instead of bringing water on trucks, thereby opening the possibility of generating a source of continuous fresh water for the community. One issue that was intensely discussed was where the water intake and outlet would be located. The company had initially planned an opening being located deeper than initially planned, which the company indicated was not feasible for technical reasons. Since it wasn't possible to reach agreement on this point, the company incorporated the community's underlying concerns into its design of the project, including screens to minimize potential impacts. The discussion also led to a commitment to limit the depth of the opening in case of significant alterations in water temperature.

A similar conversation occurred around the location of the intake and outlet pipes at the cliff and reservoir. The community wanted the road to start near San Marcos, thinking this would be more convenient for them. The company considered the options near San Marcos to be unsafe or inaccessible, and opted for a road that started further away from the village and the community, 14 kilometres to the north of San Marcos. This decision triggered a conversation about how the community could benefit from tourism related to the project.

The San Marcos Community and The Espejo de Tarapacá Project

Three months after signing the Work Plan with the Neighbourhood Council, the Environmental Assessment Service (Servicio de Evaluación de Impacto Ambiental) organized a Public Participation Workshop in San Marcos. This workshop focused on the technical aspects of the project, with active participation of the community members. The majority of community members verbally expressed their rejection of the project.

The Environmental Assessment Service also held public meetings in Iquique, Pozo Almonte and Tarapacá. These communities are fishing villages 14 kilometers north of San Marcos. Each has approximately 200 inhabitants and is relevant for the project due to its proximity to the road that will be built as part of the project. In addition, the camp that will house workers during the project's construction phase is located halfway between Río Seco and San Marcos.

Meetings between Valhalla and Río Seco started in April 2014. During these meetings, the company provided an explanation of the main project components, and concerns about possible impacts on the environment were raised. Although some issues have been resolved, the conversation centred on the labour opportunities that the project might provide, as well as ways to support local development.

In October 2014, the company held a workshop with Río Seco, during which the community did not raise any concerns about the project's environmental impacts. It did show great interest in the work and service provision opportunities provided by the project, particularly for women.

Currently, the company and Río Seco are considering options for providing support to this community. The options under consideration include training of local workers in Río Seco, trainings is activities related to the construction phase, and incentives for contractors to hire local workers.

# DIFFICULTIES AND OPPOSITION

Some community members expressed opposition to the project throughout the engagement process. # RESPONSE MECHANISM IN CASE DAMAGE TO THE SEA IS DETECTED

One of the most innovative aspects of both agreements is that they contemplate a response mechanism in case damage to the sea is detected. This mechanism responds to the fact that the community expressed, throughout the entire process, that the company must establish some sort of "insurance" in the case the project contaminates the ocean. Community members insisted on this point because they didn't entirely trust the studies. The underlying question was, what happens to us if the studies are wrong?

## When does this mechanism apply?
If the Environmental Superintendence penalizes the project for having generated environmental damage in the marine ecosystem.

## Who executes the study?
The study will be executed by a team of at least two professionals, one linked to marine sciences and the other to economic sciences. These professionals must be selected jointly by all parties.

## How does the mechanism work?
A study is commissioned that will determine:
- The existence of direct economic harm caused by environmental damage resulting from the project.
- Which people or organizations suffered this economic harm.
- How much compensation will be provided to each affected party.

## Who can be a party?
Residents of San Marcial that work in the sea and are duly registered with fishing authorities, and that have resided in the village for at least five years, as certified by the Neighbour Council.

This mechanism provides that the community can opt out of "taking legal action" against the company to determine materialy the economic damage suffered. For this reason, this mechanism may be swifter and more efficient. Use of arbitration is also possible.

For Espejo de Tarapacá, it was important to agree on this mechanism because it allows the community to ensure that the contributions under the Association Agreements are not used as a "safety net" for the company but rather the manifestation of a commitment to local development.

# LESSONS LEARNED

In interviews, representatives of both the community and the company expressed a series of lessons learned from the experience of engaging around the Espejo de Tarapacá project.

Community representatives highlighted the following lessons:

- **Openness to dialogue:** The experience of engagement showed the community, especially its representatives, that it was necessary for them to be open-minded and communicative, and to see dialogue as a way to express their concerns and interests and defend their rights. This openness also contributed to overcoming existing prejudices on both sides.
- **Long-term vision:** When it came time to establish agreements with the company, it was key for the community representatives that they had a long-term vision of what would be learned from and on how they could contribute to local development, despite the pressure that existed to generate agreements that would provide monetary resources to individuals.

Empowered and informed community:

For the community, it was essential that the dialogue occurred in an environment in which transparency, respect for differences, and information prevailed. In order to create this environment, it was essential that the community, especially its representatives, had access to technical and legal support. Only in this way, by addressing community representatives' sense of vulnerability could the parties develop relationships built on trust.

Company representatives highlighted the following lessons:

* **Adopt a time frame:** Community engagement processes are human processes that require a long period of work in order to generate enough trust to address difficult conversations. Having adequate time is essential for the company to understand the history and origins of the community as well as the social context in which the project would be inserted.
* **Presence in the territory:** The understanding that developed between the company and the communities was a result of several meetings at a human scale that were nurtured daily. In order to build these relationships, it was essential for the company to be present in the territory regularly. The team spent 10 days per year with the community team; spend more than 11,000 hours in San Marcos and its surroundings. This presence at a human scale helped to generate trust between community members and the company at a human level.
* **Understand the social context:** During the conversations about a possible social investment agreement, it was essential for the company representatives to focus on understanding how the project could be compatible with the sea and fishing vocation of the village, and then later shifted to thinking about benefits for the community.
* **Be aware of people/groups that do not feel represented:** It was a challenge to respond to the new Kelp Gathering Union's request. The company had to work on understanding and maintaining the relationship with the originally established groups while also working with this new group. To overcome this challenge, it was necessary to understand that the communities are dynamic and that people can appear that lack clear representation. It is important not to wait for tensions to explode before seeking a way to deal with potential conflicts.
* **Early engagement process:** It was essential to conduct an early engagement process, one that began prior to starting the environmental impact study and making decisions about aspects of project design. Doing so meant that the community's concerns influenced the project design.
* **First understand possible impacts, then talk about benefits:** It was important for this process to separate the conversations about the social investment agreement from those regarding the conversations about a long-term agreement on social investments. This allowed the company representatives to focus on understanding how the project could be compatible with the sea and fishing vocation of the village, and then later shifted to thinking about benefits for the community.
* **Invest in empowering the communities:** It was key to respond to the community's request to empower itself and thereby help it be a solid counterpart during the project. This helped to generate trust between different factions and strengthen trust in the process.
* **Keep working methods flexible:** It was essential for the process that the company had a flexible approach, one that allowed it to adapt to changes and the cultural dynamics of the community.
