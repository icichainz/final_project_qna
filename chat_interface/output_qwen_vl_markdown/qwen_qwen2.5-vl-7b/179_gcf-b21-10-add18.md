# A2 Project / Programme Executive Summary

## PROJECT / PROGRAMME SUMMARY

Please provide a brief description of the proposed project/programme, including the objectives and primary measurable benefits (see investment criteria in section E). The detailed description can be elaborated on in section C.

Climate change in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) is evident from the records, and several biophysical and socio-economic indicators are pointing to its adverse effects. These include increased temperatures, more frequent and intense precipitation events, and changing rainfall patterns. These changes are exacerbating the vulnerability of rural communities, key productive sectors and the overall socio-economic conditions in the country.

The DRC is one of the most vulnerable countries to climate change. The country's economy is heavily dependent on agriculture, which accounts for 30% of GDP and employs over 75% of the population. As a result, the country is expected to face increasing uncertainty and fluctuations in power generation with the changing rainfall patterns. This will have adverse effects on food security and rural livelihoods.

The DRC also has significant forest resources, which are under threat from deforestation and forest degradation. This is particularly concerning as the DRC is one of the second largest tropical forest countries in Africa. The loss of these forests would not only lead to a reduction in carbon sequestration capacity but also have potential implications for other sectors with high potential to contribute to the country's low emission development, such as agroforestry and sustainable land management.

The DRC is the second biggest and fourth most populous country in Africa, which spreads over 2.346,441 km² with a population of about 78.7 million. Yet the DRC one of the lowest rates of electrification in the world. Only approximately 10% of the population has access to electricity. The main reason for this is that the country's power sector is dominated by large-scale thermal power plants, which are owned and operated by the national utility company, SNEL (Société Nationale d'Electricité), accounts for 94% of the total installed capacity (2,687 MW). SNEL has a monopoly on electricity generation in the country. The remaining 6% is generated from small-scale hydroelectric generators, kerosene lamps and battery torches. Their fuel dependence, which is unsustainable and costly, is one of the main reasons for the low electrification rate.

The DRC's power sector is heavily reliant on imported fossil fuels to meet its energy needs. The country has no indigenous oil or gas resources, and it imports all its coal from South Africa. This makes the power sector development in the DRC continue to rely on expensive off-grid solutions with a high carbon footprint if not properly managed.

Recently, green mini-grids are gaining attention as an alternative solution to reverse this trend and accelerate access energy across rural areas of the country. Since 2015, several private companies, including large-scale developers, independent power producers and concessionsaires and individual mini-grid projects have emerged. Still, this has been done in a sub-optimal and fragmented manner with no clear regulatory framework or policy guidance to ensure that these projects are sustainable.

The DRC is currently experiencing an energy transition from fossil fuels to renewable energy sources. The country's installed grid capacity has been steadily growing over the past decade, and clear barriers exist toward full commercial viability especially for renewable energy mini-grid applications. This is due to a lack of policy support, technical expertise, and financial resources.

The DRC government, through the Ministry of Energy (MEN), in collaboration with the World Bank, the European Union, and DFID, is assisting the Government of the DRC (GoDRC) to accelerate, optimize and standardize the development of private-led and renewable-based mini-grids across the country by building a clear, bankable and replicable structure.

The GoDRC has developed a new policy framework for the development of mini-grids in the DRC. The DRC (the Program) will adopt an innovative mini-grid model powered by solar, bringing clean and modern energy to rural communities. This model is based on the successful experience of the Solar Home System (SHS) program in the towns of Bora, Bunia and Gemena (with 487,500 inhabitants altogether), each consisting of a hybrid PV system with a capacity of 12 kWp. The SHS program has demonstrated that solar energy can be used to power clean lighting and mobile phone charging points even under challenging climatic conditions.

# B.1. Description of Financial Elements of the Project / Programme

Renewable-based green mini-grid is still a new business model in most parts of Sub-Saharan Africa including the DRC.

As a mini-grid project is perceived risky and non-traditional, availability of debt financing has been limited and most projects relied on pure grant or equity. The financing barrier was higher in the DRC where the overall private sector development is lagging behind compared to other countries in the region. In addition, the lack of legal certainty and regulatory uncertainties around mini-grid, this has inevitably limited the number and scale of green mini-grid projects in the private sector.

The proposed ADB-GCF debt financing envelope for three pilot solar hybrid mini-grid projects under the Program therefore fills the significant financing gap identified and at the same time pilot a financing structure that will bring the projects to commercial viability. The project is divided into two components: Component 1 covering power generation, battery storage, and battery connections, and Component 2 covering distribution network, connections and backup. The CAPEX for the three projects is expected to be USD 87 million overall, of which USD 60 million will be for the Component 1 including PV plant (USD 25.4 million), battery storage (USD 39.6 million) and grid connection (USD 5.0 million). The Component 2 includes distribution networks (USD 26.1 million), connections and metering infrastructure (USD 8.1 million), emergency backup genset (USD 2.8 million) and other costs.

USD 87 million of CAPEX only covers the initial investment to install the grid and reach 23,300 connections (for all three sites) in five years; it is expected that periodic re-investments will be made throughout the concession period (20-25 years) to replace batteries and other equipment. The total CAPEX for each project is estimated at USD 14 million over the battery replacement cost. Sponsors will be required to arrange re-investment financing as and when required.

The overall USD 87 million (estimated) CAPEX of the three projects is expected to be financed through a blended senior debt providing about 45% of the funds, and a tranche of equity, quasi-equity and investment grant (Figure 1). The GCF contribution amounting to USD 20 million is expected to contribute half of the USD 40 million senior debt tranche which will finance the projects. The inflation rate in the DRC was highest in August 2017 at 73%, which was the peak in recent years and continuously decreased with the latest inflation rate standing at 43.2% (April 2018). The main instrument applied to curb the inflation rate was the Central Bank of the Democratic Republic of Congo (BCDC) raising interest rates from 5% to 7.5% effective January 2017, in order to curb the escalating inflation rate. Although the policy implemented by the Central Bank has been effective, this has come with higher interest rates on lending and a corresponding domestic credit expansion. As a consequence, domestic credit expanded at an annualized rate of 38%.

The country is ranked at 192nd out of 190 countries in the World Bank's 2018 Doing Business report. Financial intermediation services by deposit money banks are limited to about 5% of GDP and financial intermediation services by non-deposit money banks are less than 3 percent of GDP. Retail banking is largely underdeveloped, and most banks act as financial agents for the government or extend credit to the mining sector. The country has a small number of commercial banks, which are also the primary providers of funding for the mining and petroleum sectors. No stock market operates in the country, but a small number of private companies have issued shares on foreign markets. There is no secondary market for corporate bonds or equity securities. With no corporate debt market present in the country, the fixed income market is restricted to short-term government issued treasury bills with maturities up to 28 days, which are dematerialized and easily tradable through commercial banks.

Borrowing options for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) are limited. Maturities for loans are usually less than one year. The majority of SMEs in the country rely on informal financing from family or friends. Some discards loans from providing long-term loans. Since local banks have limited holdings in the domestic currency (Congolese franc), they are not able to provide sufficient liquidity to meet the demand for cash. In 2017, the average CD holding was roughly USD 12 million per bank, though the economic downturn prompted the Central Bank to increase its minimum CD requirement from USD 5 million per bank by October 2017. Foreign currency deposits currently account for almost 90 percent of bank holdings.

The DRC has roughly USD 6 billion of deposits in the banking system, up slightly from 2015. Estimated USD 10 billion of savings are held outside the formal banking sector. The BCDC announced a new policy to reduce the interest increase in bank generation occurred after all 2013 as the GDFC switched public employee payments from cash to bank transfers. This is expected to reduce the amount of money that is not deposited into banks and held in the informal economy, which is estimated at USD 5 billion. According to the Banque Centrale du Congo (BCDC) strategic plan, the aim is to reach more than 20 million bank accounts by 2018.

The country has a large number of microfinance institutions (MFIs), but only about 3% of the population have access to banking services. The Congolese government has established the Association of Microfinance Banks in Congo (AMBC). Most account holders withdraw their balance in full shortly after their salary is deposited.

The banking sector consists of twenty licensed banks. These include well-renowned international banks that operate across the country, as well as local banks with a limited number of branches and operations concentrated in major cities or banks. Though the rate of access to banking services remains quite low in the DRC, mobile banking is fast-growing. The BCDC has been working on legislation to improve the regulation of the commercial banks, the Association of Microfinance Banks in Congo (AMBC), and the legislation for developing shareholder rights, develop the legal and regulatory framework for liquidations and also develop a legal basis for acting as lender of last resort.

# C3 Project / Programme Description

## Overview of Component 1 and 2: Three Green Mini-Grid Projects

The DRC Green Mini-Grid Program will pilot an innovative private-led electrification approach with renewable-based mini-grid projects in three locations in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). The project aims to provide access to electricity, improve energy security, and reduce emissions by displacing the potential future use of diesel and kerosene. High solar irradiance, speedy implementation, and the possibility to locate the power plants near the demand areas thus minimizing transmission-related issues make this solar-based mini-grid approach a viable option for the DRC.

The project will be implemented in three locations: Kinshasa, Goma, and Lubumbashi. These locations are well recognized basic human needs for thousands of Congolese people, and accelerate the country's transition toward a low carbon economy. The project is expected to provide access to electricity to over 100,000 people by 2030. The largest amount of fossil fuel use will be avoided over the next 20-25 years as a direct outcome of the Program. A standardized mini-grid model will be developed and replicated across the country, which will eventually enable the emission reduction at scale beyond the three projects.

The preparation of the mini-grids have been led by the DIFD-supported Essor Accès to Electricity (Essor A2E) initiative in collaboration with the Ministry of Energy and Mines. The project is divided into two phases: Phase I and Phase II. In Phase I we will procure three solar PV mini-grids through a competitive tendering process (to be launched in Q4 2018) in the form of a public-private partnership (PPP). The first component includes the generation, distribution, and LV networks to reach customers. The second component consists of emergency backup generation, distribution, and LV networks to reach customers. In Phase II, the project will focus on replicating the model across the country. A standardized mini-grid model will be identified for each mini-grid, with the payment by end-users will be based on a prepaid system with mobile money to ensure sustainability.

The project is expected to provide access to electricity to over 100,000 people in three locations: Kinshasa, Goma, and Lubumbashi. The project is expected to reduce fossil fuel use by over 250,000 tons of CO2 equivalent (CO2e) per year.

The AIBD-GCF financing envelope under this funding proposal will provide up to US$18 million of blended debt finance for the three mini-grids. The debt component is expected to cover the cost of the mini-grid components (distribution, connections, backup and other costs) be financed by equity, quasi-equity and grants from other co-financers.

#### DFID's Essar Access to Electricity (A2E)

The overall strategy and objective of the DFID Essar Access to Electricity (A2E) initiative is to decrease perceived risks from investment in green mini-grid projects through the provision of pre-feasibility studies, credible processes, standardized concession agreements, and a stapled financing package to selected projects.

The DFID Essar has provided advisory services to the GeoGRC and GeoGCC for the procurement of the projects with an aim to develop a coherent and predictable long-term strategic governance framework to encourage private sector investment in mini-grids. The objective is to ensure that the project pipeline is robust, credible, and investment thereby increase competition in the market, which will ultimately lower tariffs for end-consumers.

In addition, the Sustainable Energy Fund for Africa (SEFA) housed within the AIDB has approved the grant for project preparation of the mini-grids. The SEFA grant will provide technical assistance to the DFID Essar Access to Electricity initiative. Further, the SEFA-grant will help build an enabling framework and stakeholders' capacity for green mini-grid development and management. The SEFA's TA grant contribution will co-finance and scale-up A2E-funded activities.

Three pilot sites with strong unmet demand potentials have been identified for the Phase I through a market assessment (Figure 9). These are Isiro, Bumbu, and Kinsinthea. The project proposes to develop mini-grid sites, proposing a technical solution that balance the cost, sustainability and reach of the mini-grids. The tendering process will be run for three separate mini-grids with each site being awarded separately.

The sponsors for each of the mini-grid will be selected through a competitive two steps process, including an open Request for Proposals (RFP) followed by a second stage selection process. The RFP will include detailed technical and pre-feasibility studies including detailed demand studies, technical studies, normalized financial models including key assumptions, and other relevant information. The sponsors will be required to submit their proposals in the form of PV generation will be set as requirements for bidders. ### Pre-feasibility studies

One of the key concerns of the projects developers, investors and operators is the difficulty to get accurate and reliable demand estimation (anchor off-takers, SMEs, and households), viewed as a "volume risk". Reliable estimates will be necessary for the project feasibility study and for the Concession Agreement. The pre-feasibility studies will go beyond the assumptions of the tendering package and will assess the expected demand growth and to define the appropriate financial structure. Robust pre-feasibility studies demand of the type carried out by international consultants or by international investors and DFIs, have been carried out and will be provided to the bidders as part of the tender package.

### Demand studies require as much granularity as possible with a preliminary geographical scanning of the population and on-the-ground surveys based on statistically significant sample sizes of the different demand segments. The main subsequent steps are: (i) an estimation of the total electricity demand in the concession period; (ii) an estimate of households and small businesses over the concession period on; (iii) the estimated electricity demand (Wh) as a function of the tariff, taking into account the expected price elasticity of demand and the time lag processing of the demand growth. Demand studies also provide a sensitivity analysis and an estimation of price elasticity.

For the demand study, historical and current industrial activities and socio-economic profiles of the three towns have been studied in order to estimate the total electricity demand for each town over the concession period and its forecast. In order to have a comprehensive view of the demand forecast, multiple simulations were developed: including a baseline scenario with no new development and an alternative scenario with significant new development by a strong economic dynamism. For each town, an optimist model was built assuming high suppressed demands and supporting new industrial development (such as agro-processing, chemical and manufacturing) as a result of stable electricity access.

## Existing regulatory regime

The GoR is supporting strategic development and reforms in the power sector, which is in the midst of a significant transition. Key objectives have been outlined for the energy/electricity sector that include liberalization, increased transparency, and improved governance.

The law n°14/011 dated 17 June 2014 (the Electricity Law, "Loi sur l'Electricité") regulates the electricity sector. It provides for the creation of a regulatory authority to ensure compliance with the law, including the implementation of energy conservation and efficiency measures; and energy pricing policies that reflect economic costs for both suppliers and consumers in the liberalized energy market. The 2014 Electricity Law also sets out the generation, transmission, distribution and export of electricity.

The law n°14/011 dated 17 June 2014 (the Electricity Law) repeals the following laws:

- Law n°98/035 dated 16 July 1998 on the liberalization of the electricity market.
- Law n°10/010 dated 1 April 2010 on the creation and functioning of the AIEF.
- Decree n°14/010 dated 21 April 2014 providing for the creation, organization and functioning of the AIEF.

The Rural Electrification Agency (Agence Nationale des Services Électriques Ruraux - "ANSERE") will have the mandate to provide electricity in rural areas. The law n°14/011 dated 17 June 2014 provides for the inclusion of electrification projects led by private or community project leaders.

The Decree n°16/014 dated 21 April 2014 provides for the creation, organization and functioning of ANSERE.

These two agencies are still in the process of being created. A preparatory committee has been established to set up the constitution of the AIEF and ANSERE. The law n°14/011 dated 17 June 2014 directly sets out by its Article 35 specific provision in the Concession Agreement stating that AIEF, once constituted and fully operational, will be able to contest the tariffs agreed in the concession agreement. It is assumed however that if any change in tariff occurs during the period of operation, this matter will be discussed with ANSE or the latter is the functional.

## Mini-grid concessions awarding authorities

The existing law does not state precisely who should be the Awarding Authority in the case of a mini-grid (which covers both generation and distribution). The law n°14/011 dated 17 June 2014 provides for the creation of a "concession agreement" or "délegation de service public", which allows to have both the generation and distribution covered within the same concession contract and which makes the State the Awarding Authority rather than the Province. The Project will be subject to a tender process in accordance with the law n°14/011 dated 17 June 2014 (the Loi sur la Régulation de l'Electricité) but it is still in the process of being established. In its absence, this role is being carried out by the Ministry of Energy and Water Resources (Ministère de l'Energie et des Ressources Hydrauliques - "MERYH"). The law n°14/011 dated 17 June 2014 provides for the creation of a new regulatory authority, the AIEF. Until this is established, the MERYH will be responsible in conjunction with the DFD (Eneo ASE project) UCM will be responsible for the oversight and management of the tendering process as well as the awarding of concessions to private sector developers. The law n°14/011 dated 17 June 2014 provides that the objective is simply to simplify the upstream administrative process for the developers during the tendering process and once the concession is awarded.

## Currency regime

The local currency is the Congolese Franc (CDF), although the economy is highly dollarized with around 90% of the banking sector deposits and lending conducted in US Dollars. ## Tariffs

Under the 2014 Electricity Law, private operators are authorized to propose specific tariffs for each project. # H.1. Paradigm Shift Objectives and Impacts at the Fund Level

## H.1.1. Paradigm Shift Objectives and Impacts at the Fund Level

### Background

The ADB-GCF Green Mini-Grid Program for the DRIC aims to deliver each 5-10 MW solar based mini-grid in three towns of the country, supplying electricity from a sustainable and efficient energy source than micro-diesel generation currently used to bridge the gap. The program will be implemented through the selection of up to five pilot projects selected through the government's tendering process. Use of locally available renewable energy sources is expected to reduce the cost of mini-grid development and possibly to locate the power plants near the demand areas make this technological choice more attractive for private sector investors.

Envisioned mini-grids will provide access to clean energy to approximately 150,000 people in the DRIC, avoiding emissions of 660,000 tCO2e over 20 years.
