### B.1 Climate Change and context (max. 1000 words, approximately 2 pages)

#### Climate rationale: Effective climate mitigation through development of new permanent carbon sinks

When trees are planted on non-forested land, they capture and store additional carbon dioxide (CO₂) from the atmosphere. In sustainable forest plantations¹ the forest is created over time and once the first plants reach the canopy, the CO₂ uptake increases significantly and continues to increase as more trees grow and mature perpetually. With such forest management a permanent carbon stock is created², whilst providing raw material to the timber industry.

Arboto Fund targets creating 75 000 hectares of new forest areas which will sequester up to 20 million tonnes of CO₂ during the lifetime of the programme. Arboto Fund follows the Forest Stewardship Council (FSC)³ for forest management and certification, ensuring that all trees planted are grown on land where no other trees have previously been grown long ago⁴ and therefore Arboto Fund will never replant or convert existing natural forest or forests managed under forest plantations.

#### Methodological approach

To estimate the carbon sequestration resulting from the implementation of the programme, Arboto has developed a robust methodology that is based on internationally recognized approaches. First and foremost, Arboto follows the general guidance of the IPCC⁵ as described in the 2006 Guidelines for national GHG inventories and the 2003 Good Practice Guidance (GP) for afforestation and reforestation activities. The GP also provides a framework that allows more and more countries in their national inventories and reports to the UNFCCC and supplemented by the 2013 Good Practices Guidance as well as the 2019 Reference. It will continue to become the standard approach to be used for all future projects under the Arboto Fund. The methodology is based on a dynamic inventory system⁶ since the beginning of implementation of each project are monitored annually. Only those carbon pools that are changing in time (e.g., stem volume, aboveground biomass) and/or are influenced by climate change or land use changes happen in the tree biomass, mainly in the stem also in the branches, leaves and roots; however carbon is not stored in these pools but only sequestered⁷. The annual inventory system allows to monitor the stem volumes in the plantations is measured first hand through forest inventories annually⁸. Stem volumes are thereafter converted into carbon stocks using a conversion factor.

Arboto only claims net carbon sequestered in forest plantations that is additional and permanent. To do this Arboto uses approaches from the methodologies of the CDM, as well as the VCS and the Gold Standard; the most widely recognized methodologies for measuring carbon sequestration in forests⁹. The baseline scenario¹⁰ (the business-as-usual scenario) is assessed and any potential sequestration in the baseline and sequestration achieved before the project implementation are subtracted from the total sequestration achieved after the project implementation that may result from the implementation of the programme is accounted and deducted from the programme sequestration. All carbon stocks that are not additional to the baseline scenario (e.g., natural regeneration, regrowth) and/or losses due to harvest a maximum sequestration that can be claimed is the long-term average, which is set using the recognized methodology.

### Permanence

Permanence is a concern in reforestation projects. Arbore follows the approach of the VCS and Gold Standard. These certification methods issue permanent carbon credits and thus need to ensure that GHG benefits are permanent. This means that the project must be able to show that the trees will continue to provide economic viability, social benefit, and environmentally sound conditions in the long term. Both terms consider state FSC certification as a proof of sustainability. Following this approach, all of Arbore's projects meet the criteria for permanence.

### FSC was founded in 1993 through joint effort of environmental and social NGOs as well as producers and retailers of wood products. As such, it is a global standard for the responsible management of forests. It requires full participation. FSC requires adherence to principles and criteria which need to be followed in a manner appropriate to the scale, intensity and risk of the project. FSC certification does not automatically last forever. The certified projects go through periodic external audits and in addition, this certification has to be renewed every two years.

### The FSC principles are:

- **Principle #1: Compliance with laws** - The certified entity shall comply with all applicable laws, regulations and national standards governing the management of forests and agreements.
- **Principle #2: Workers' rights and employment conditions** - The certified entity shall maintain or enhance the social and economic well-being of workers.
- **Principle #3: Indigenous peoples' rights** - The certified entity shall identify and uphold indigenous peoples' legal and customary rights of ownership, use and management of land, territories and resources affected by management activities.
- **Principle #4: Community relations** - The certified entity shall contribute to maintaining or enhancing the social and economic well-being of local communities.
- **Principle #5: Biodiversity conservation** - The certified entity shall efficiently manage the range of multiple products and services of the Management Unit (area) to maintain or enhance long-term economic viability, biodiversity conservation and ecosystem resilience.
- **Principle #6: Environmental values and impacts** - The certified entity shall maintain, conserve and/or restore ecosystem services and environmental values of the Management Unit, and shall avoid, repair or mitigate negative impacts on them.
- **Principle #7: Management planning** - The certified entity shall have a management plan consistent with its policy statement. The management plan shall be developed and kept up to date based on monitoring information in order to provide an effective basis for decision-making. The management plan should include criteria and indicators to guide staff, inform affected stakeholders and interested stakeholders and to justify management decisions.
- **Principle #8: Monitoring and assessment** - The certified entity shall demonstrate that progress towards achieving the objectives of its policy statement is being made.

## Additional mitigation benefits through conservation and land use

Creating sustainable forest plantations also means that any existing natural forests or areas with high-biodiversity values, such as old growth forests, are conserved. On average this means that between 20% of the overall project area is conserved, with the responsibility of maintaining the integrity of these areas being placed on the entity implementing the project. These conservation areas may be in the form of a buffer zone around the plantation or they may be separate areas which however are not claimed as part of the programme. Together the plantation area and the conserved natural areas increase the resilience of the overall landscape against climate change impacts. Finally, in case of Arbaro Fund's projects, the conservation areas will also include any areas where timber harvesting is prohibited (e.g., wildlife reserves or saam timber for e.g. furniture manufacturing or construction). In these products the carbon stock continues to be stored and is not released into the atmosphere as it would have been if the timber had been harvested and sold.

Arbaro Fund, by afforestation or reforestation of deforested land and protecting attached conservation areas of natural forests, creates new carbon sinks and contribute to reduced emissions from deforestation and land use change. This will make an important contribution to enable the target countries to achieve their climate targets, as foreseen in the Paris Agreement and the national climate strategy.

### Climate eligibility criteria and conditions

Arbaro Fund is governed by its investment strategy, climate eligibility criteria and its ESMS. Three key building blocks of the investment strategy are:

1. Climate mitigation benefits of the projects and their ability to provide effective carbon sequestration.
2. Long term financial viability of the projects and their ability to provide returns to the investors; and
3. Social and environmental impacts, including the potential for co-benefits from project implementation and operation.

As part of the climate eligibility, Arbaro assesses the baseline, barriers, investment case, funding mobilization potential as well as mitigation benefits (net sequestration impact) of the proposed project together with co-benefit assessment. The results are then used to determine whether the project is in line with Arbaro's investment strategy and ESMS and determine whether the proposed project will proceed in Arbaro's investment process.

In Arbaro’s target countries, FSC certified forests are still rare. The forest area with FSC certification represents a small fraction of total forest area (less than 10% on average). This is because FSC certification is more expensive and tends to be much larger scales than forest plantations. Forest production under FSC standards is not a common product in Arbaro countries, but Arbaro recognizes that needed additional support to become the industry standard and contribute to the shift towards sustainable production in the sector.

## Reducing the vulnerability of local communities through improved agricultural practices

Rural areas in developing countries are characterized by dependence on agriculture and natural resources; high production costs, low productivity, and limited alternative employment opportunities. These factors, combined with distinctive characteristics of rural areas make them uniquely vulnerable to the impacts of climate change and existing vulnerabilities such as poverty, gender inequality, and lack of access to education and health services in many ways. The livelihoods of rural communities in the target regions of the programme rely on subsistence agriculture, which is highly dependent on rainfall patterns and weather conditions.

The projects realised under the programme might involve outgrower schemes or community development programs that will provide training and technical assistance for smallholder farmers to improve their agricultural practices. Such schemes will allow local farmers to use their land more efficiently and diversify their income base. In addition, certain best practice approaches such as agroforestry can be promoted which not only increase the productivity of farms but also support such farmers to select crops that are suitable for their land, thereby reducing climate risks. Similar outcomes can be achieved through the promotion of improved livestock management practices or by improving access through education or the provision of improved planting materials.

## Mitigating the causes of urbanization

By promoting sustainable livelihoods in the countryside, the programme will generate labour in rural areas with no or only limited alternative employment opportunities – with subsistence agriculture often being the only form of economic activity. This will reduce the pressure on urban areas and help to mitigate the migration of young people tend to migrate to cities to find employment and overcome economic disparities. Retaining such talent in rural areas is also important for the long-term sustainability of communities.

#### Adequacy of the financing structure

Sustainable plantation forestry achieves its highest mitigation and adaptation impact when new forest plantations are established on land that is not suitable for other uses. In such cases, a cash flow profile from successful investments creates major shifts to significant investment in the sector. Greenfield plantations require large upfront capital investments (ca. 3-6 million USD per ha) which can be recovered over time through timber sales and/or other forest products. The size of the first investment depends on several factors. For example, if eucalyptus trees are mainly produced for biomass or energy purposes, a smaller initial investment is required than when the main target market is wood pulp. If the target is to produce higher value timber as for the construction or furniture industry, trees can take more than 20 years to mature and require larger investments per hectare (ca. 15-30 million USD). The cost of establishment is recovered over a period of 10-12 years under favourable growth conditions to reach a sufficient height and diameter for high value end uses. ### Converting natural forests and other natural ecosystems

A key component of the project delivery planned in each of its forest plantations is the mandatory FSC certification. This means that in addition, at and near an FSC certified forest plantation, wildlife corridors, streamside zones, riparian areas, and other conservation value areas will be identified and taken into account in the layout of the plantation. A key requirement of the FSC certification is that no natural forests will be converted for any purpose. This means that under no circumstances should natural forests be converted into plantations or other land uses, including conversion of natural forests to afforested or reforested degraded-landscapes for the commercial, yet sustainable, production of timber resources. Besides creating additional carbon sinks, establishing forest plantations on degraded lands can also contribute to improving resilience and reduce the detrimental effects of land degradation.

# Ethiopia

The constitution prohibits gender-based discrimination. Ethiopian government has set a National policy on Ethiopian women in 1993 with the goal of improving the conditions of women in political, social and economic life. In 2006 Ministry of Women's Affairs (MOWA) developed a National Action Plan for Women's Empowerment (NAPWE). The NAPWE is part of Growth and Transformation Plan II (GTP II), one of the Major Targets is to "Develop Women empowerment, participation and ensure their benefits". The National Forest Sector Development Program (NFSDP) also includes women as a key consideration. Ethiopia's National Adaptation Plan (NAPA) identifies gender as a key consideration and recognizes women as particularly vulnerable to climate change due to socio-economic inequalities.

The country is a federation of ethnically-based administrative states. Eighty-three percent of the population lived in rural areas in 2005. Women accounted for 50% of the rural population in 2004, but only 18% of the urban population. Agriculture accounted for 44% of GDP in 2009; industry and services accounted for 33% and 43%, respectively. In 2005, the agricultural sector employed 83% of the economically active population (EAP). However, agriculture is a potential for agricultural development, only about 20% of the total arable land area is cultivated.

Additionally, agricultural production is extremely vulnerable to climate conditions and the disruptive effects of war and civil conflict which have had an impact on Ethiopia. Subsistence, rain-fed farming systems dominate agricultural output. Almost 12 million smallholder farmers produce about 95% of agriculture's share of GDP; more than half of the country's smallholders are women. The main crops grown in Ethiopia include maize, sorghum, teff, wheat, barley and millet. Cereals account for about 70% of the agricultural GDP. In 2005, 12,753,000 women were active in agriculture, accounting for 45% of the agricultural labour force and 77% of all cereal producers.

Land reform was implemented in 1988. As a result, systematic registration and user-right certification have taken place in four regions: in the Tigray region since 1998 and in the Amhara, Oromiya and Southern Nations, Nationalities and Peoples' Region since 2002. Since 1998, more than 5 million hectares of land has been registered under user-right certification. The new federal and regional land use proclamations are the basis of the land reform and they include more detailed rules and regulations for land tenure such as security for investment, arbitration procedures and facilitating investments.

Land is state property with citizens having rights; alienation of their rights is allowed, but land cannot be sold or mortgaged. Foreign investors are permitted to mortgage leased land. Unlike the rest of the country, the Tigray regional state has a gender-progressive land proclamation and progressively implements it. Here, land registration took place very early and both husband and wife have equal rights over land. Upon marriage, they take equal shares of names and upon separation and dissolution of marriage, they take away equal shares of the land.

There is a traditional gender division of labour in the household, with women being responsible for food production and care work within the home and men being responsible for income generation. In productive work, women and men have assigned traditional roles, particularly in agricultural production and marketing: men are considered mostly responsible for land clearing and agriculture while women are seen as the harvest producers and processors, often also the responsible for providing food to the household while men are responsible for providing other consumption goods.

Some crops, particularly plantains and lablab, are considered women's crops, while others – notably cassava – are considered men's crops. However, the extent to which traditional gender divisions of labour are adhered to varies by region, socioeconomic status and rural/urban and formal/informal categories. For example, in polygamous households where there is a single polygamous household, women perform tasks normally done by men. Women are among the worst affected by poverty because they have scarce access to resources and little or no control over their income. In 2013, 58% of all employed women were poor, 72% of all employed women and 90% of all rural women work in agriculture. Women are responsible for more than half the production of food crops and more than 50% of cash crops.

# Paraguay

The constitution prohibits gender-based discrimination. Paraguay developed a national gender in climate change strategy, the "Estrategia Nacional de Género ante el Cambio Climático". Its overarching goal is to promote the active and effective incorporation of gender issues in national climate policy making. In 2007 labour force are 2.9 million people, of which ca. 1.3 million (38%) women. Of the female labour force 32% is dedicated to agriculture, 60% to services and 45% to industry. Many women adopt a dual role in their households and communities. Women's participation in decision-making processes is estimated that 1 of 5 rural households is led by a woman. In rural areas women are typically active in subsistence agriculture and to some extent small agricultural trade. However, most of these activities are not remunerated or marketed. Many women in rural areas have only limited access to education, credit and land.

Almost 9 out of 10 productive agricultural units are less than a ha/ha and often a team of farms owns 80% of the total area cultivated. The rapid expansion of livestock farming, the degradation of natural ecosystems as well as the limited income generation capacity of smallholder farmers are quoted as elevated threats to women in rural areas, as they tend to be relatively more dependent on subsistence agriculture than men.
