### B.1 Description of Financial Elements of the Project / Programme

#### 4. Grant Financing is requested from the GCF to reduce the impact of high-intensity wave activity through the compounding effects of sea level rise and intensifying storm events, that is amplifying coastal inundation and erosion. It is projected that this will be achieved by increasing the coverage of coastal protection along the coast of Tuvalu.

The GCF grant will increase the coverage of coastal protection from the baseline 570m to 2,785m benefiting nearly 29% of the entire population (16,340 people) in the three target areas of Nukufetau, Vaitupu and Funafuti (including Nanumea and Nanumaga) along areas that have a high concentration of houses, schools, hospitals and other social and economic infrastructure.

#### 5. Tuvalu is a Least Developed Country with total population of 10,640. The relatively high per capita income is considered "barely sufficient to cover the high economic cost of business in Tuvalu given its geographic remoteness and relatively limited economic size". National income is heavily dependent on volatile foreign aid, and external indebtedness is high.

Tuvalu's livelihood and economic assets were recently devastated by Tropical Cyclone Pam. According to the Tuvalu Tropical Cyclone Pam Report and Recovery and Vulnerability Reduction Plan, the total loss and damages were estimated to be approximately US$105 million. The recovery and vulnerability reduction activities across eight sectors* are estimated to require a further US$87.64 million. The economic damage is expected to have a significant impact on Tuvalu's economy in the short term, with the potential for long-term effects of mass migration and increased burden on social services. The cyclone has also exposed the vulnerability of Tuvalu's coastal areas, which are already highly vulnerable due to sea level rise and intensifying tropical storms.

Despite the extreme level of vulnerability, Tuvalu currently does not have a single engineered coastal protection infrastructure project that is thought to withstand current and future impacts of sea-level rise and intensifying tropical storms. The existing coastal protection infrastructure in Tuvalu consists mainly of low-lying areas such as Nukufetau and Nukutafai. The combined effects of high upfront investment required for coastal protection, the public good and non-excludable nature of the benefits from such projects, and the lack of political will to implement the project have resulted in a fragmented approach to coastal protection.

The Government and the community have implemented the recognized solutions only at a slow pace and in a highly fragmented manner. The current situation is characterized by low levels of awareness among communities about the need for appropriate response measures; the past initiatives have often resorted to community-scale interventions that hardly address the underlying causes, energy, and financial constraints that drive risks into the design. Within GCF support (i.e., 100% concessionality) is proposed for the project so that, in conjunction with domestic co-financing, Tuvalu can fully implement this project.

#### 6. The GoT has committed US$86.86 million to co-finance the proposed measures of the project. It comprises the Infrastructure Maintenance Budget (US$2.3 million) as well as an in-kind co-financing (US$56.56 million). The Infrastructure Maintenance Budget is intended for the maintenance and repair of existing coastal protection infrastructure, which are the coastal protection infrastructure constructed in the project. # C1. Strategic Context

Tuvu is the fourth smallest nation in the world. It comprises nine inhabited islands with a population of 10,640. The total land area is 28 km², where the majority of people live on the low-lying coastal areas. The average elevation is 1.6km. Tuvu is one of the most vulnerable countries in the world to the impacts of climate change, particularly to projected sea-level rise and increases in the severity of cyclones. When combined with considerations of poverty, limited economic development, and a high degree of dependence on foreign aid, the vulnerability of Tuvu's economy and people to the adverse effects of climate change is likely to have severe long-term effects on sustainable development of the country.

## 1.3 Distance from international markets, remoteness or one island status

The small size of the country and economy, which are highly dependent on external shocks, and a limited natural resource base present significant economic development extremely challenging for Tuvu and the government of Tuvu to provide employment to 15% of the adult male population. There is also reliance on income from the Tuvalu Trust Fund. The economy is fragile as fishing revenues and foreign aid are highly volatile. The country's economy is dependent on the global economy, IMF reports indicate that Tuvu's economy has contracted by almost 41% of GDP by the end of 2013. The debt is expected to have risen to 58.9% in 2014 due to a recent loan agreement on a fishing joint venture. The share of external debt as a percentage of GDP for Tuvu is among the highest in the region, and the country's economy is even more so, being among the smallest in the region, highly dependent on foreign aid.

## 1.4 Considering the small size and fragility of its economy

In addition to its physical and environmental vulnerability, Tuvu is also vulnerable to climate change impacts. The Pacific Climate Change Science Program (PCPS) study recently concluded that tropical cyclones are expected to decrease in number by the late 21st century but become more intense. This will have significant economic implications on future damages in terms of human lives, infrastructure and livelihood assets as research indicates that, per year, the average damage from a tropical cyclone is estimated at US$30 million (US$54 million if the sea level near Tuvu has risen by about 0.5 mm per year since 1999 [a total of 9 cm over this period]). Under a high emission scenario, the projected increase in storm surge and wave heights will be more significant than current manifestations of climate change – continually intensifying cyclones and sea level rise - will have dire impacts on Tuvu. The country is also vulnerable to other extreme weather events such as droughts, floods, landslides, damages to natural assets such as Tuvu's fragile groundwater layer and many other economic/social/cultural assets.

#### SMALL AND LOW-LYING ISLANDS

The geographical features of the islands, characterized by their small land area with low elevation, make Tuvalu particularly vulnerable to climate change impacts including sea level rise and coastal erosion. Tuvalu occupies a total land area of 26 km² (10 sq mi), making it one of the world's smallest countries in terms of land area. It is also one of the world’s most densely populated small island states, with a population density of over 35 people per square kilometer.

#### HIGH COASTAL LENGTH RATIO

The high coastal length ratio (the length of coastline relative to the landmass) of the country has been acknowledged as an effective indicator of vulnerability. Tuvalu is one of the countries that have the highest ratio in the world, and it is among the countries with the highest risk in the world.

#### GEOGRAPHIC AND ECONOMIC ISOLATION

The nine islands of Tuvalu are dispersed with the entire island chain extending over 500 km (310 mi) from north to south. The high cost of shipping goods and services, coupled with high costs, and unstable communication networks make the exchange of people, goods, and information between these islands extremely difficult.

#### NARROW ECONOMIC BASE

Although incidence of absolute poverty is low in Tuvalu with a GNI per capita at $5,260, many experience economic hardships, where they do not have sufficient opportunities to access financial resources to cover basic needs. The national revenue base is heavily influenced by the fluctuations of the global economy.

#### CLIMATE CHANGE IMPACTS

Climate change impacts are putting additional strains on Tuvalu’s efforts towards attaining sustainable development. Available climate change projections suggest that Tuvalu will face rising sea levels, increased storm surges and more frequent extreme weather events in the future. The two projections would pose a significant threat to the country where average elevation is barely above 4m and damages from wave action are already widespread.

#### ECONOMIC IMPACTS

Despite the level of exposure and vulnerability of the country with vulnerable coasts extending for about 8km, the current country does not have a single coastal protection structure that withstands the future combined impact of sea-level rise and increased storm surges. The lack of such structures has led to significant economic losses in the past. Once extreme events strike, as seen during Cyclone Pam, the country faces considerable setbacks in terms of economic recovery.

# F2. Technical Evaluation

This proposal builds on an analysis of international best practices, extensive experience of the authors of this project in implementing similar projects in the Pacific, lessons from on-going efforts in Tuvalu, and several pre-feasibility assessments carried out in the country recently.

A Feasibility Study assessing the various options for consideration including: hard engineering solutions (e.g. seawalls), soft engineering solutions (e.g. groynes), and a mix of both, of international best practices (ranging from 'retreat', 'accommodate', or 'protect') reveals that an effective use of 'protection' with all its associated costs is not currently feasible in the short term. The only options available are to retreat or to leave things as they are.

Previous studies (JICA 2011; UNEP 2014; World Bank, 2015) have also identified several coastal hazards but to address these hazards and associated problems currently being experienced, a feasibility study, supported by the LDCF-funded UNDP project, was carried out in two islands of Nukutavake and Nanumea. The World Bank assessment was carried out on Funafuti.

The following pages provide an overview of the technical evaluation of the various options for consideration in detail in the application of gravel beach nourishment and reclamation along 2.6 km of the western (lagoon) shore of Tongatapu Island in the Funafuti atoll.

Both the UNDP project and World Bank reports suggest that structural solutions utilizing geo-textile sand container systems are a cost-effective erosion mitigation measure for the outer islands. As described earlier, geo-textile revetments are considered suitable in the context of outer islands because of several reasons: first, they offer some flexibility when there is a need to adjust the location or length of the structure; secondly, they provide a more flexible and easier mode of logistical management compared with other engineered solutions, which is critical in outer islands where landing facilities are limited.

The technical evaluation of the various measures for different sites suggests a mix of hard and soft engineering solutions. In Funafuti, the proposed project aims to rehabilitate 1,000 m of the eroded and degraded foreshore on the lagoon side of the island. The site is located in the western part of the island, which is the most vulnerable area due to its largest population center and is the center of commerce and trade for the nation. Based on the feasibility and scoping studies already carried out, it was concluded that a hard engineering solution made from precast concrete units (Seabee) would provide the most cost-effective solution for the condition in Funafuti.

For Nanumea, a geo-textile container revetment is an attractive and viable option for the following reasons. Presently, there are no structures along the vulnerable shoreline of the island. The vulnerability of this area is due to the erosion of ecosystem-based adaptation options such as sand redistribution and beach nourishment, dune and beach ridge reclamation, and mangrove planting. The most critical issue in Nanumea is the lack of a hard structure that can protect the vulnerable shoreline facing the outer ocean, i.e., high level of exposure, but rejected immediately due to the lack of space for construction on the island. A geo-textile revetment constructed between the village and the beach would impede pedestrian access forcing people to use alternative routes which may lead to premature and unnecessary damage to the structure itself. Alternatively, if a gap were left in the wall for pedestrian access, the structure could be easily damaged by vehicles or other heavy equipment. Therefore, it is recommended that the implementation of this solution would therefore require prior public consultations to understand the mobility paths and needs of the community.

## Selected Risk Factor 10
### Description
Terrestrial and marine noise including through the use of construction equipment and material dumping will occur as a result of this project. This can impact on local communities and marine and terrestrial fauna using the adjacent area.

### Risk category
Social and environmental

### Level of impact
Medium (5.1-20% of project value)

### Probability of risk occurring
Medium

#### Mitigation Measure(s)
An assessment of the terrestrial habitat where the coastal protection infrastructure is to be located should consider any sensitive receptors including communities. Further, noise shields should be constructed to reduce the potential for noise to reach local communities and marine and terrestrial fauna using the adjacent area. The construction of the coastal protection infrastructure will also need to take into consideration the final location of coastal protection infrastructure to ensure underwater noise does not impact marine organisms and animals.

## Other Potential Risks in the Horizon
### Potential risks in price determination
The cost of materials for the project will likely involve minimum of two contractors: first to carry out island-level assessments in all islands; second to construct the coastal protection structures as per the detail drawings produced as a result of the island-level assessment. The contractor selected for the construction work is expected to be awarded at scale on the project, there is a certain level of uncertainty in the pricing of the final work contracted.

Although the cost estimates have been developed with careful consideration and through consultations with experts who have worked in the region, the overall project budget must be carefully monitored.

### Potential risks of community engagement
Community engagement will be an issue that is shared by all levels of society. At the same time, there is a sense of "consultation fatigue" within Tuvalu communities. This is because of some past development assistance that had a series of consultations without concrete benefits on the ground. This could potentially create impatient demands to see the outcomes of this project and may result in delays or even rejection of the project if it does not deliver.

The island status, implementation schedule, expectations, and community engagement requirements will be clearly defined at an early stage.
