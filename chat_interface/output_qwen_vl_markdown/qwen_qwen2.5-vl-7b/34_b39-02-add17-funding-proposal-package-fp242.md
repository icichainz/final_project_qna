## Small Island Developing States (SIDS) are particularly susceptible to the impacts of climate change.

As developing economies rely on sectors vulnerable to climate patterns such as tourism, agriculture and fishing, SIDS in the Caribbean region face significant challenges. These include increased frequency and intensity of extreme temperatures, impacts on the natural habitat (i.e., bleaching of coral reefs), and increasing intensity of natural disasters. While women are disproportionately affected by gender-based exposure and expertise suggests that women and girls face higher levels of vulnerability as well as gender gaps in high-value crops, water management and sectors such as fisheries and agriculture, as well as gender gaps in health care and disaster risk management and response. Reduced access to transport and other infrastructure services has an impact on the delivery of essential goods for health care and other essential services.

Furthermore, Small Island Developing States (SIDS) in the Caribbean are presently heavily reliant on fossil fuel energy imports, which not only compromises the competitiveness of their economies but also results in high greenhouse gas emissions on a per capita basis. Consequently, reducing the dependence on these expensive fossil fuel imports through the adoption of renewable energy technologies and energy efficiency measures is critical to achieving climate change mitigation goals.

Initiatives that contribute to building economic growth and bolstering the fiscal capacity of Caribbean SIDS governments are also essential for ensuring long-term sustainability of adaptation and mitigation efforts. This presents an opportunity for Caribbean SIDS to upskill current workers and shift existing profiles toward more sustainable, low-carbon pathways.

These countries have committed to migration and adaptation actions, with estimated costs surpassing USD 52 billion. However, existing financial commitments in these countries only slightly exceed USD 4 billion, resulting in a significant gap of USD 48 billion. To address this funding gap globally, the private sector manages USD 210 billion, but a very small fraction is dedicated to climate investments.

The accumulation of SIDS in the Caribbean is driven by recurring disaster-induced damages, including from climate change and extreme weather events. The combination of fiscal constraints and climate impacts makes relying solely on domestic funds for adaptation and mitigation efforts challenging. In addition, limited access to financing, particularly for small-scale projects, further hampers the region's limited private sector capacity in green business and green finance as well as the appropriate financial instruments.

The barriers to paradigm shifting pathways towards net-zero and climate resilience are related to macroeconomic constraints, limited access to financing, externalities and associated market failures, lack of knowledge on adaptation and mitigation options, regulatory frameworks that support or hinder technology deployment, high cost of access to technologies, social inequalities for women as well as other vulnerable populations, inadequate institutional capacity and coordination among stakeholders, weak public-private partnerships, limitations in the availability of data and information, limited sector-wide knowledge on adequate business models and bankable adaptation and mitigation solutions.

In this context, IDB aims to establish the Caribbean Net-Zero and Resilient Private Sector Program (the "Program"). The Program seeks to catalyze funding in support of net-zero climate-resilient private sector investments in the Caribbean region. It will focus on five priority sectors: Agriculture, Forestry and Other Land Use (AFOLU); Sustainable and resilient infrastructure; Electricity including renewable energy and energy efficiency; Sustainable transport; and Blue Economy.

The Program's 5 priority sectors are:

1. Agriculture, Forestry and Other Land Use (AFOLU)
2. Sustainable and resilient infrastructure
3. Electricity including renewable energy and energy efficiency
4. Sustainable transport
5. Blue Economy

The Program will address identified barriers to conceptualize climate change projects and devolve investments. This Program will support the private sector's alignment with adaptation and mitigation efforts in the SIDS Host Countries by providing technical assistance, capacity building, and financial resources. The Program will also seek to deploy and demonstrate commercially viable adaptation and mitigation solutions in the region. Furthermore, across all priority sectors, the Program will provide technical assistance and financing through IDB's gender mainstreaming process to identify priority areas for closing gender and/or diversity gaps.

B.1. Context (max. 100 words, excluding footers if any pages)

This section describes the context and rationale of the Program. Please refer to Annex 2 for comprehensive information on all aspects.

Key geographical characteristics of the Caribbean Islands

The islands in the Caribbean are relatively small; only 13 have an area over 1000 km². The terrestrial land area of the Caribbean is ca. 230,000 km² and nearly 87% of it is in the five largest islands of Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica, Puerto Rico, and Trinidad & Tobago. The region has a high degree of biodiversity; the biodiversity of the Caribbean include the high-endowment on individual islands or island groups, the diversity of habitats, and the presence of endemic species.

SIDS Host Countries have an aggregate population of 17.8 million people, with an uneven distribution across the eight countries, where approximately 75% of the population is concentrated in the Dominican Republic and Jamaica, and the area of SIDS has a population less than 10% of the total population in the region.

Climate change problem

SIDS are particularly susceptible to the impacts of climate change. As developing economies relying on sectors very sensitive to climatic conditions, SIDS will be disproportionately affected by the ongoing rise in sea levels and increases in sea surface temperature, changes in rain patterns and landforms, impacts on the coastal zone, and increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events. The projected direct disaster losses from climate change-related disasters in the region have increased by 0.1% to 2.6°C per decade over the past three decades, and rainfall patterns have shifted; with the number of consecutive dry days expected to increase. Additionally, sea level rise is projected to cause a significant loss of land area in SIDS, which presents risks to the region’s freshwater resources and to its largely coastal population dependent on tourism and fishing activities. The impacts of climate change are also felt through increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events (e.g., hurricanes, droughts), changes in administrative roles and informal employment, adding to the multidimensional vulnerability to risk. In the climate change projections for SIDS, the number of people living below the poverty line is expected to increase; losses total US$22 billion annually by 2050, roughly a 10% of the current Caribbean economy. According to the World Bank, the cost of adaptation in SIDS is estimated at $34 billion per year and could be as high as approximately US$135 billion in losses from 165 weather events, mostly storms and floods.

Given their small population, SIDS' contributions to global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions are negligible. However, the region's vulnerability to climate change is much higher than that of the world average (4.4°C/2050). Even though mitigation interventions in the Caribbean may have a relatively small impact in terms of reducing global GHG emissions, they will be critical for achieving the emission reduction targets committed by SIDS; it is crucial for these countries to meet the emission reductions targets committed in their Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) under the Paris Agreement.

# Historical trends and projections in the Caribbean region

## Hazard: Air temperatures

### Historical trends
The Caribbean has experienced warming over recent decades.

### Projections
- The GCMs (SOGMA) suggest that the central and southern Caribbean basin is expected to experience a reduction of up to 20% in the number of days with maximum temperature exceeding 35°C by the end of the century.
- The GCMs predict a slight decrease of about 2% in the annual mean minimum temperature, from around 29.5°C at present to just below 28°C by the 2060s and projecting up to 17°C by the century's end.

## Hazard: Sea Surface Temperature (SST)

### Historical trends
- SST has increased over recent decades.
- The increase in SST is projected to continue into the future.

### Projections
- Under a business-as-usual scenario, SSTs are expected to increase from 0.5°C to 1.2°C by the end of the century (relative to 1986–2005).
- Under an RCP8.5 scenario, SST is projected to increase from 0.7°C to 2.3°C in the 2030s and to 2.7°C in the 2090s.
- By the end of the century under this scenario, SST will be within the range of the warmest years in the present.

## Hazard: Sea levels

### Historical trends
The Caribbean has experienced a rising trend in the sea level of the order of 1.8 ± 0.5 mm per year between 1946 and 2007.
- Higher rate of increase in later years, with an acceleration between 1993 and 2015.

### Projections
- The projected range for sea level rise is from 1.3 to 2.8 m by the end of the century relative to 1986–2005.
- The range is 0.73 to 2.04 m, based on a scenario consistent with the RCP8.5.

## Hazard: Hurricanes

### Historical trends
The frequency and duration of hurricanes have increased in category 4 and 5 hurricanes over recent decades, associated peak wind intensities and mean rainfall for same period.
- The frequency of hurricanes has increased in frequency of hurricane occurrence from just below 1% to around 2.7% per year since the mid-1980s.

### Projections
- Under a business-as-usual scenario, the trend towards more potent storms is expected to continue into the future with an increase in frequency from 2% to 11%.
- The intensity of these hurricanes might experience a notable escalation of up to 25% on rainfall rates, while the outer bands could become wider and extend further inland.

## The need for adaptation

The following section presents climate threats in the respective countries as well as potential solutions that could be employed to address these threats. The list of adaptation measures proposed for each project will be based on market demand and analyzed with a gender lens, with relevant KPIs where applicable.

### 1. Agriculture, Forestry and Other Land Use (AFOLU)

#### Potential Adaptation Solutions

- **Efficient Water Management:** Drip irrigation systems to capture and store rainwater for agricultural use.
- **Improved Crop Varieties:** Select crop varieties that require less water, crop rotation, and intercropping.
- **Early Warning Systems:** Early warning systems to provide timely information on extreme weather conditions, the gender-differentiated digital divide and use of ICT; gender-sensitive early warning systems.
- **Agriculture Insurance:** Provide insurance for farmers against the impact of extreme weather events. Agriculture insurance can be provided in the form of crop insurance or index-based insurance.
- **Coastal Zone Management:** Develop and implement coastal zone management plans to protect existing green belts and buffer zones to reduce erosion, provide protection from storm surges, and promote the growth of salt-tolerant crops varieties that are tolerant to saline conditions in case of increased salinity. This will also help to preserve seeds that adapt well under changing coastal conditions.

### Vulnerable groups

Climate change presents unique impacts among vulnerable populations, and these disparities are closely linked to social inequalities and the persistence of poverty. For instance, residents of informal settlements and marginalized neighborhoods in urban areas face additional risks due to a lack of resilient infrastructure and basic services such as water supply and sanitation. These communities often rely on local markets for food, which can have devastating consequences in these communities by affecting housing, workplaces, and livelihoods.

People in extreme poverty are particularly vulnerable to climate change due to their limited access to resources, shelters, and other protective measures. They may also be more susceptible to the impacts of climate change on health and housing. According to research from the World Bank (2021), in the most critical scenarios, climate change could increase the number of people living in extreme poverty by 35 million.

Within the impoverished world, certain groups face a higher incidence of poverty, such as women, children, people with disabilities, and indigenous communities. For example, data from the Observatory for Gender Equality in Latin America (2019) shows that women-headed households are more likely to be poor than men-headed households; there were 117,246 women in similar situations, highlighting their lack of economic autonomy. Climate change can exacerbate this situation by directly and indirectly affecting food security, pushing these groups into chronic poverty.

The impacts of climate change also reflect in access to essential resources for daily life, such as water, shelters, and sanitation facilities. Women are particularly vulnerable due to difficulty or insecurity in accessing these services, which has implications from a gender perspective and time use. Surveys on time used conducted in various countries in the region indicate that women spend more time than men on activities related to food production (The Association of Commonwealth Universities, 2021). Women, especially rural Indigenous, and women in subsistence farming are primarily responsible for family food security and water collection, making them particularly vulnerable.

In the Caribbean, there are several indigenous populations, and they are among the most vulnerable to climate change. From the First Peoples of Trinidad and Tobago to the maroons of Jamaica, and with at least nine main ethnic groups occupying the islands, these communities have a rich cultural heritage and extensive traditional knowledge about the environment (The Association of Commonwealth Universities, 2021). However, changes in climate patterns are affecting their livelihoods.

For example, the report policy "Gender Inequality in Climate Change and Disaster Risk in Guyana" (2021) points out that Indigenous women are particularly vulnerable to the impacts of floods and droughts due to their dependence on natural resources for food production and reproductive roles, like ensuring water, food, and fuel security, as well as their limited mobility.

Finally, people with disabilities face additional challenges during extreme weather events and in subsequent adaptation and recovery efforts. The lack of accessible infrastructure and services increases their vulnerability.

## Barriers

While countries in the Caribbean have shown political will and leadership advancing their pledges towards carbon emissions reductions, there are several barriers that need to be addressed. These include:

- **Macroeconomic constraints**: The current macroeconomic conditions with high inflation and interest rates, coupled with limited access to credit, make it difficult for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in the Caribbean to attract international investors to invest in small projects with higher transaction costs; companies with limited borrowing capacity are unable to access credit at reasonable terms.
- **Limited access to financing**: Private sector companies in the Caribbean face several structural challenges, such as lack of access to finance and high interest rates. The cost of capital is also a significant barrier for SMEs, especially those operating in the Caribbean Small and Medium-sized Enterprises (SMEs). Climate change framework

SIDS are recognized among UN institutions as being particularly at risk of climate change. Caribbean SIDS have high fiscal debt. In 2018, the total debt burden of the Caribbean stood at US$65.2 billion, representing over 70.5 % of subnational government revenue. The region is also highly exposed to natural disasters and extreme weather events, which often restricts their access to concessional loans, as many sources of concessional funding require the project to be located in a low-income country. In addition, SIDS are subject to high interest rates on concessional loans and high interest rates applied to debt-based financial instruments, which in turn exacerbates existing debt levels. The accumulation of debt is further exacerbated by climate change impacts such as increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events, loss of productive capacity caused by disasters, including climate change. Regular annual disaster losses in the Caribbean are expected to increase from US$1 billion per year (2005–2014) to US$3 billion per year (2015–2024). The number of people exposed is expected to escalate in the Caribbean due to population growth and rapid urbanization of coastal areas where the impact of hydroclimatic events is greatest as well as increasing exposure of assets, and climate change-related migration.

However, there are also opportunities for SIDS to benefit from the growth of climate finance markets. One noteworthy example is the UN Task Force on Climate-Related Financial Disclosures (TCFD), which is dedicated to developing consistent and comparable disclosure standards for financial institutions, including sovereign wealth funds, insurance companies, banks, and investors to keep stakeholders informed. Additionally, there are established frameworks, such as the Green Bond Principles (GBP) and the Sustainability-Linked Loan Principles (SLLP). These are the prevailing standards worldwide for issuing green, social, and sustainability bonds® However, these initiatives are still in their early stages and further efforts are needed to achieve their objectives and bridge the gaps.® Approximately 24% of financial institutions in Latin America and the Caribbean have issued green or sustainable bonds.® In addition, it is acknowledged lacking the necessary mechanisms to identify, evaluate, and manage climate risks in Latin America and the Caribbean.® The majority of companies are still with only a 20% compliance rate across the 11 recommended disclosures of the TCFD for the region.

Consequently, drawing in private sector participation requires the development of institutional capabilities to embed climate considerations into financial decision-making processes. This can be achieved by establishing an enabling investment environment and, primarily, the creation of "bankable projects", which can be positioned as offering substantial returns to investors.

## Alignment with Paris Agreement

All Sub-Projects must also be deemed as aligned with the Paris Agreement under ICF Invest’s climate strategy. In addition to the general alignment criteria by GCF Invest, the Sub-Project must comply with the following additional criteria for alignment with the Paris Agreement and Concessional Finance for Blended Sector:

### Additional Criteria for Alignment with the Paris Agreement

1. **Principle 1: Additionality:** DFU support of the private sector should create a condition that is beyond what is available, or that is otherwise absent from the market, and should not crowd out the private sector.
2. **Principle 2: Crowding-in and Minimizing Concessionality:** DFU support to the private sector should, to the maximum extent possible, be provided in a manner that does not crowd-out concessional finance.
3. **Principle 3: Commercial sustainability:** DFU support of the private sector and the impact achieved by each operation should aim to be sustainable. DFU support must therefore be expected to contribute towards the long-term financial viability of the operations.

## Mitigation Measures

The 2020 IDB Invest Sustainability Framework mitigates the risk of recipients not complying with national regulations, as well as IDB Invest and GCF environmental, social policy, gender requirements, and standards. This risk mitigation is achieved through the following components of the framework:

### IDB Due Diligence and Oversight
The framework includes a thorough due diligence process that ensures recipients adhere to all relevant policies and procedures. These encompass the 2020 Environmental and Social Sustainability Policy, the Access to Information Policy, and the IDB Invest Exclusion List. These policies serve as comprehensive guidelines for ensuring compliance. The framework's oversight and advocacy offer further support to recipients in meeting these requirements.

### Requirements for Real Sector Clients
Real sector clients are obliged to adhere to the policies mentioned above. They are also required to implement the 2016 IFC Performance Standards for managing environmental and social risks within their operations, as well as comply with the International Finance Corporation (IFC) performance standards established by the World Bank Group. These requirements collectively reduce the risk of non-compliance with environmental and social policies.

### Investment of Financial Institutions (FI)
FIs that provide loans and investments made through financial institutions undergo a dual-layer risk mitigation process. First, financial institutions must establish a robust Environmental and Social Management System (ESMS) to ensure compliance with national regulations and IDB Invest standards. Second, the FIs conduct a comprehensive screening process to identify and mitigate environmental and social risks. By limiting funding eligibility of FIs to projects with a moderate risk profile, the framework effectively addresses the risk of non-compliance.

In conclusion, the IDB Invest Sustainability Framework mitigates the risk of recipients failing to comply with national regulations, IDB Invest and GCF environmental, social policy, gender requirements, and standards. It does so by providing comprehensive guidelines for compliance and implementing a dual-layer risk mitigation process that significantly reduces execution risks and enhances project success.

## Introduction

To robustly assess the level of gender inequality which persists in the Caribbean, gender-disaggregated data is key. This, however, is limited as less than half of all indicators used to monitor progress towards the Millennium Development Goals are collected and reported by each regional statistical system. This includes data gaps on gender-differentiated wage earnings, financial inclusion, time use and unpaid care work.

While women in the Caribbean experience greater economic vulnerability compared with men, they also face other vulnerabilities that cannot be ignored. While women and girls in the Caribbean experience favorable outcomes in health and education, opportunities exist to improve and support gender equality in these areas as well.

While women have a consistently higher life expectancy at birth than Caribbean men, and one that is comparable to their female counterparts throughout the region, this is not always indicative of the quality of care received. The incidence of maternal mortality is higher among women in the Caribbean compared with other regions globally. Access to healthcare is publicly provided in several countries, but women have a higher likelihood of mortality due to non-communicable diseases and injuries.

Improved educational outcomes among girls in the Caribbean have not always translated into improved gender equality in the labor market. While data shows Latin America and the Caribbean to be one of the regions with the highest female participation rates in education globally, comparative studies specific to Caribbean states find evidence of reverse gender inequality in educational performance (ECLAC, 2012). Girls outperform boys at the primary and secondary schooling levels, even in countries where girls are less likely than boys to complete their education. However, women and girls have a higher representation in enrolment and graduation shares compared to men and boys.

### Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries and Other Land Use (AFYOLU)

Despite Caribbean countries' position in global food trade as net importers, agriculture continues to be one of the main drivers of economic growth and employment across the region. The sector is also critical for employment and food security.

However, a high level of gender-based occupational disaggregation persists throughout the region, whereby women are more likely than men to work on small farms and in subsistence farming. Women have limited access to land, credit, and other productive resources (CDB, 2016a; CDB, 2016c). Men dominate agricultural activities such as large-scale commercial farming, livestock production, and the provision of skilled labor and formal jobs in the sector.

Among registered farms in the region, more than 77% are owned by men compared to approximately 20% owned by women (CDB, 2016c). Country-level data reflect this trend. For example, in Jamaica (World Bank, 2022b), in Barbados, almost 85% of registered farms are owned by men. In Burdwood, Guyana, the proportion is even higher at 94%. Women's limited access to land and credit results in their low levels of formal employment in agriculture, forestry and fisheries (AFY). Comparatively, women are heavily involved in informal activities such as subsistence farming, livestock rearing, and small-scale commercial farming on family farms, which is often not reflected in official employment statistics. In fact, women's participation in agriculture is too small to be registered in official statistics in some countries, while gender-disaggregated data are even more limited.

Women's involvement in subsistence farming and sale of fruits and vegetables from their backyard gardens was key to continued food security during economic downturns.


Gender-based occupational disaggregation also occurs within the value chain. Men are predominantly engaged in hunting, forestry, fisheries and livestock farming, while women are more represented in market vending and retail activities, as well as sorting and packing of crops like nutmeg and cocoa. Women's limited access to land, credit, and other productive resources, as well as their lack of alternatives for livelihoods such as small-scale agriculture, has led many women into informal activities.

Women face significant barriers in scaling up their business due to domestic responsibilities and lack of credit. This is further compounded by the fact that women are more likely than men to be employed in disaggregated roles within the value chains, which undoubtedly impacts women's earning capacity and resilience compared to their male counterparts. Climate change has impacted the sector by way of crop loss due to more frequent droughts and floods. Women are also less likely to have access to land titles or credit lines that would allow them to invest in agriculture.

# Electricity including renewable energy and energy infrastructure

Electrification is a key component of sustainable development. Access to electricity can improve quality of life in rural areas by providing lighting for studying or working at night, powering refrigerators and freezers, and enabling the use of electric appliances such as radios, televisions, and computers.

Electricity access also has significant impacts on women's lives. Lack of access to energy is a constraint. Still, some communities within the Caribbean are still have limited access to electricity supply, particularly in the rural areas of Belize, Guyana, Jamaica and Suriname. Women often bear the brunt of this lack of access as they are responsible for cooking, heating water, lighting homes and other domestic chores. This reduces the quality of life in these homes. Women, and where applicable the eldest female child in the household, are often responsible for collecting firewood or charcoal to cook meals, caring for young children or the elderly, or other domestic chores. Energy poverty makes these activities more time-consuming and physically demanding, especially when done outdoors or continuing their schooling. This may also pose multidimensional risk as the use of coal or burning fire wood for cooking has negative health implications on women who most often take up this task. Data from the Caribbean Development Bank (CDB) shows that 50% of households in Jamaica, which puts these women and their families at a greater disadvantage (CDB, 2018), since these women carry the burden of energy poverty.

Energy access can improve women's lives by providing them with more time to engage in other activities. Improved access to electricity will not only increase women’s time availability, but it will improve access to information, online education and training opportunities, as well as provide access to new technologies such as mobile services or food preparation. Improved access to energy will also improve GBV outcomes as women in rural areas are more prone to sexual and/or physical threat in dimly lit areas. Women-owned SMEs who adopt renewable energy solutions can benefit from improved productivity, profitability and technology or the energy transition of their business. Improvements in gender-disaggregated data on energy use is needed to better understand these impacts.

In terms of economic benefits, women form only a small proportion of the energy-related labor force. Although the region has made progress toward gender parity in education, existing stereotypes about subject choices see less women than men enrolled in science, technology, engineering and mathematics (STEM) subjects at all levels of education. Women are also underrepresented in decision-making positions within the sector. There is insufficient representation or mentorship to encourage young girls wanting to get into these fields. In addition, there is a lack of awareness among women about opportunities available through recruitment schemes by universities as well as employers to build awareness of the variety of opportunities in the sector.

The energy transition and the variety of renewable energy sources provided by the climatic characteristics of the Caribbean present opportunities to close existing gaps. Various islands including Trinidad and Tobago are pursuing renewable energy and low carbon initiatives. Globally, it is anticipated that 10% of electricity will be generated from solar power by 2035 (IRENA, 2016), of which 43 million is expected to be in renewables[1]. Climate change and disaster risk. Climate change and gender equality are among the world’s most pressing and complex development challenges. The location and tropical climate of the Caribbean region make the islands extremely vulnerable to climate change. In just the last ten years, the effects of human-induced climate crisis have become more profound. Where most islands previously experienced one or two hurricanes per year, now they experience three or four. Additionally, each year, departures from climate-normals have seen record-breaking warm weather, long periods of drought, and increased rainfall. The number of tropical storms in the Caribbean region, from June 1 to November 30 annually, has also become more violent in recent years. While climate change and gender equality are two distinct issues, they intersect in ways that exacerbate pre-existing vulnerabilities arising from socio-economic structures, gender biases, institutionalized discrimination, and social norms and expectations. As a result, exposure to vulnerability is not equal across the region.

Preparedness and vulnerability. Although limited data exists to assess the extent of a digital divide and ICT use, it is thought that women and men respond differently to early warning systems (EWS) messages given gender differences in their time use and bargaining power within households and societies. The Latin American and Caribbean (LAC) region’s socioeconomic context makes it highly vulnerable to the effects of climate change. Poverty attenuates vulnerability to climate change impacts, and extreme weather events linked to climate change push more people into poverty. These effects are more severe for women and girls in LAC countries because they have less access to resources, education, institutionally, or otherwise marginalized limited adaptive capacity. For instance, gender inequality increases women’s vulnerability to climate change while Indigenous peoples are among the first to face the direct consequences of climate change due to their close relationship with the environment and its resources. In fact, women are over-concentrated in climate-vulnerable sectors such as agriculture, fishing, and forestry, which are emerging high-potential climate-friendly sectors (e.g., low-carbon transport and green construction).

The effects of climate change are more severe for women and vulnerable groups, compared to men. For instance, women are disproportionately affected by the loss of livelihoods due to climate change because women’s livelihoods depend on natural resources, and yet women have unequal access to, and control of, these resources. Women in rural areas, particularly those living in poverty, indigenous peoples, and traditional communities are among the first to face the direct consequences of climate change, due to their dependence upon, and close relationship with, the environment and its resources. Climate change exacerbates existing gender inequalities by increasing women’s vulnerability to violence and economic marginalization; loss of land and resources, human rights violations, discrimination, and unemployment. Women are also less likely than men to have access to information about climate change, their voice and participation in climate solutions is limited. ## Gender-related Risk Assessment

Following IIBR Invest’s Sustainability Policy, IIBR Invest is also committed to the identification of potential gender-based risks and impacts and requires clients to implement effective measures to avoid or mitigate these risks and impacts as part of the environmental and social due diligence and will require the client to address these risks and impacts.

IIBR Invest has an internal tool, the Gender Risk Assessment Tool (GART), that guides social and environmental specialists to: 1) identify gender-related risk factors of the transaction; 2) evaluate prevention measures for each identified risk factor; 3) determine whether there is a gap or gaps identified. The gender-risk assessment also takes into consideration sector-and-country-based factors, including IIBR Invest’s Gender Equality Action Plan (GEAP), which is included with the support of IIBR Invest Contextual Risk Tool; and, at project level, during due diligence site visits, with consultations with beneficiaries, affected communities, and other stakeholders. The result of the environmental and social risk assessment is documented in the Environmental and Social Summary (ESS). The ESS includes a section on Gender that summarizes the gender-risk assessment results and describes the measures to be implemented by IIBR Invest, as well as current gender-related management programs and gender policies of the client.

When gender-related risks and impacts are identified, IIBR Invest requires clients to implement gender-related risk mitigation measures in accordance with the Environmental and Social Action Plan (ESAP) that is included in the legal documentation of the transaction and is a contractual obligation that clients must comply with. The ESAP includes a description of each required action, the deliverables, and the timeline for implementation.

Previously to Board approval, transactions must comply with disclosure requirements. For all projects, the ESS and ESAP must be disclosed at least thirty (30) days prior to the Board approval of a transaction.

Once approved, expected outputs of the transaction are included in the Results Matrix (RM) of the project. The environmental and social specialist are responsible for supervising project’s progress against these results.
