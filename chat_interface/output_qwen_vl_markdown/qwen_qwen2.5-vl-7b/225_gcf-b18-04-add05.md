### A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

The Bhutan for Life (BFL) project will secure 51 percent of the nation's territory as managed under its network of Protected Areas (PAs). The BFL project is a key component in Bhutan’s efforts to meet its climate change commitments, as stated in its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) under the Paris Agreement. It is also central to the country’s plans for meeting its biodiversity conservation goals and maintaining a minimum 60 percent of its land area under forest cover. The BFL project thus has the full backing of the Government of Bhutan, with strong policy support received during extensive consultations.

Despite the nation's network of PAs being relatively intact, many of these areas, and the people living in or adjacent to them are facing increasing pressure from economic development in the form of increased demand for forest products, wildlife resources, and natural disasters. These threats are exacerbated by the adverse impacts of climate change – projected to worsen over time – which will result in more frequent and severe droughts, shorter growing seasons, increased temperatures, and longer dry seasons, resulting especially in increased risks from landslides, floods, and forest fires. The primary constraints on Bhutan’s capacity for addressing these threats are capacity and funding, and the BFL project directly responds to these constraints.

The BFL project will employ a multi-faceted approach built around creation of a seeking fund to support improved management practices in PAs while providing the time needed for communities to adapt their livelihoods. This will ensure long-term revenues sufficient to maintain these management improvements. While transformational for Bhutan, the project is also replicable and scalable with potential for replication in other countries in South Asia and other countries. Mitigation and adaptation benefits will be generated in a cost-effective manner, with GCF resources leveraged to support national priorities.

While there are strong co-benefits from the BFL project with regard to social and economic development and biodiversity conservation, there is a sound rationale for GCF support grounded in the climate mitigation and adaptation benefits. The project has the potential to reduce Bhutan’s greenhouse gas emissions by 10 million tonnes of CO2e over the project’s life, and 145,000 mostly vulnerable rural inhabitants will directly benefit from the improved resilience of their communities and livelihoods. During execution, strong stakeholder engagement will be maintained, with gender considerations mainstreamed.

# Detailed Project/Programme Description

## C.1 Strategic Context

The Kingdom of Bhutan is a small, landlocked, least developed country with a total area of 38,394 km², characterized by rugged mountainous terrain with elevations ranging from 150 meters to more than 7,000 meters above sea level. Predominantly rural and remote, the majority of Bhutan's population lives in small villages engaged in small-scale or subsistence agriculture. Electricity from hydropower is the country’s largest export, contributing significantly to its economy, which also relies on tourism as a major source of revenue.

The Royal Government of Bhutan (RGoB) has always considered environmental conservation a centerpiece of the country's development philosophy and has made it a priority in all aspects of planning. The country's first modern legislation was the Forest Act of 1968, specifically aimed at protecting forests. Similarly, the National Forest Policy of 1974 was the first formal policy for the country, and helped establish a policy framework that continues to guide Bhutan’s forest management practices today.

Due to limited economic development, low population, and extensive forests (forest cover is approximately 70% of total land area), Bhutan has not been significantly impacted by climate change. However, RGoB's strong commitment to sustainable development, Bhutan is unique worldwide in that it is carbon negative, a net sink for greenhouse gases (GHGs). The country’s annual forest absorption capacity is estimated at approximately 63 million tons of CO2 per year, while the country's total annual GHG emissions have hovered around 2.2 million tons of CO2e (2013 figures).

Over the past 20 years, Bhutan has made unparalleled commitments to low carbon development, climate mitigation and adaptation, and biodiversity conservation under the UNFCCC/RCPs. RGoB committed to remaining carbon neutral forever. Bhutan enshrined its commitment to maintain 60% of its territory under forest cover in the country’s constitution. The country also signed up for a number of international commitments and national actions on mitigation laid out in its 2015 Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) under the Paris Agreement, which put forward nine projects and activities that will contribute to Bhutan's climate change adaptation and sustainable use of natural resources management. RGoB is also developing a national REDD+ program, which is included in its NDC.

Given this context, Bhutan has developed a National Climate Change Strategy and Action Plan (NCCSAP), Forest Resource Management System (LR), and/or Forest Ecosystem Monitoring System (FEMS) to support the implementation of these systems. The REDD+ program will contribute to both national forest conservation and climate change mitigation goals.

The Royal Government of Bhutan has thus made clear its commitment to following an environmentally and socially sustainable path to development – despite being a small, mountainous, least developed country with many pressing needs. Bhutan is committed to maintaining the carbon neutrality pledge and calls for international support for its climate change responses. Maintaining forest cover in the country’s constitution, as well as the NCCSAP, is central both to mitigation and adaptation strategies, with Bhutan for Life project serving as the cornerstone for this strategy. The project will also include a number of other elements expected to include: the BFL project for managing landscapes under the PAs network; measures to improve management capacity in protected areas (PAs); improved forest resource management; climate change adaptation steps to control growing transport and industrial emissions, protection of vital water supplies in the face of a changing climate; and enhanced preparedness for and response to climate-induced natural disasters.

### Climate Vulnerability in Bhutan's environment and economy:

As a landlocked mountain nation in the global "third pole" of the larger Himalayan region, Bhutan is highly vulnerable to the impacts of a changing climate. The increase in global temperatures will have significant consequences on the environment and human activities, with resulting impacts that will only worsen as temperatures continue to rise with increasing global emissions.

### Impacts on water resources:

The impacts of climate change are particularly significant and acute. For example, communities throughout Bhutan enjoy high water quality due to the pristine nature of their rivers and streams. However, as precipitation patterns shift and become more erratic, increased sedimentation and resulting siltation of formerly clean sources is an increasing risk to human health.

### Impacts on agriculture:

The agricultural sector in Bhutan contributes significantly to GDP through the contribution of the hydropower sector; there are increasing reports of local acute water shortages due to droughts. The combination of limited irrigation infrastructure, high rainfall variability and very little storage and pumping infrastructure for irrigation, they are highly vulnerable to such non-stationary changes.

### Impacts on ecosystems:

The observed climatic trends in Bhutan will lead to shifts in seasonal streamflow, ecosystems, and distributions of vegetation types across the landmass. The alternation between drought frequency and the increase of habitat fragmentation have been identified as key vulnerabilities for biodiversity conservation and their wildlife.

Another climate-related threat, of particular relevance in Bhutan's rural areas, is the aggravation of human-wildlife conflict. The combination of farmers tending to the margins of large intact ecosystems of high biodiversity has already resulted in significant levels of human-wildlife conflict with wildlife (especially elephants) frequently raiding crops and encroaching on settlements. This trend will likely continue as climate change leads to increased competition for warming temperatures, increased precipitation variability and increased frequency of extreme weather events, all making the human-wildlife interface more volatile.

Taking the government allocation for FY2015 and adding an average contribution of 0.5 million USD per year from the Bhutan Trust Fund, the total budget would be US$ 3.7 million per year or US$ 4.2 million in current USD, a little less than half what would be needed for a proper sustainable management. This leaves an average annual gap of $1.8 million.

A large share of the public funding baseline, about 16 million USD is dedicated for salaries for existing staff, while the remaining budget covers only basic operating costs (i.e., gasoline, daily allowances for patrolling, utilities and rent) in each protected area. In addition to the public funds, Bhutan's protected areas receive support from international donors through various projects and programs. However, this approach is not sufficient to manage the entire protected areas system to deliver the full potential climate mitigation, adaptation and biodiversity conservation benefits.

**Bhutan for Life - An innovative solution:** This is where Bhutan for Life (BFL) comes in, providing one-time, 14-year bridge financing that will fill this resource gap and push Bhutan's PA at a higher level of management and delivery. This financing will be used while the country gradually increases its own financing and management resources, so that by the end of the BFL project period, the PA system is fully funded from domestic sources.

The primary BFL short-term outcome is to enable Bhutan to immediately begin upgrading the management of natural resources in half of its territory, explicitly accounting for the impacts of climate change to enhance resilience for forest and wildlife conservation. The long-term outcome is to develop Bhutan's human and budgetary resources so that the country can take on, unaided, the task of establishing a fully funded and managed protected areas system to meet greenhouse gas mitigation goals and build climate resilience to benefit people and nature.

How it will work: BFL will employ a funding model based on business models used to organize and finance large, complex projects designed to provide assurances to both investors and recipients. The approach begins with the development of a detailed project plan (DPP) for Bhutan's PA system, including an implementation plan for Bhutan's PAs, with targeted goals, activities, and costs (see discussion below and Annexes 5 and 6).

The financial gap between the country's current Protected Areas System (PAS) budget and the cost of this comprehensive project is estimated to have been US$ 1.8 million per year for FY2014-2017. This amount will increase year by year, its financial and human resource allocation to the PAS in order to reach full project funding at the end of the 14-year project period (see Annex 5).

## C3. Project / Programme Description

BFL goals (outcomes), milestones (outputs), and activities were identified, refined and costed by a team of protected areas experts, Bhutanese government representatives, and conservation and finance experts from the Royal Government of Bhutan (RGGB) and WWF through a highly detailed, consultative and iterative process. As a result, BFL is now a comprehensive project with a clear set of programmatic results and one dealing with the management activities as presented in the figure below.

The rationale for BFL's program of activities is rooted on the understanding that the integrity of Bhutan's protected areas, forests and biodiversity is critical to the country's climate change mitigation and adaptation goals. To these ends, Component A (as well as a large number of activities under Component D) focuses on forestry and land use climate change mitigation measures in order to maintain forest quality and extent within 1.1 million hectares through forest ecosystem conservation.

BFL will also focus on the following: detect forest cover changes, combat the climate change increased risk of forest fires, promote rural alternative energy technologies such as biogas and household solar, and improve land use planning and management in order to reduce deforestation risks and hence maintain or increase the current rate of forest growth inside PA's. These activities, over the BFL 14-year life, will increase carbon sequestration in PAs forests by 35.1 million tons of CO2eq.

# RATIONALE FOR GCF INVOLVEMENT

## D.1 Value Added for GCF Involvement

Worldwide, protected areas are public goods and part of the global commons, providing ecosystem services to local, national and international communities. Although entrance and other user fees are collected in many national parks, particularly in developing countries, they do not cover the full costs of managing these areas. The Bhutanese closed area system as a financially self-sufficient endeavour, let alone turn a profit. In all cases protected area systems depend on external funding to meet their operating costs. The GCF is the only multilateral climate fund that can provide such funding and its grant-on-financing is critical to ensure the full funding of the Bhutan for Life project, even considering a range of other financing options.

### Alternative I: More government funding

The Government of Bhutan is already extending to a maximum its own contribution to the project. A full 46% of BFL, total costs (or 39% of the additional funding) will be provided by the national budget. In addition, however, the country has committed to pay 100% of the PAs annual recurring costs "by the end of the project."

### Alternative II: More grants from other sources

Since early 2015, WWF* has helped the RGoB in a worldwide campaign to raise grant funding for BFL from major foundations, environmental agencies and individual donors. As of April 2016, the total amount raised was $34 million (or approximately 23% of the additional funding), which is considered to be the limit of charitable contributions to BFL.

### Alternative III: Loans

Even highly concessional loans were not considered appropriate to finance BFL because they would have a significant negative impact on the country's debt service ratio. Bhutan is not an income generating activity, and because the country is already highly indebted. With a public debt to GDP ratio of 105%, the Bhutanese government is strongly opposed to increasing further its sovereign debt to finance environmental conservation.

For all these reasons, accessing GCF grant financing is critical to the implementation of BFL. Furthermore: (a) Since the project will be implemented over a period of 24 years, and the total amount of the grant would have a significant average rate of 3.5 dollars of co-financing for each dollar of GCF financing ($1.7 dollars per year), it is essential that the BFL project accesses as much GCF financing when considering only the additional financing, (b) As discussed below (section E.2.1.), BFL is poised to become a model for enhancing climate and environmental benefits from other PAs around the world.

# E.1 Impact Potential

## 1.1 Project/programme impact potential

### Mitigation Impacts

BFL is designed to deliver major national and international mitigation impacts through improved management of land and forest areas, dissemination of renewable energy solutions among rural households and capacity building on sustainable land use.

#### 1. Mitigation through the long-term conservation of Bhutan's carbon sinks:

- Through a host of activities to improve the management of Bhutan’s protected areas (e.g., physical demarcation of PA borders, training and equipment for forest protection, reduction in illegal logging and improved fire control), BFL will ensure that the current carbon stock in the PAs, estimated at approximately 206.2 million tons of CO2eq, will be preserved in perpetuity.
- Note that this is not a claim of emission reductions but rather an estimate of the additional carbon sink that would otherwise have been lost (a figure of 35.1 million tons of CO2eq).
- Still, BFL contributes to its long-term conservation of Bhutan's carbon sinks as it makes part of the “conservation, sustainable management of forests and enhancement of forest carbon stocks,” all of which is part and parcel of the PAs.
- The information on sources and methods of measurement is provided below.

#### 2. Mitigation through natural growth of forests in PAs:

- Different from other mature forests, Bhutan’s forests have been steadily growing since the 1990s, and this process has been well documented by local and international agency studies, as well as by independent academic studies.
- That related this natural forest growth to the effective protection provided by the PA system in Bhutan. The PAs are not only effective but also essential for protecting forests in protected areas.
- We expect this trend to continue with BFL.
- Investment in BFL will result in an additional 34.8 million tons of CO2eq being sequestered in PAs as a result of protecting forests from illegal logging, fire, and other forms of degradation, as well as through forest restoration and rehabilitation activities, including combating illegal logging, better controlling forest fires, disseminating climate-smart agriculture practices, and promoting the use of renewable energy sources within or in the vicinity of PA (the information on sources and methods of measurement is provided below).

#### 3. Mitigation through reforestation of degraded forests and pastures:

- Over the 14-year life of BFL, approximately 20,000 ha of deforested and/or degraded forests will be reforested, resulting in the additional sequestration of 58 million tons of CO2eq.
- The information on sources and methods of measurement is provided below.

#### 4. Mitigation through distribution of renewable energy solutions:

- Solar panels, biogas and improved biomass stoves are expected to cover up to 10% of the households in Bhutan.

The question of BFL mitigation additionality in the short and long-term

Detailed estimations (see below) put BFL mitigation gains during its 14-year implementation period at 35.1 million tons of CO2eq (34.8 MtCO2eq from natural forest growth and 0.3 million tons of CO2eq from reforestation activities).

Because natural forest growth in PAs may continue, at least in the short term, without any major interventions, there might be questions about the mitigation additionality of BFL implementation. Leaving aside the moral of the story that forests are a good thing and should be conserved, it is clear that Bhutan’s PA forest cover has been falling to its low now. While the threats to Bhutan’s forests are few and low, they are bound to grow in the near future unless action is taken. The most significant threat is deforestation due to development pressures, especially from large-scale forest fires (mainly in the southern PAs). Development is inevitable and large infrastructure projects including farm roads and hydropower dams will continue to be built. In the absence of BFL, it would not be surprising if Bhutan were to run several scenarios of what could happen to Bhutan’s PA forest in a 14 year and 20-year horizon with or without BFL.

* **In the short term – 10 years or less** - Bhutan’s forest may keep growing, even in the absence of BFL as are similar effort to improve the management of the country’s PAs. So, in a 14-year horizon BFL mitigation additionality could go from zero, to 25% to 86% of the BFL estimate of 34.8 MtCO2eq (depending on different assumptions about forest growth).

* **On the other hand, in the long term – 30 years or more** - there is no doubt that in absence of BFL, or a similar effort to improve the management of the country’s PAs, forest deterioration will be at work, so that by year 2045 Bhutan’s PA forests would have lost about half their current carbon stock. This would result in a fair mitigation estimate of 34.8 MtCO2eq. That is, not only forest growth will stop, but, today's existing forested land, would be shrinking.

With this genus of figures available, the reason for preferring the 34.8 MtCO2eq as BFL 14 year mitigation target is that it can be monitored and reported on BFL lifetime. All other figures, either defined under "counterfactual" scenarios or will only be available for on-the-ground monitoring years after the BFL implementation.

Experience from many countries have shown that protective conservation of resources is much cheaper than restoration therefore BFL provides a cheaper way of conserving the carbon stock of Bhutan and ensuring continuation of the current trend of forest increase and growth.

#### E.4.1. Vulnerability of country and beneficiary groups

Bhutan’s location, climate and topography make it highly prone to a range of hydro-meteorological hazards that will increase with climate change, including glacial lakes outburst floods (GLoFs), flash floods, riverine floods, landslides, soil erosion and droughts. The country is also vulnerable to the impacts of monsoon seasonality, including the monsoon season, and will be more severe with expected climate change. Moreover, most of the country’s infrastructure, including roads, bridges, hydropower plants, and other public facilities are located at low levels. Human settlements are located along main drainage basins, and are therefore at high risk of flooding. Some of the expected impacts on Bhutan due to climate change include:

**Water:** Bhutan is endowed with a number of rivers fed by glacial lakes originating from the medium to the high Himalayas, with long-term average annual flows of 73,000 cubic meters per year. The major rivers provide water for irrigation and hydropower generation. The country’s hydroelectric power plants are designed to provide for all other uses with emphasis on water supply and irrigation. As temperature increases, although the monsoon-dominated annual precipitation cycle is expected to remain largely unchanged over South Asia, future decades are predicted to see a decrease in winter precipitation and an increase in summer precipitation. Therefore, winters are predicted to become wetter and warmer. The wetter monsoon months will coincide with accelerated glacial melting, causing river flows to increase even more, particularly during the rainy season.

## Forest and other terrestrial ecosystems

Forests play an important role in Bhutan’s hydrological development of rivers. Bhutan’s water resources are regulated and sustained through the country’s rich forest cover that protects the higher elevation areas from erosion. The forests also provide a habitat for wildlife and serve as a carbon sink for generation of hydropower and are an integral part of the farming systems. Predicted drier and hotter winters will increase forest fires, and the loss of forest cover may further motivate deforestation.

### Anticipated Adaptation: National Action Programme on Adaptation (NAPA)

The NAPA identifies climate change as a major threat to agriculture, which could lead to increased crop failure and stress on livestock rearing, which will affect the rural poor who depend on crops and livestock. Occurrences of glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs) due to glaciers retreating as temperatures increase would cause significant damage to infrastructure in the downstream areas. This is likely to have a negative effect on the country. Considering that 31% of the agricultural land is located on slopes, the country is also likely face more frequent landslides and soil erosion.

### Health: Malaria

Malaria is an important vector-borne disease in Bhutan, especially in the southern lower elevation regions, and is likely to spread as suitable temperatures reach higher altitudes. Climate-related hazards such as GLOFs and landslides are also likely to increase in frequency and intensity, which will have a negative impact on the existing food insecurity.

Considering that Bhutan’s economy heavily depends on hydropower, agriculture and forestry with a majority of the population engaged in small scale farming, it is clear that the country's economic development and well-being are being highly vulnerable to climate change. Addressing Bhutan’s adaptation risks may require some engineering solutions such as building dams or reservoirs, but there is also an urgent need for more inclusive based approaches to building resilience, as the country has a long conservation track record, a vast experience of managing biodiversity in ecosystems, and a challenging topography that limits the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of building structures.

The country’s protected area network makes it a prime candidate for the BFL approach of climate-informed conservation. This will help to ensure that Bhutan’s terrestrial ecosystems are resilient enough to support flora and wildlife to adapt to increasing climate change impacts. This approach is recognized internationally, including in UNFCCC technical work, which was undertaken in the preceding three years. The country has been proactive and proactive in developing strategies for South Asian terrestrial ecosystems, many of which are part of BFL programming.

### E.6.4 Application of best practices

BFL will apply best practices to address the primary challenges of climate change in Bhutan's protected areas system and corridors. These include increasingly frequent and intense extreme hazards such as droughts, floods, and landslides; more erratic rainfall patterns leading to increased water stress for agriculture and livestock production, which regional evidence indicates is also beginning later and ending earlier; and slower-onset changes like increasing temperatures, melting glaciers, and rising sea levels that are expected to have significant impacts on ecosystems, wildlife migration, and a host of other impacts that will affect ecosystem services on which surrounding communities in and around the protected areas rely.

To address these climate change challenges, the project will rely on best practices in generating and applying climate risk information, particularly through WWF's ADVANCE partnership with climate scientists at Columbia University. The project will also use the Global Information System (GIS), ADVANCE seeks to greatly simplify the complexity and uncertainty in climate science by working directly with climate scientists to provide clear, actionable, and relevant climate projections for decision-making on this information, including best available downscaled projections using NASA's NEX database of modeled 25 km resolution data. The project will use the climate risk information generated through ADVANCE to guide BFL staff and MoAF staff, and is being tailored to the decision-making needs of BFL. It will be specifically used to guide the various components of the project including the development of a climate change adaptation strategy for Bhutan and its potential impact on the PA system. Additional planned assessments will rely on best practices and available technologies to assess the vulnerability of communities in and around protected areas, including impacts on water supplies, sediment retention and flood control from forests; likely to change based on the ADVANCE projections of future climates.

The Government and other partners will rely on the considerable experience and expertise of the larger WWF network for implementing climate change adaptation projects and programs in communities in and around protected areas and programs for decades, including in nearby Nepal and India, resulting in considerable learning from these experiences. These lessons learned can be applied to Bhutan's context through iterative approaches for vulnerability assessment, adaptive capacity, and ecosystem services assessments, community and on-farm best practices, and the development of climate change adaptation strategies. The project will also incorporate improved varieties, early warning systems, spring source protection plans, weather station installation and monitoring; and alternative livelihoods, among others.

## F.2. Technical Evaluation

This project focuses on a combination of interventions to enhance climate resilience in natural systems and the ecosystem services they provide, which will help Bhutan to adapt to rapid and complex impacts of climate change. This will require substantial investment in research and assessment to determine exactly what impacts communities and ecosystems already face, and what thresholds they are likely to face in the future. A number of key questions need to be addressed, including assessing the climate vulnerability of communities and ecosystems, and current and future climate risk.

Historical weather station data is limited in Bhutan, both in terms of geographic scope (number of stations across the country) and years available, with data only recorded as far back as 20 years in most cases. Combined with the extreme variability of precipitation patterns within the country, this makes it extremely difficult to predict a weather and climate in Bhutan is a challenging task. The National Adaptation Program of Action (NAPA) is a useful resource for understanding the current status of climate change adaptation efforts in Bhutan. However, due to future projection futures at 40 km resolution, there is a critical need for an update. Climate risk information from the ADVANCE project will be used as part of this update. The ADVANCE project has generated a high-resolution NASA data and representing a fuller range of potential future options by sampling from downloaded data from 21 models. This data set provides a more robust basis to assess climate change impacts in Bhutan, which is necessary to access exist system thresholds in the face of potential changes through scenario planning processes.

This context analysis will be used as part of the project’s baseline assessment and will provide an overall decrease in rainy days, or how forest composition will change with a 2 degree C temperature rise and increase in precipitation. This information is critical to supporting the various climate smart or climate-resilient management plans called for in numerous activities.

People in each valley region in Bhutan can be considered indigenous people (IP), with almost every village having at least one clan that is IP. ## H.2 Arrangements for Monitoring, Reporting and Evaluation

BFL progress will be closely monitored on the ground by the Royal Government of Bhutan, WWF, and third party organizations. Each protected area manager will submit activity-based technical and financial reports annually to the BFL Transition Fund Board through the project coordination unit. In addition, the BFL team will collect and analyze data to assess the effectiveness of BFL indicators with specific reference to measuring anticipated climate change mitigation and adaptation benefits.

Every five years, protected areas and biological corridor effectiveness will be evaluated using the Management Effectiveness Tracking Tool (METT) methodology. This tool is widely used in Bhutan and around the world to assess management effectiveness. The methodology is a rapid assessment based on a scorecard that uses 10 indicators of effective management. A modified version of METT, known as METT+, has been adapted specifically for the context in Bhutan. In addition to these regular assessments, the Bhutan for Life project will conduct an initial evaluation in Year 2 and a mid-term evaluation in Year 5. The final evaluation scheduled is in Year 10. A midterm evaluation will be conducted in Year 3. A mid-term evaluation in Year 5, and a final evaluation in Year 10 for the GCF funding will be conducted.

Monitoring and results reporting on BFL progress will be facilitated by establishing a strong baseline. The 2016 national forest inventory data will serve as the initial baseline against which BFL will measure its impact on the baseline situation in the country, and was conducted using METT+. Since METT+ will be used by BFL there will be direct and clear comparisons to this report with respect to baseline and progress.
