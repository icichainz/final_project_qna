1. Ethiopia ranks as one of the countries at most “extreme risk” to climate change; 60% of the country is dryland, where rainfall variability is high and extreme weather events are increasing in frequency and severity of drought. Climate change therefore has significant implications, given the reliance of many of the communities in these areas on rainfed agriculture for their livelihoods.
2. Ethiopia’s agricultural sector accounts for about 40% of GDP and employs approximately 85% of the population (c.10.3 million people) with catastrophic food shortages. Ethiopia's exposure to drought and floods is heavily influenced by the El Niño/La Niña phenomenon. In 2015/16, Ethiopia experienced one of the worst droughts in decades.
3. Climate projections show high uncertainty with rainfall projections ranging from -25% to +40% by 2050. It has been estimated that climate change could reduce agricultural productivity between 8 and 27%. The country’s GDP is projected to fall by 2% to 3%. If no adaptation measures are taken currently, climate change-induced impacts are projected to result in a -21.6% reduction of the total economic output (GDP) by 2050.
4. Ethiopia has been identified as one of the countries most vulnerable to adverse effects of climate change and development; it is particularly vulnerable to the adverse impacts of climate change. All assessments – including the Second National Communication on Climate Change, the National Adaptation Programme of Action (NAPA), 2007) have articulated this. Climate change is further increasing this uncertainty in three main ways:
   - Increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events
   - Continued rainfall variability with more frequent extremes
   - Parts of the country could see changes in key seasonal rainfall.
5. While Ethiopia has made progress on climate change adaptation, there are still challenges to overcome: high dependence on rain-fed agriculture; inefficient water resources management at the local level; low economic development and poverty levels.

6. Cognizant of the current and future impacts of climate change, the Government of Ethiopia (GoE) has developed the Climate Resilient Green Economy Strategy (CRGE). The CRGE is a comprehensive strategy to address climate change. It focuses on resilient water systems at the heart of this CRGE strategy; now mainstreamed into Ethiopia’s Second Growth & Transformation Plan (GTP II), 2015-2025, and the National Climate Change Action Strategy (NCCAS). The GoE submitted it to UNFCCC in June 2013. Ethiopia has since ratified the Paris Agreement in April 2016.

7. The project will contribute to the implementation of the CRGE strategy. It will transform the way that water is being utilized and managed in Ethiopia. The main objective of this project is to improve access to safe drinking water, sanitation services, and irrigation for smallholder farmers by increasing agricultural productivity through improved water management systems. This project is expected to benefit 330,000 people (73,333 households) with the current average family size of 4.3; directly (35%) Farmers will be able to increase their income by 20% and indirectly (65%) 189,000 women and children and 999,000 people indirectly. 7,850 ha of degraded land will be rehabilitated, of which 5,000 ha shall be covered by trees. The project is expected to have a positive impact in decision making in and productive activities and an overall increase in agricultural productivity are also expected.

8. The design of this project combines improved water access and resource management in line with the Ethiopian Water Master Plan (EWMP) and the CRGE strategy, which aims at reducing the adverse impacts of climate variability in the country. The project will introduce proven technologies (Solar powered ground water Pumps and Groundwater recharge systems) to improve water access for smallholder farmers and rural communities while utilizing natural resource management based adaptation methods (Physical and Biological Conservation).

# C1. Strategic Context

## Ethiopia is a federal state subdivided into nine regional states, two administrative cities, 68 Zones and 670 rural Woredas (districts) and 15,000 villages. Kedogo is the administrative unit in the northwestern part of a region that covers 1,119,683 sq. km with the population of 56.6 million (2013 est.), of which more than 84 percent live in rural areas. Analysis of climate change vulnerability and adaptation strategies (e.g., Climate Change Vulnerability Assessment, Exposure Maps, Agriculture Sector C4R Strategy) demonstrates that all political regions are vulnerable to one or more of the following:

### Climate change context

The IPCC's AR5 revealed that globally induced climate change has a major effect on annual rainfall compared to any other factor. Ethiopia is highly dependent on rain-fed agriculture and there has been increasing high variability in rainfall, temperatures, and trends that are of huge significance to Ethiopia given its dependence on rain-fed agriculture. The year 2016 was the driest since 1984 with a 35% decrease in total precipitation compared to the long-term average. Fluctuations have included changes in the intensity and frequency of extreme events, with the incidence of droughts and flooding increasing in the last 10 years relative to the decade before, leading to severe impacts on people's livelihoods.

### Climate change is already impacting Ethiopia

Climate current variability is imposing a significant challenge on Ethiopia. The country has experienced an increase in temperature by 0.5°C per decade since 1960 and this trend will continue with projected increases of 1.3°C between 1980 and 2060, with an increase in the number of hot days and nights of 20% and 29%, respectively. The country is also experiencing a decrease in precipitation by about 5% per decade since 1970 and will experience increasing temperatures in the coming decades; mean annual temperature will increase in the range of 0.8 to 3.4°C between 2060 and 2080, with an increase in the number of hot days and nights by about 5% compared to the baseline 1961-1990 level. Precipitation is expected to decrease in the northern regions of Ethiopia, while southern regions will experience increased precipitation.

### Food security

In Ethiopia, food security greatly depends on rain. Agriculture is the foundation of the economy, employing 85% of its population and contributing about 42% of GDP. The majority of farmers are smallholders who rely almost entirely on rain-fed subsistence agriculture for their survival whilst 10% of the population depends on their pastoral livestock's for their livelihoods. Ethiopia is one of the most vulnerable countries to climate change, with a high dependence on rainfall and traditional agricultural practices. In 2016, Ethiopia experienced its worst drought in over two decades and only about 250,000 hectares out of potential 5 million hectares is irrigated. In this context, it should be noted that the agriculture sector contributes 46% of the national GDP and accounts for 90% of the national export earnings.

### Policy context

Ethiopia's overall goal is to build a climate resilient green economy by reducing vulnerability and extreme weather events which are exacerbated by climate change. Ethiopia has already prepared several national level strategies, including the National Adaptation Programme of Action (NAPA), Ethiopia Program of Adaptation to Climate Change (EPACC), Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation (REDD+), Water for All (WASH) and Water for All with Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) projects. This program supports and aligns with the priority areas of the above national flagship strategies and programs. Specifically:

### Climate Resilient Green Economy (CRGE) Strategy (2011)

The strategy aims to address both climate change adaptation and mitigation objectives. The strategy focuses on five sectors: agriculture, water, energy, transport, and urban development (including cities and green cities). By implementing adaptation and mitigation measures in these sectors, the CRGE Strategy seeks to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by 460 million tons of CO2 equivalent per year.

#### National Adaptation Programme of Action (NAPA)

NAPA focuses on agriculture and livestock as priority areas to address climate change. In particular, NAPA aims to increase agricultural productivity through drought-resistant insurance and early warning and flood forecasting systems. The project also seeks sustainable use of wetland and capacity building and institutional strengthening.

#### Nationally Appropriate Mitigation Actions (NAMA)

Ethiopia has also proposed Nationally Appropriate Mitigation Actions (NAMA) and submitted it to the Green Climate Fund. The NAMA focuses on selected sectors including agriculture, land, energy, biodiversity conservation, and water resources management.

#### Ethiopian Programme of Adaptation to Climate Change (EPACC) (2011)

EPACC aims to build a climate resilient economy through adaptation initiatives implemented at sectoral level. The EPACC programme links climate change adaptation with the sustainable use of natural resources. It identifies key climate change adaptation measures, such as improved water management and control of soil erosion, disease resistance in crops and livestock diseases, land degradation, loss of biodiversity, decline in crop yields, increased vulnerability to droughts, inequality, urban waste accumulation, and dependency due to environmental shocks.

### Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation plus (REDD+) Strategy

The REDD+ strategy aims to reduce emissions from deforestation and forest degradation. By attaching monetary values for carbon stocks, the strategy aims to show the value of forests.

### Ethiopia's Second National Communication (SNC)

Ethiopia’s INDC covers both mitigation and adaptation strategies. The priority sectors covered under Ethiopia’s NDC are agriculture, energy, water resources management, and urban development. The SNC seeks to reduce vulnerabilities due to climate change.

### Climate resilient (CR) strategy for water

The CR strategy aims to improve access to water and sanitation services through improved water resource planning and management. It also seeks to improve local water storage facilities or participatory water management systems in the context of rain-fed agriculture, and strengthen data systems on water resources.

### Climate resilient (CR) strategy for agriculture and forest

Activities under the management of natural resources for sustained water availability are included as immediate investment which are related to sustainable agriculture and land management plans.

### One WASH Programme

The programme intends to achieve increased coverage of sanitation facilities for households in Ethiopia and integrates water, health and education. It also includes the promotion of integrated water resources management, productive uses of water, environmental protection, and capacity building, and other related activities.

Specifically, activities under components 2 and 3 are aligned with the One WASH Programme.


The government of Ethiopia designed the Water Sector Policy and Water Sector Development Universal Access Plan (UAP)17 and revised UAP. It has achieved significant progress in the sale of water supply services through the private sector and the establishment of a national regulatory framework for water supply.

This initiative has enabled Ethiopia to achieve significant progress in access to improved water and sanitation facilities, with 80% of households having access to improved water sources as of 2015. The country now has access to improved water sources as per the GTFI standards18 and only 24% use an improved sanitation facility (based on the JMP). However, Ethiopia still faces challenges in improving access to safe drinking water and sanitation facilities.

It is estimated that 2.4 million deaths (4.2% of all deaths) could be prevented through improved hygiene and access to safe drinking water and sanitation facilities. This would also reduce the number of children under five years old who die from diarrheal diseases. These disproportionately affect children under the age of five, killing more young children each year than malaria or AIDS.

Furthermore, Ethiopia has adopted the principles of Integrated Water Resources Management (IWRM) and has made significant progress in implementing the National Water Master Plan (NWMP). The NWMP is a strategic plan that embraces IWRM principles and approaches and is at a stage of implementation. However, capacity constraints remain as well as challenges related to climate change.

WASH programs have a significant impact on health and education, and are of particular concern, as described in the recent report by the World Bank19. Ethiopia has also experienced extreme weather events such as floods, cyclones and droughts, alongside increasing temperatures. Such extremes pose particular challenges to the capacity of WASH programs to protect health, and there is accumulating evidence that climate change will exacerbate these risks.

### Baseline: Improved access to water to build a resilient livelihood

- Only 27% of the communities within the targeted Woredas have access to improved water sources.
- With 43% of the population living in areas with no access to improved water sources and 30% of the communities requiring travel of prolonged hours to get access to a water source (protected or unprotected).
- Rainfall variability and the potential for extreme weather events are increasing, which will further reduce access to safe drinking water sources.
- Selection of women as project beneficiaries is critical. The average literacy rate in Ethiopia is 42%, but this task usually requires three to four hours a day. Thus, gender inequality is high in the target Woredas, as the majority of the population is illiterate and unable to read or write. This is especially true for women, who are involved with 82% of people not being able to read and write.
- About 58% of females, in comparison to 38% of males, have no formal education. In addition, only about 10% of the population has access to secondary or higher education.
- Women are the primary water carriers and users. Women spend many hours each day fetching water. Often the sources they use for drinking water are not protected from contamination by livestock or other pollutants; therefore, they are more exposed to polluted water than the rest of the household, but they also use this source of water for food preparation and cooking.
- Due to poor water resources management and a lack of technical knowledge on how to build resilient infrastructure, only 5.3% of the targeted communities practice irrigation agriculture either in the form of traditional or modern methods.

# G.1 Risk Assessment Summary

## Available* research** shows that effective risk assessment should consider the following:

- **Category 1: Operational Risks**, which are internal risks that are controllable or avoidable and that ought to be eliminated or avoided. This risk category is best managed through active prevention: monitoring operational processes, identifying potential problems, and taking corrective action.
- **Category 2: Strategy Risks**, which are different from preventable risks because they are not inherently under the control of the organization. These risks are often beyond the scope of the project and are being sought. This category needs a risk management system designed to reduce the probability that the worst-case scenario will occur and to improve the ability to manage or mitigate such events when they occur.
- **Category 3: External Risks**, which are those risks that are outside the influence or control of the organization. Sources of these risks include natural and political disasters and larger macroeconomic shifts. Because such events cannot be prevented from occurring, their management must focus on rapid identification and mitigation of their impact.

## By applying this approach, the risk assessment has concluded that the proposed project is medium risk. This is considered to be tolerable given the ambitious nature of the project; low risk would imply an inadequate level of investment.

### Eight specific risk factors have been identified, four each from the Preventable and Strategic Risk categories:

- **Financial Risks**: These are risks related to financial performance and include the likelihood that the project will not achieve its financial objectives. The likelihood of this occurring is assessed as low with "inadequate operational control" being the main mitigating method.
- **Operational Risks**: These are risks associated with the day-to-day operations of the project, such as delays in achieving milestones or scope creep. The likelihood of these occurring is also assessed as low with "inadequate operational control" being the main mitigating method.

### By comparison, the majority of the strategic risk factors have been assessed as having a medium likelihood of occurrence and are therefore considered to be significant risks:

- **External Risks**: These include natural disasters such as earthquakes or hurricanes. The likelihood of these occurring is high with "inadequate operational control" being the main mitigating method.
- **Operational Risks**: These relate to the day-to-day operations of the project, including delays in achieving milestones and scope creep. The likelihood of these occurring is also assessed as medium-high.

## H2. Arrangements for Monitoring, Reporting and Evaluation

### 100. Approval and monitoring (M&I) are key elements of management and reporting tools that support successful delivery of this project. Without careful monitoring, the necessary data are not collected; hence learning is limited and the project may not achieve its objectives.

#### 200. The monitoring and reporting system of the proposed project will be gender sensitive and will follow guidance from the GCF and comply with GCF M&I policy, ensuring that the project maintains a simple and effective approach to both routine reporting and regular reporting at all levels. It is expected that it will be based on the following core activities:

##### 201. Quarterly Progress Report: The Executing entities will submit aggregated quarterly progress reports to the CRGE Facility, which will coordinate the overall implementation and delivery of the project. The CRGE Facility will also provide technical support for the preparation of these reports.

##### 202. Quarterly reporting will capture activity and output-level information. The narrative section of the quarterly report should be limited to one page per quarter.

##### 203. Annual Institutional Learning Events: The EE’s will undertake an annual learning event to reflect on the changes being observed and to take stock of progress made. These learning events will help sharing of experiences, lessons learned, and best practices among project partners.

##### 204. Annual Performance Assessment: The Executing entities will submit an annual Performance Assessment Report (PAR) on the project outputs. The PARs inform two monitoring activities at the project coordination level: a mid-term review and final evaluation.

##### 205. Joint Monitoring Missions: Joint monitoring missions will provide an opportunity to engage stakeholders of the project, including representatives from the CRGE Facility, in discussions about progress organized by the CRGE Facility or line ministries, to be undertaken annually, and involve the Executing entities and other relevant project partners.

##### 206. Mid-term Review and Terminal Evaluation: Half-way through the project lifetime and during the final three months of implementation respectively, the CRGE Facility will organize a mid-term review with consultant/evaluator. Both reports will summarize the results achieved (Objectives, Outcomes, Outputs).
