## A.2. Program Executive Summary (max 300 words)

Nigeria is the largest oil producer in the world and with a population of over 180 million people, the availability of clean energy is critical to meet the needs of its citizens. The country has significant potential for renewable energy production across both urban and rural communities. According to the 2015 World Climate Change Vulnerability Index, Nigeria is one of the most vulnerable countries in the world and with anticipated effects of global warming, rising temperatures will increase energy demand.

It is estimated that 68 million people, or 35% of the population, lack access to grid-connected electricity (YE 2015). Even within the urban areas, there are significant challenges for energy access. The country faces a number of challenges as a result of gas transmission and distribution constraints. Despite the infrastructure challenges, as the country develops its renewable energy sector, it is important that the development of this sector does not compromise the achievement of Nigeria's Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC). NDC targets a 2030 state whereby electricity generation from renewables will be at least 15% and the share of electricity generated from gas will be at source at least 50% from cogen plants. Further to this, the country has signed the African Bulk Electricity Trade Treaty (ABETT) with the aim of establishing a regional bulk power market in Africa by 2030. Nigeria through its IPP program aims to develop 14,768 MW of renewable energy capacity and supply c.15% of the power to the grid. To date, none of these projects have been able to reach financial close.

As such, the African Development Bank (AfDB), as a AfDB Group infrastructure institution with USD30 billion in committed resources and USD4.4 billion in outstanding commitments for renewable energy in Africa, is proposing to work with Nigeria to develop a 250 MW Solar IPP Support Program which will provide financial support for the development of a 250-MWp renewable power (PV) project to ensure that the financing, construction, and operations of the first utility scale power project in the country. Amidst the current framework of the Nigerian energy sector, this program is expected to be a catalyst for further investment into other projects through innovative structures, will support the further development of long term funding solutions for the sector.

The main activity of the Program is to provide long term financing to Nigeria Solar IPP projects, senior debt financing at the project level. The Program will have two distinct financial instruments: (i) concessional debt financing which will be comprised of a DFIs tranche (with competitive terms), and a GCF tranche (with concessional terms); and (ii) non-concessional debt financing for projects that meet the selection criteria.

The Program is expected to avoid or avoid 40,729.371 t CO₂e on an annual basis (58,731 t CO₂e over the life of the Project). The Program will also provide a risk sharing mechanism for investors in the sector and will reduce perceived risks of investing in the Nigerian renewable energy sector and catalyse private sector investment in the sector (through a capital franchise) on a best effort basis.

### B.1 Description of Financial Elements of the Program

#### Financial Structure

The Program is structured to use up to US$100 million, with up to US$100 million to be equity provided by AFC, ADB and GCF. The intent is for the competitive debt to be provided alongside local financial institutions in order to support the project. In view of the current market conditions, it has been determined that the Nigerian power sector, local financial institutions have limited appetite and capacity (tenor and pricing constraints) to provide debt financing on a stand-alone basis. Hence, up to US$100 million Program may only support an aggregate of projects with total costs of up to US$430 million.

Nevertheless, following an extensive market sounding exercise with various local and regional financial institutions, there is interest in providing debt financing for the project. The Program will therefore be structured as follows: to ensure local financial institution participation for up to US$45 million (naira equivalent). To further support local financial institutions, the Program will provide a 30% equity component from AFC, ADB and GCF. In addition, the commercial tranche debt will be replaced by funding from the DFIs to enable the projects benefit from much needed long-term financing at lower interest rates. As such, in order to ensure that the Program is not constrained by its structure-able limitations, the Program will only proceed with senior debt financing provided by AFC, ADB and GCF.

## Power Sector Financing by Local Commercial Banks

The energy sector has had significant constraints obtaining financing from local commercial banks because local banks have been reluctant to participate in the sector subsequently due to poor cash flow position of the sector and the difficulty in securing foreign exchange lending once the Naira depreciated significantly against the US dollar.

To support the sector, international agencies have initiated schemes to provide security to banks in sector lending. In 2014, the Development Credit Authority (DCA) of the U.S. Agency for International Development (USAID), together with the World Bank, introduced a scheme that provided security to commercial banks in foreign exchange lending on the DSGC and GENCOs. The aim of the scheme was to provide a guarantee to one commercial bank per project to cover up to 50% of the total loan amount for projects under the DSGCs and GENCOs. However, there were never any disbursements under this fund, partly due to an adverse macroeconomic environment in Nigeria that led to the need to concentrate foreign exchange lending once the Naira depreciated significantly against the US dollar.

In 2016, a commercial bank and the French AFG signed a USD$100 million power sector credit facility to support capital expenditure on the DSGC. The facility was provided at a single-digit interest rate for a maturity of between 7 and 12 years, with a moratorium of 2 to 3.5 years, depending upon the project. The facility was secured by a pledge over the assets of the DSGCs. However, once the commercial bank was reluctant to lend on pre-projects identified as cash flow positive due to the lack of preferential foreign exchange lending, the facility was not disbursed.

There is an estimated total exposure of N100 billion based on projected disbursement date of USD$25 billion (N20 billion for DSGCs and N8 billion for GENCOs), with an additional N300 million provided in 2014 to support facility upgrades.

### Case Study: Azura Edp 450 MW IPP

Azura's history of utility scale renewable energy IPP projects is limited, and this one was the largest IPP project that was advanced in Nigeria. The technology used for financing is the BOMW from Azura Edp IPP project and this was dominated by DFIs financing. Prior to this, there was a 20MW AES Eko IPP, which was completed in 2001, but it is not currently operational.

The Azura Edp IPP, the first of a new wave of project-financed greenfield IPPs currently being developed in the country, achieved financial close in December 2015. It is also the first power generation project in Nigeria to receive World Bank financing under the Green Climate Fund (GCF) and the first private sector-led renewable energy IPP project recently came on stream in 2018.

It is believed that the project played a crucial role in signaling to private investors despite challenges facing the country, and it is expected to be successfully developed in Nigeria. It also expected to boost power generation, (providing electricity to an estimated 14 million people) stimulus infrastructural development and economic growth in the region.

The Project will be developed in three phases, starting with a 450MW open cycle gas turbine power station and a short underground gas pipeline connecting the power plant to the Escravos-Lagos Pipeline System, and a substation for connection of the power station to the national grid.

As analyzed by Olanrewaju Ajayi, there was no direct precedent in the market for Azura. The financing structure was rather complex with senior debt provided by DFIs, and local and international commercial banks, inclusive of a Nigerian bank. The total cost of the project is estimated at cUS$857 million, which includes a grant of USD130million provided to First City Monument Bank Limited (FCMB), for on-lending to the Project. In addition to this, there was also a loan from the World Bank under the GCF.

Total debt amounted to cUS$687 million and total equity amounted to cUS$190 million (including in-kind contributions from the Edo State Government of Nigeria). One lender also provided a letter of credit to backsting the payment of the project costs. The financing structure is complex, with a split between two structures where the EDC is procured by the equity side under a collateral arrangement).

Although the successful closing of the Azura Edp transaction has demonstrated that under the right circumstances this investment can be structured and executed in Nigeria, other utility scale IPPs have not reached financial close since then.

## Climate Change Overview

The Project is aligned with the Nigeria Intended Nationally Determined Contributions (INDC) to address climate change under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). As stated in the INDC, the government of Nigeria has committed itself to reducing its greenhouse gas emissions by 2030. The INDC also outlines a range of programs specifically including solar PV. The NDC indicates that the impacts of climate change to the country vary across different regions and sectors. Climate change is expected to have significant adverse effects on agriculture, especially those related to eliminating poverty and hunger and promoting environmental sustainability. Climate change will also affect water resources in Nigeria, with a decrease in rainfall over most parts of the country while a decrease in precipitation in northern savannah, putting a considerable proportion of the Nigerian population at risk.

Variability in rainfall will not only impact the agriculture and food security sector, as the decline in yield in rain-fed agriculture is expected to be as much as 50%, but will also result in droughts and decrease in surface water resources. This will have an adverse effect on the availability of drinking water for both human consumption and irrigation and seawater bodies. This is turn affecting hydroelectric power generation in Nigeria, which are far from the coastal areas and cover a large part of the country and frequently suffer from low flow into the dams thereby affecting the electricity supply.

Nigeria has been actively engaged in international climate policy negotiations since it became a Party to the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) in 1994. Nigeria submitted its First National Communication (NC) in 2003 and a Second National Communication in February 2014, Nigeria is held to account for its greenhouse gas emissions under the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement. In September 2012, the Federal Executive Council approved the Nigeria Climate Change Policy Response and Strategic Plan.

42.5% of the Nigerian population is currently below the age of 14. The country has a high population growth rate due to improved access to healthcare services and declining infant mortality rates. Between the years 1990 through 2010, the GHG emissions increased from 164 million tonnes (MT) CO2e to 263 MT CO2e. This increase is associated with predicted economic growth is expected to drive the country to reach 587 MT CO2e by 2030 (NDIC, 2015). Historical emissions in Nigeria during the period 1990 – 2010 have been estimated as 2.64 Mt.

### Energy Sector Overview

## Tariff Frameworks
As part of the reforms, a revised Multi-Year Tariff Order (MYTO) was introduced in 2012 to implement cost reflective tariffs which became effective in 2013. The implementation was delayed due to elections, a lengthy process for tariff approval by the National Assembly, and institutional framework.

## Institutional Framework
With the enactment of EPSRA, the defined National Electric Power Authority (NEPA), the sole power regulator in Nigeria, was established on April 15, 2013. NEPA is responsible for unbundling into 6 Generation Companies ("GenCos"), 11 Distribution Companies ("DisCos") and the Transmission Company ("TransCo"). The unbundling process was completed by June 2014.

## Market Development
The market development process has been slow, with GenCos and DisCos being able to directly enter into commercial contracts. MSEs are still in the process of developing their business models and will be required to play a role until the sector achieves fiscal strength.

## Economic Impact
Although the power sector reform was well received, it has not yet generated the expected outcomes. The electricity market is now more competitive but it does not entirely threaten the entire power value chain but also the collaterals of key sectors in Nigeria such as the banking sector which is the main debt provider to GenCos and GenCos.

## Transmission Network
The Nigerian electricity transmission network was restructured into a TON. As part of the unbundling process, the TON has been established with a clear mandate and role for the transmission company responsible solely for dispatching electricity from the GenCos and distributing power from DisCos to the ultimate consumers.

### RATIONALE FOR GCF INVOLVEMENT

#### D1. Value Added for GCF Involvement

The result of the recent economic downturn, especially as it relates to the significant downward trend in commodity prices, particularly the oil prices slump has taken a toll on the Nigerian economy and specifically its ability to attract foreign investment, including the provision of a Partial Risk Guarantees (PRG) from the ADB. Local commercial banks are unable to provide:

1. The types of terms (15–18 years) required to finance these projects in a sustainable way, as they are typically limited by their own internal credit risk limits.
2. The preferred currency of funding (usually USD) because of recent prolonged and severe FX convertibility and availability issues.
3. The typical quantum of funding ($20 million and upwards), because of the already existing significant requirements for project preparation and development costs, which are not covered under the PRG.
4. Competitive financing structures given their lack of appetite and interest in a niche and sub sector.

Without the proposed GCF investment size and structure, the Nigerian Solar IPP Projects will be unable to achieve the policy objectives set out by the Federal Government of Nigeria (FGN) for the projects. The FGN has committed to US$150 million in funding for the projects at US$ 11.5 cents/kWh. However, since none of the 14 projects could reach financial close within the already extended timeframe of 2016, the FGN has been forced to extend the deadline by another year and is currently reviewing tariff levels down to US$7.5 cents/kWh which is more sustainable for FON. The projects are expected to be bankable at a tariff level of US$ 12 cents/W. ## Assessment of the independent Technical Advisory Panel

### Impact potential

#### Scale: Medium

Nigeria's renewable energy resources are significant, and in particular, the country has great potential for solar energy industry development. Nigeria lies just north of the equator, in a region where the sun is always shining. The northern part of the country receives an average of 2,000 kilowatt hours per metre squared (kWh/m²) of irradiation. Studies say that this region has the highest solar potential in Africa and is one of the most arid regions of the country, which is the least developed and poorest region of Nigeria. Horizontal irradiation in the northern part of the country, where the project solar power plants are planned to be constructed, can reach 2,500 kWh/m² per year. This is equivalent to about six hours of sunshine a day because this part of the country is often cloudy and has a longer rainy season. This region receives more than double the amount of solar energy as the rest of Nigeria.

In Nigeria's nationally determined contribution (NDC), submitted by the Federal Government of Nigeria (FGN) in 2015 to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), Nigeria committed itself to reducing its greenhouse gas emissions from the baseline emissions by 45 per cent (equivalent to 1.4 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent [CO₂eq] per capita by 2030). The NDC also includes a commitment to increase the share of renewables in electricity generation to at least 6 per cent by 2030, with an unconditional commitment. Reduction of transmission losses in power and gas industries and increased energy efficiency are among the key measures included as part of Nigeria's development plan for low-emissions development. The NDC also includes a commitment to increase the share of renewables in electricity generation to at least 6 per cent by 2030, with an unconditional commitment.

FGN with the support of the World Bank, developed and started implementation of the Power Sector Recovery Programme (PSRP) oriented on policy, regulatory, operational, governance, and institutional reforms. The PSRP was launched in April 2015 under the NDCs. This programme aims to restore the financial viability of Nigeria's power sector, improve transmission infrastructure, increase access to electricity for rural areas, reduce energy theft, and reset the Nigerian Electricity Supply Industry (NERSI) for future growth. This programme is expected to increase generation capacity and support the development of on-grid solar potential existing in country.

As the first step in implementing the NDC commitments, FG approved its feed-in tariff (FIT) regulations in 2015. The FITs are designed to provide a stable framework where 2 gigawatts (GW) will be generated through renewables and the electricity distribution company will have to source at least 50 per cent from renewables. In February 2016, the FIT policy came into effect but later it was changed (see next page).

### Nigeria Solar Independent Power Producer (IPP) Project

In addition to the framework of the Nigeria solar independent power producer (IPP) project, FGN (through the Nigerian Bulk Electricity Trading (NBET), which is the power off-taker) signed 14 utility-scale solar power purchase agreements (PPAs) in 2016 to supply 1.32 million MWh per year of electricity. However, these projects are still struggling to reach financial close due to financial constraints and high risk. In particular, the reduction in tariff from USD 7.5 cents/kWh to USD 7 cents/kWh has significantly reduced the capacity of development financial institution lenders in the funding market to provide the capital needed for developers to develop the project due to significantly reduced interest rate returns. Hence, there is currently no utility-scale on-grid power in Nigeria.

In response to recommendations from the Ministry of Finance of Nigeria, FGN is proposing a reduced tariff of USD 7.5 cents/kWh to allow the projects to reach financial close. Meanwhile, in order to ensure that all IPPs programmed throughout the term of the PPA, still meet projects under the program's criteria and are not excluded via the revised tariff structure which enables a lower tariff while still covering the costs of debt.

In addition to the above, in response to the local private sector and utility-scale solar power producers, the Africa Finance Corporation (AFC) jointly with the African Development Bank (AfDB) has developed this funding proposal aimed at reducing the financial constraints faced by IPPs. The program aims to reduce existing financial barriers in parallel to other ongoing programs implemented by FGN to reduce costs of debt for IPPs and provide a more competitive financing structure that will enable them to implement the first utility-scale, on-grid solar power project, which could have a significant demonstration effect.

The funding proposal falls in the GCF results area for energy access and power generation, and its main activity is to provide long-term financing to the solar IPP projects. The funding proposal aims at providing USD 10 million (USD 94 million as a senior loan for concessional lending and a USD 6 million equity tranche) from the GCF. In addition, the World Bank, AfDB, AFC and AfDB also contribute up to USD 130 million each (USD 94 million for a commercial loan below the GCF limit of USD 250 million and USD 36 million as an equity tranche or potentially a commercial tranche). USD 140 million is ten per cent equity from sponsors and USD 45 million potential contribution from local banks. The senior debt financing will comprise a development financial institution (DFI) loan of up to USD 70 million, which will be provided by AFC (commercially) and potentially a commercial tranche (from local financial institutions). Support will be provided for equity contributions of up to 30 per cent of total project costs with the balance through equity up to 30 per cent.

In most Sub-Saharan African countries, lack of access to electricity and other forms of energy adversely affects businesses, thereby reducing standards of living and leading to a depression of the overall economy. Limited access to energy for the majority of the population, low income levels, poor infrastructure, and healthcare limits human development in many spheres, including but not limited to education, nutrition, food security, water and sanitation, market-based work, health and gender equality. Even with an installed capacity of about 7,640 MW in December 2017, available daily generation capacity has been erratic, ranging from 3,000 MW to 5,000 MW due to gas availability, transmission and distribution constraints. # REGULATORY POLICY FRAMEWORK RELATED TO GENDER

## The Nigerian Context

### 2.1.1 The Gender Policy Framework in Nigeria

The 1999 Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria prohibits discrimination on the basis of places of origin, sex, religion, status, ethnic or linguistic group. Successful governments have always prioritized gender equality and upholding this to promote gender equality and women's empowerment in varying degrees. To facilitate gender equality and women's empowerment, the Federal Government of Nigeria created a framework of legal and policy framework and put in place institutional mechanisms in this regard.

Moreover, Nigeria as a member of the United Nations signed and ratified various relevant international instruments, treaties and conventions without reservation. These instruments have always emphasized that member nations put in place the necessary legislative measures to eliminate gender discriminations and ensure equality and human dignity to all men and women.

The Federal Government of Nigeria in 2000 adopted a National Policy on Women. This Policy was reviewed in 2006 and upgraded to become the National Gender Policy. Other key government policies with gender equality and empowerment of women as their focus include the National Economic Development and Social Development Strategies (NEDS) in May 2004; and the Transformation Agenda of the immediate past administration who, in developing the Vision 2020, had a Special Interest Group on Women to oversee the development of policy statements and strategies that would ensure gender equality and empowerment built on equitable contribution of the Nigerian women, men and children.

### 2.1.2 Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women

The Federal Republic of Nigeria consists of 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory (FCT) with diverse cultural and religious practices even within each state. In many cases, including issues relating to women and children, federal laws are not applicable at state levels. Attempts to domesticate Article 18 of the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (the Convention) by Federal Government have generated public and parliamentary debates, with some legislators, religious groups and leaders arguing that the provisions will erode local customs and religious beliefs.

## 2.2 Sharia Law in Nigeria

### 2.2.1 History of Sharia Law in Nigeria

The values, beliefs, ideas, arguments and discussions of Sharia Law have been a significant part of the religious and cultural context of Nigeria for centuries and date back to the revivals brought about by Usman Dan Fodio in the early 19th century. The purpose behind the revival was to improve the standard of living of Muslim women. After the death of Usman Dan Fodio, the practicalization of precepts based on this religious system of rule where obscured for several decades due to a series of military interventions. However, there was a resurgence of the rule based on Sharia Law precepts with the declaration by Governor Ahmed Sani Yerima of Zamfara State in 1999.

## 2.2.3 Challenges with Implementing Sharia Law in Nigeria

Although the historical foundations of Sharia Law may not have included an overt gender bias, there are currently widely acknowledged biases in the implementation of penal codes under Sharia Law. As an example, there is a social custom where widowers according to Sharia law can fall into economic poverty when the husband passes away because they traditionally have no right to inherit. 

## 2.2.3 Sharia and Gender Issues in Northern Nigeria

## 2.4.3.3 Vulnerable group inclusion

The AfDB ISS defines vulnerable individuals or groups as those within a project's area of influence who are particularly marginalized or disadvantaged and who might thus be more likely than others to experience adverse impacts from a project. Vulnerability is considered here by identifying the conditions on an individual or a group that faces negative conditions as the result of the implementation of a project.

Vulnerable status may stem from a person’s gender, economic status, ethnicity, religion, cultural behavior, sexual orientation, language or physical and psychological health conditions. Vulnerable groups may include, among others, female-headed households, those below the poverty line, the landless, those without legal title to assets, ethnic, religious and linguistic minorities, Indigenous Peoples, and persons with disabilities.

Vulnerable groups are more likely to be exposed to adverse impacts in large-scale projects with a large area of influence, potential cumulative impacts, and multiple affected communities, than in small-scale projects that have site-specific issues.
