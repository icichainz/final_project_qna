A.21. Executive summary (max 750 words, approximately 1.3 pages)
The agriculture sector in northern Ghana — which supports ~80% of the total workforce — is dominated by smallholder farmers practicing mostly rainfed, subsistence farming, using mostly traditional agricultural practices and minimum technology.

The long duration of the dry season experienced in northern Ghana results in prolonged periods of food shortages. High temperatures in northern Ghana already contribute to reduced water retention capacity and frequent drying up of wells. The lack of rainfall coupled with high temperatures has led to increased evaporation rates, which further reduce soil moisture content. This combination of factors can lead to climate change exacerbating the problem.

The long duration of the dry season also results in a proliferation of weeds, pests and fungi that cause significant damage to crops. These organisms are able to thrive due to the lack of rainfall and high temperatures. The prolonged dry season also leads to increased soil erosion and damage to assets and public infrastructure, as well as reducing storage capacity of reservoirs in northern Ghana, leading them to dry quicker in the dry season as well as increase the risk of dam collapse, leading to downstream flooding.

The proposed project objective is to enhance the climate resilience of vulnerable smallholder farming communities in northern Ghana by improving food security and enhancing the agro-based rural economy. The project strategy is threefold comprising the improved climate data and early warnings made available to facilitate proactive drought and flood

### B.1 Project context (max. 1000 words, approximately 2 pages)

#### Context*
Approximately 80% of the 4.16 million people living in northern Ghana are smallholder farmers who have traditionally relied on traditional, small-scale rainfed agricultural systems to provide food security and income.

Smallholder farming households in northern Ghana face increasing vulnerability due to climate change. The Upper East Region (UER) and North East Region (NER) of northern Ghana experience rainfall between three to six months annually during the dry season. This is coupled with a decline in the quality of natural ecosystems and changes in climate. The main drivers of this are deforestation for wood and charcoal production and urbanization, as well as overgrazing and conversion of natural vegetation for agriculture; or overgrazing by cattle herders, leading to soil erosion and loss of unsuitable farming practices.

#### Climate analysis*
Current trends show that rainfall in northern Ghana occurs between June and October; this is also the period with the highest temperatures (Figure 1). Historical data shows a substantial change in rainfall patterns over time. The early wet season (−8 mm April, −8 mm May, −17 mm June) has shortened by about two months since 2005. The late wet season (−9 mm July, −12 mm August in the 2010 decade) and an increase during the late wet season (−5 mm September to October). This is coupled with a decrease in total annual rainfall from 874 mm in the early 1960s to 630 mm in recent years. The start of the wet season has become erratic with the start of the wet season becoming more difficult to predict. In the past, the wet season started in April and ended in October; however, since 2005, it is now starting later (May) and ending earlier (September). This is coupled with extremely heavy rainfall in September or October, indicative of a shortening of the historical rainfall season. The overall trend shows that there has been an increase in the number of days with more than 10 mm precipitation month April to September from the 2000s, with a decreasing trend of 2.6 days per decade. The overall trend also shows that there is now less rain during the dry season and more rain during the wet season with increased occurrence of extreme rainfall events with severe consequences for smallholder farmers.

Rainfall is expected to become more frequent over time with events that historically occurred once every five years becoming more common and occurring on average 1.5 times per year by the end of the century. ### Climate change impacts

The Guinea savanna and Sudan savanna ecoregional zones of northern Ghana, on which the livelihoods of millions of people depend are under considerable stress because of inter alia: i) unfavorable climate conditions and trends; ii) extreme land and environmental degradation related to increasing population growth rates; and iii) unsustainable agricultural practices. ### Impact on food systems

Climate change is impacting dry season food production. Few smallholder farmers have access to irrigation infrastructure and as a result are highly dependent on rain for their livelihoods. As described above, low rainfall in the dry season can lead to reduced yields of staple crops such as maize, sorghum, millet, and cassava.

In-season gardening can supplement food production outside of the major growing season, but can only be practiced in areas with sufficient water availability. The frequency and intensity of extreme weather events associated with climate change is exacerbating water shortages during the dry season. High temperatures in northern Ghana already contribute to low soil moisture levels that are exacerbated by high evapotranspiration rates from crops, trees, and streams — that serve as sources of water for both livestock and household use, including for dry season gardening.

Food security during the dry season is further impacted by the high rate of post-harvest losses (PHLs). Post-harvest losses in staple foods in Ghana such as maize, cassava, yam and rice amount to over 35%, 24%, 34%, and 7% of yield respectively. The main causes of PHL are: i) insect damage; ii) fungal diseases; iii) physical damage due to ranges of the weeds, pests and fungi that cause food spoilage — as well as an increase in their multiplication rates; iv) low storage capacity; v) poor post-harvest handling practices; vi) inadequate drying; vii) reduced seed viability; viii) discoloration; ix) caking; x) mouldy smells; and xi) the production of mycotoxins in stored food.

The shallow groundwater system in Ghana is a dynamic system that mostly recharges through direct rainfall infiltration, especially in the northern regions. The shallow aquifer ground water resources are already classified moderately vulnerable to climate change impacts. Increased temperatures, drought occurrence and potential evapotranspiration will reduce the resource recharge.

Inversely, extreme rainfall events often result in severe flooding across many areas of northern Ghana, destroying crops and livestock. Fluvial flooding results in river overflows, and there expose crops and livestock to flood damage and loss, especially for those living along riversides. Floods also destroy infrastructure such as bridges, roads, canals, irrigation systems and roads. Flooded places also called flash floods, occur after heavy rainfall, often only on short time periods. The northern region of Ghana, which supports >90% of the total workforce – is dominated by smallholder farmers practicing mostly subsistence farming. Given that smallholder farms in northern Ghana are almost entirely rainfed and have limited access to irrigation infrastructure, they are highly vulnerable to climate change impacts, especially dependent on the availability of natural resources, making them highly vulnerable to changes in their environment and weather patterns. The framework provides guidance for addressing these risks and includes a risk matrix to identify potential negative impacts related to invasive species; adopting integrated pest and nutrient management approaches; avoiding large-scale or disruptive land use changes that could impact biodiversity habitats in cropland or pastures; and supporting controlled early burning, rather than outright fire suppression.

### 1. Overview

#### 1.1 Overview
In Northern Ghana, agriculture employs roughly 80% of the region’s workforce and is predominantly rainfed and relies on traditional farming methods, and assessed to have “limited technological inputs.” This dependence on natural climate conditions leaves farmers highly exposed to climate risks such as erratic rainfall patterns, prolonged dry spells, rising temperatures, and higher frequency of extreme rainfall events. These factors are exacerbated by a combination of factors, including erratic climate linked weather patterns, environmental degradation, population pressure, rapid population growth, and disruptions caused by dam spills from the Tanne and Birem reservoirs.

Exacerbating impact of climate risk on the development pathway: Climate change is intensifying these risks, leading to reduced crop yields, increased food insecurity, economic impacts, increased frequency of extreme rainfall events, prolonged dry spells, rising temperatures, and heightened respiratory diseases. These factors have far-reaching effects such as reduced crop yields, loss of arable land, hazards having cascading effects on the livelihoods of smallholder farmers. Farmers already operating at subsistence levels struggle to adapt, leading to worsening food insecurity and poverty.

Thus, effective solutions must integrate climate adaptation, environmental restoration, and livelihood diversification strategies in order to build resilience against these risks.

3.1 Existing gender inequalities in northern Ghana

Ghana has a long history of commitment to gender equality, dating back over 50 years to the establishment of the Affirmative Action Act — shortly after achieving independence in 1957. Gender equality is fundamental to Ghana’s constitution, which states that “all persons shall be equal before the law” and “a person shall not be discriminated against on the grounds of race, colour, ethnic origin, religion, creed, social or economic status.” More recently, Ghana has committed itself to achieving gender equality as a key component of its development agenda, such as part of their commitment to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Despite these efforts to ensure equal rights for women and men in law and policy, there are several gender-specific barriers limiting the capacity of communities to adapt to climate change.

The ability of men and women to adapt to climate change is dependent on their: i) involvement in policy development and decision-making; ii) integration into local committees that are focused on climate change adaptation; iii) access to information about climate change and its impacts; iv) adoption of new practices. Building the capacity of both men and women will lead to increased adaptive capacity at the household and community levels.

Barriers to achieving gender equality and building the adaptive capacity of women in Ghana include: i) patriarchal attitudes within family structures; ii) limited influence in decision making; iii) increased out-migration of men leaving women with an increased labour burden*; iv) greater difficulty for women to access credit, land, and other resources. Women are also more vulnerable to climate change. Approximately 68% of women in rural Ghana are employed in the agricultural sector*. However, fewer than 20% of northern households are headed by women*, and therefore, the role of women in farming decision-making is limited. These challenges are compounded by a lack of policy

### Access to and control over resources

A major factor influencing the economic and social status of a group of people is their access to and control over assets. In rural communities such as those in northern Ghana, land ownership is often concentrated in male hands. Only about 70% of households in Northern Ghana are engaged in small-scale subsistence farming for their livelihoods. ### 3.6 Gender and climate change

The gender inequalities described above disproportionately affect the adaptive capacity of women to current and future climate change, as opposed to men. The effects of climate change on households and communities will be exacerbated if the adaptive capacity of both men and women are not improved. Women in rural areas are particularly vulnerable because they often bear a greater responsibility within a household, having knock-on effects on families, children and the community. Although traditional practices may have helped them to adapt to some extent during past periods of harsh climatic conditions experienced in northern Ghana, it is unlikely to adapt quickly enough to overcome the effects of future climate change[17].

Women — along with children and the elderly — are among the most vulnerable groups to the effects of climate change. This is because women and female-headed households have fewer resources to cope with reductions in food and water security and the effects of extreme weather events, such as droughts and floods. Women also bear a greater responsibility for natural resources, where people often rely on climate-sensitive natural resources for their livelihoods. Several factors contribute to the vulnerability of women to climate change including, but not limited to, the effects of migration or water availability and decreased agricultural productivity[18], all of which relate on climate-sensitive livelihoods. These are described in more detail below:

- Women who migrate from rural areas due to lack of access to land and cultural practices, men often migrate out of rural areas in search of work in other sectors. Women are left behind to not only continue with their agricultural activities but also to care for children and the elderly[19]. When this agricultural work comes in the form of subsistence farming, with little or no pay, leaving women without the financial resources to adapt to climate change.
- Women who do not have access to land due to lack of ownership of the land they are working on, women are unable to make the necessary decisions or access the necessary funds to invest in agricultural inputs such as seeds and fertilizers that can improve yields and food security. This affects not only the women themselves but also their households and potentially the wider community.
- Water scarcity associated with shifting rainfall patterns and drought can have a substantial impact on women, most of whom are responsible for the collection of water for household uses such as cooking, cleaning and drinking[20]. The time required to collect water is often long. Effort is required for women to collect water — with women having to travel further to collect water. Similar problems arise when there is a lack of access to clean water in rural areas where women become scarce as climate conditions worsen. This compounds the additional workload placed on women maintaining both domestic duties and agricultural responsibilities, with little capacity to adapt to climate change.
- Many rural Ghanaian women are reliant on natural resource-based livelihoods that are climate-sensitive[21]. As the availability of natural resources becomes threatened by climate change, so do income generation and food security.

### 3.7 Gender-based violence (GBV)

Despite being considerably underreported, rape and domestic violence are considered to be a widespread problem in Ghana[1]. particularly when married women are thought to be unfaithful. As many as 27% of Ghanaian women experienced some form of domestic violence in 2016[2]. There is also a high prevalence of sexual abuse among children, with 35% of girls and 48% of boys reporting having been sexually abused at least once.

In cases of domestic violence and rape, Ghanaian women are often faced with obstacles when reporting cases to the police. The criminal justice system in Ghana is not equipped to handle GBV. Domestic violence is a private matter that should be settled outside of the criminal justice system[3]. Magistrates may block women’s attempts to pursue cases of domestic violence through the courts, and there have been reports for family members to intervene on behalf of their “husbands”. Women face similar hurdles when reporting cases of sexual abuse, often being unable to complete police forms or having to wait hours in line before being seen by a police officer. They are also often referred for police medical forms. Any instances of GBV or sexual exploitation and harassment (SEAH) during pregnancy can be reported through the Domestic Violence and Victim Support Unit (DVSU) as elaborated in the project’s grievance redress mechanism (GRM). ### 5.1. Gender and agriculture

In the agricultural sector of Ghana, gender dynamics shape the responsibilities and contributions of men and women across numerous communities in northern Ghana. These dynamics are evident in the division of labour, access to resources and the economic roles that men and women play within their households. For example, in the Upper West Region, agricultural work in the Tuggha community is divided along gender lines, with men taking on physical tasks such as ploughing, sowing, weeding and harvesting while women are involved in compost production. Women also support men’s work by collecting water and preparing food during the planting season. However, their participation in agriculture, particularly in cash crops like yams, is limited due to the physical demands of the task. Additionally, women are often responsible for selling agricultural produce at local markets, but they must first seek permission from their husbands or male relatives before doing so.

This is because it is believed that women should not use pesticides due to the belief that they can harm women’s reproductive capacity. ### 7.1 Introduction
The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and the Ministry of Food and Agriculture (MoFA) are leading the preparation of a GCF-funding proposal to develop Climate-resilient landscapes for sustainable livelihoods in Northern Ghana with United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) as its international accredited entity. Agroecological systems in northern Ghana, on which the livelihoods of smallholder farmers rely, are under considerable stress because among other factors: i) climate change; ii) extensive environmental degradation; iii) poor land management and iv) inadequate farming methods. Therefore, this proposed project will be implemented in eight districts in northern Ghana that have been specifically chosen because of their high vulnerability to climate change impacts.

The project is designed to address the needs of women, children, youth and people with disabilities as well as different age groups. These direct beneficiaries represent ~10% of the total population of the eight districts and 1.5% of the people in Ghana.

The Gender Action Plan (GAP) has two primary objectives: i) to guide the project in understanding and addressing the differential impacts of climate change on women and men; and ii) identify gender entry points for actions that ensure equitable benefits for women, men, and other vulnerable groups. The GAP will be developed through a participatory process with key stakeholders involved from the outset and throughout the proposed project’s implementation. Building on the gender assessment (Part One), the GAP ensures targeted actions to address existing gender gaps and inequalities faced by women, people with disabilities and the youth.

### 7.2 Impact Statement
Research and anecdotal evidence indicate that women, children and youth are among the groups most vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. In addition, a large number of vulnerable people in communities that are highly dependent on local natural resources for their livelihoods will be directly affected by the project interventions.

Expected total numbers of direct and indirect beneficiaries (reduced vulnerability or increased resilience) are:
- 691,125 people directly benefit from project interventions (331,743 men and 359,385 women); and
- 2,861,674 people indirectly benefit from the project interventions (1,459,474 women and 1,402,200 men).

The project will also:

### Outcome Statement

The project aims to enhance the climate resilience of at least 134,410 women directly and approximately 2.2 million women indirectly by improving their access to resources for agricultural practices and/or grant facilities.

The GAP outlined in Table 3 serves as a framework for integrating gender considerations into the climate-resilient and sustainable livelihoods project. Women's groups, including widows and divorcees, are actively involved in planning, decision-making, and implementing climate-resilient agricultural practices, early warning systems, and livelihood interventions. The EPA, MoFA, WRC, and other governmental bodies will collaborate closely with the project management team to ensure that gender considerations are integrated into all aspects of the project.

The plan will be monitored through gender-disaggregated indicators such as participation rates and consultation outcomes, with reporting scheduled at least quarterly. Data collected from these monitoring activities will be used to ensure continuous feedback and adaptation of the plan. During the 2024 mission, women in the target district local communities indicated that they have previously been unable to attend workshops or training sessions in the afternoon and early evening as a result of their childcare responsibilities. Therefore, it is proposed that training sessions for women within the local communities will take place during weekdays in the morning hours. The implementation status of the GAP will be a recurring agenda item for all project steering committee (PSC) meetings.
