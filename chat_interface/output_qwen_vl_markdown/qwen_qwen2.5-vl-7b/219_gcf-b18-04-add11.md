### A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary

The Green Climate Fund (GCF) is supporting Colombia in scaling up climate-resilient intertidal water resource management practices in La Mojana, one of the poorest and most climate-vulnerable regions in Colombia.

Extreme weather events are becoming more frequent and intense due to climate change, leading to increased competition with climate projections expecting these to become more frequent and intense. Loss of agricultural crops that sustain livelihoods is common as well as loss of fish stocks from prolonged dry seasons. Flooding and droughts resulting from prolonged dry periods are common and worsening with time. In addition, climate-induced pressures are straining already stressed water sources in the region, affecting both supply and quality.

2. In response, the government of Colombia has formulated the Climate Change Adaptation Action Plan for La Mojana (CCAP), which is a comprehensive plan to address the drivers of vulnerability in the region which were reactive and focused on infrastructure solutions that failed to address comprehensive risk. The La Mojana Action Plan aims to promote a comprehensive approach to climate change adaptation by addressing the underlying causes of vulnerability, including economic conditions of the local population, in order to adapt to projected floods (See Section 3.2.1). The Action Plan is based on an integrated assessment of the region’s physical and socio-economic vulnerabilities using hydrological and hydrological models of the La Mojana region including flooding dynamics as well planning processes that include spatial planning, land use planning, water resource management, infrastructure development, environmental protection, infrastructure, sanitation, socio-economic development, environmental dynamics recovery and strengthening of Government capacity. The Action Plan is designed to address the drivers of vulnerability in La Mojana by addressing large economic drivers and investing in infrastructure such as housing, public works, and wetland canal restoration.

3. In this context, GCF funding will be used to directly complement the implementation of the La Mojana Action Plan to tackle the underlying causes of vulnerability in the region. The project will address climate change-induced loss of natural resources, limited access of early warning services and products, unsustainable management practices affecting household resource, non-adapted local livelihoods to climate variability and limited knowledge on how to adapt to climate change. The project will be implemented through four outputs aimed at enhancing climate resilience of vulnerable communities in the La Mojana by focusing on four output areas: (1) Supporting the development of a comprehensive water management plan for La Mojana, (2) Promoting climate-resilient water resource infrastructure and ecosystem restoration, (3) Improving early warning systems for Climate Change Adaptation, and (4) Strengthening institutional capacity. The project will be implemented on developing technical models and guidelines to enable decision making for long-term water management planning, establishing a comprehensive water management plan for La Mojana, promoting climate-resilient water resource infrastructure and community alternative water solutions, wetland restoration to recover its valuable water management services, development of an early warning system for climate change adaptation in La Mojana, and the promotion of traditional best practices, enhancing early warning systems monitoring and products, investing in climate resilient home gardens and other climate-resilient livelihoods.

4. The proposed project will advance a significant paradigm shift as GCF resources are used to operationalize the first comprehensive climate adaptive regional development plan which will serve as a model for the rest of Colombia. This includes not only addressing the drivers of vulnerability by investing in infrastructure but also focusing on infrastructure but also on restoring ecosystem services for regional water management. Hence it will revolve around restoring and enhancing ecosystem services, including wetlands, to provide resilience against climate change at all levels of water in the wetlands through the seasons by directly empowering vulnerable communities and regional authorities. In addition to this, it will enhance the adoption of new technologies to overcome threats posed by climate change impacts on the availability of water supply. This does not only address the vulnerability in the context of climate change, away from the historical “rationalization” of water management, wherein water was evacuated right from its source and used for urban consumption but also addresses the resilience of communities that have been tested in Colombia while promoting a paradigm shift in the adoption of technology for water supply. The project is designed to promote community involvement to promote their long-term resiliency and foster project ownership with a gender-balanced focus.

### General Profile of La Mojana

The region of La Mojana is a large floodplain located between the northern foothills of the Andes and the plains in the eastern part of Colombia (Figure 2a). It comprises 11 municipalities spread throughout 4 departments (Sucre, Bolívar, Cordoba and Antioquia), with a total population of 405,628 people. La Mojana is characterized as a fluvial-detention overflow plain, which receives the water from the Magdalena River and its tributaries, including the Cauca and San Jorge Rivers – all of which flow into the Atlantic Ocean (Figures 2b and 2c).

### Climate and Hydrology

14. La Mojana's location in a deltaic plain makes it subject to increasing annual floods that vary in scale depending on the intensity of precipitation in the upwind mountainous region. It is divided into flood-prone area (swamps, lagoons, streams, rivers) and non-flood-prone areas (agricultural land). The floodplain experiences flooding for 3–6 months each year; a transitional zone (17%) that is flooded for 3 to 6 months per year; and emergent lands (9%) that are only inundated during the wet season. The average annual rainfall in La Mojana is approximately 2,050 mm.

The annual precipitation varies between 1,000 and 4,500 mm. Climate projections state that most of La Mojana will see a reduction of total precipitation levels by up to 30% during the wet season (June–November) in the coming decades.

### El Niño Southern Oscillation

The El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO) phenomenon has an important influence on the climate in La Mojana. During La Niña years, La Mojana experiences extreme rainfall patterns and higher flood levels. It's a delta plain characterized by its high vulnerability to flooding due to its location at the confluence of several rivers.

20. The economic base of La Mojana is mainly rural, with high dependence on agricultural activities. The reliance on agriculture makes the area extremely vulnerable to climate variability and the impacts from floods and prolonged dry seasons. This vulnerability is further exacerbated by the lack of water infrastructure in the region, which leads to food shortages and droughts. This includes the disappearance of sorghum® and the shortage of fodder for cattle.

21. The main economic activities are livestock production (practiced in 395,000 hectares), followed by mixed livestock and crop farming (in 48,000 hectares). Other economic activities include fishing and hunting (59,800 hectares)®. In terms of land use type activity, 70% of the land is under pastures followed by 24% under rice cultivation.

22. At the same time, agricultural activity affects the natural water flow dynamics in La Mojana, as there is an increased pressure from cattle ranching on the natural wetlands, as well as water use from crop and livestock production that affect the hydrological cycle of the region. In addition to this, the prolonged dry seasons and floods in La Mojana, there are seasonal changes in water levels (called pulses) that create an aquatic-terrestrial transition zone called mangroves. These mangrove areas are very important for the ecosystem as they provide a series of new pulses are key to most of the productive activities in the region (i.e. fishing, livestock, rice, hunting), turning them into areas that are closely related to economic activity and food security in La Mojana.

Ecosystem Services

23. Thirty-seven percent of La Mojana is wetlands. Wetlands provide valuable ecosystem services particularly in relation to water management. In La Mojana, community livelihoods are based on the services provided by the wetlands. These wetlands provide a wide range of ecosystem services including flood regulation and control, water purification and water supply as well as their economic value through agro productive use (water for livestock and agriculture) and fishing. The wetlands also provide other important services such as carbon sequestration, soil formation, and the region's wetland ecosystems including supply services (food, fuel, minerals and materials, plant resources, forest cover and shade), regulating services (pollination, water purification, flood regulation and control), supporting services (decomposition, fertility, and nutrient cycling) and cultural services (recreation, identity, leisure and learning). Supply services are the most important for the region as they provide 50% of total services provided by wetlands.

24. Climate change has increased the community's reliance in these services as water has become more scarce due to prolonged dry periods that have overtaxed the existing water infrastructure. Flooding in the region has and will become more frequent, leading to a loss of wetland areas and an increase in soil erosion. This is particularly true for the region. However, the capacity of the local wetlands, already stressed due to management inefficiencies and pollution, is not sufficient to cope with these changes. The prolonged dry periods have led to the destruction of forested areas and extended dry periods have stopped the regeneration processes of the riparian forest and river mangroves (Lupocas), both which have an important role in water purification (see details on pg. 217 of Annex II).

25. Climate change has had, and will continue to have, significant consequences for La Mojana ecosystems including this loss of wetland areas. The prolonged dry periods that are increasing due to projected temperature increases for La Mojana will have detrimental effects on the structure and type of ecosystem functions. Growth rates of living forms adapted to the early ecological succession phases could be affected by the projected increase in temperatures, which may reduce their capacities to colonize their natural habitats and leading to introduced species or pests/proliferation.

#### Water Scarcity in La Mojana

Limited access to water is a major problem for sources of water for both households and communities.

At present, the communities in La Mojana rely on water supply from the wetland areas and canals. Micro-aqueducts and shallow wells act as sources of water for drinking, household needs, and agricultural purposes. These sources are not sufficient to meet the demand during dry periods when water is scarce. The situation in the region is currently, and projected to experience as a result of climate change. Access to water supply in La Mojana has been affected by the increase in temperature and decrease in precipitation levels, which have led to an increase up to 115 days due to climate change.

The existing water infrastructure is currently insufficient (covering only 50% of the communities' needs) to meet the expanding demand. The use of shallow wells for ground water access via micro-aqueducts has been used in the past to address water supply in densely populated areas in La Mojana, this has not been used as an option in rural areas due to the high cost and low efficiency of these systems.

The vulnerability of rural communities is further exacerbated by the limited capacity of the wetland systems to provide water during dry periods. The lack of a sanitation system and waste management in the region leads to contamination of surface and ground water sources, which can be impacted by floods and prolonged dry periods. Reliance on groundwater through the drilling of wells for water access and the creation of new aqueducts has been identified as a major risk (e.g., the use of shallow wells can lead to the spread of cholera or nuclease). The NAW will invest through this project in the construction of 20 new wells and 15 aqueducts, which will be installed with filters that are needed on the proper protocols that will need to communicate information to communities and the proper protocol for maintenance and repair.

The use of shallow wells is not recommended as a long-term solution due to the high cost involved other than treating it with bleach or using household lines as filters. Little information is provided at the household level in terms of water quality, which can be compromised during extended droughts when water quality can become compromised. ### Activity 3.1.3 Adaptation of existing water infrastructure solutions in the region

40 GCF funding will go to retrofitting 96 existing mini aqueducts in rural areas in 7 of the most water vulnerable municipalities in the region. These systems have been built by communities and are intended to provide water to 48,840 people[1], have been consistently failing due to old pumping and tank systems. This has caused shortages in the daily supply of water required to meet the needs of the communities. Municipalities have stated that they will not be able to continue to operate these systems as they are currently designed because they often leave communities without water for long periods of time, particularly during the long summer dry seasons when the demand is highest. The existing mini aqueducts and other small-scale water supply infrastructure systems are becoming more prone to functional failure and increasingly vulnerable to flooding and contamination. Through this project, it will be possible to replace these old systems with new solar-powered pumping systems that change in size as to provide sufficient water according to current and future needs, to better withstand flooding and operate at a lower cost.

- Sustainable pumping system: All fuel-based technical pumps will be replaced by solar powered pumps that have the required power capacity to extract water during dry periods. Solar panels have been used successfully for many years in rural areas of Colombia and other countries with similar climates. The solar panels have long life spans and few movable parts. Current pumping systems, which form the most common type of water supply infrastructure in the region, are not sustainable because they often fail due to over-pumping, high electrical consumption and electrical malfunction. The solar panels will provide power for up to 10 hours per day depending on the size of the system and the amount of sunlight available to the aquifer to recharge. In addition, the power produced will allow the tanks to fill up to meet water needs.

### Region of La Mojana

The region of La Mojana has been one of the Colombian territories which, because of its geographical location and vulnerability to climate change, has experienced a series of extreme weather events such as floods, cold pulses and drought. The latest great disaster in the region related to flooding was recorded in 2010 and 2011, topologically because of its conjunction with the second rainy season which was influenced by the La Niña phenomenon, resulting in an increase in precipitation.

#### Flooding

Floods in 6 towns in the department of Sucre (Caimito, San Benito Abad, Guaranda, Suchit, San Marcos and Maguía) affected 102,77 people and 24,981 hectares of cultivated land. In the department of Bolívar, 72,390 people were affected in 5 towns (Carmen de Bolívar, La Ceja, El Carmen, San Juan del Cesar and Tumaco), 160,000 people were affected while in the town of Necocli, 13,500 victims were reported. As a result of these floods, the National Health Service recorded 24 deaths due to drowning and 7 cases of acute respiratory health crisis; destroyed 180 educational sites affected, 9,395 homes destroyed and 316,641 hectares affected.

#### Vulnerability

The region of La Mojana is also vulnerable to drought conditions that appear after the floods. Between 2015 and 2017, there were three consecutive years with below-average rainfall, which had negative impacts. The decline of flows affected the supply of water for human consumption and irrigation. During this period, the lowest levels were recorded in the rivers Maguía, Cauca, San Juan del Cesar and Tumaco, which led to water shortages. According to the report by UNIDROIT, 719 municipalities in 28 departments of the country had some kind of drought during that period.

#### Approach and Methodology

270\. The economic analysis of the proposed project was carried out in accordance with the Guidelines for the Economic Analysis of Projects of United Nations Development Program (UNDP). The economic efficiency of the investment is measured by means of net present value (NPV) at a discount rate, internal rate of return (IRR), and the economic internal rate of return (EIRR). For consistency purposes, all proposals developed with the support of UNDP have opted to use a 10% discount rate, in line with the existing practice of multilateral development banks.
