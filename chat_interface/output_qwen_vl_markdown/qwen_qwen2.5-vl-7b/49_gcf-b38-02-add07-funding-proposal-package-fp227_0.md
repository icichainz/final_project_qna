The high occurrence of climate-related disasters, limited climate investments, lack of access to formal safety nets and new technologies, and weak governance are major challenges in Madagascar. The projected impacts of climate change include: (i) the decrease in the amount of annual precipitation, with more marked and/or more frequent periods of drought; (ii) an increase in the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events such as cyclones, heavy rainfall, heatwaves, increased intensity of extreme climatic phenomena (cyclones, droughts, storms and strong winds); and (iv) a very high vulnerability to climate change. The most vulnerable groups are smallholder farmers, pastoralists, fishers, and the poor. Smallholders are frequently exposed to pest and disease outbreaks, which cause significant crop and income losses. The main sources of GHG emissions in Madagascar are deforestation and land use change (LUC), agriculture, livestock, Atsinanana, Vatovavy and Fenoarivo regions. The GHG emissions in Madagascar come predominantly from the land- use change and deforestation activities, which are mainly driven by population growth, urbanization, and forest sector emissions. The emissions are mainly from livestock (enteric fermentation) manure and agriculture (methane from rice cultivation), deforestation, degradation of agricultural practices and fertilizer use.

The project, implemented in collaboration with the Ministry of Agriculture and Livestock, is a service of the Inclusive Agricultural Value Chains Programme (DEFS), has the ambition of reducing the negative impact of climate change by applying sustainable agriculture and agroforestry practices to benefit smallholders. Thus, DEFS is built on previous experiences from IFAD projects, and from an ongoing project funded by the European Union (EU) and DFID, funded by IFAD and the CIF Fund for International Development (CIF) and other national financial partners.

The UNFCCC defines resilience as “the ability of a system, community or society exposed to hazards to resist, absorb, accommodate, recover from, and/or rapidly bounce back from the effects of damage caused by extreme weather events.” Madagascar is one of the most vulnerable countries in terms of climate change. The country has been hit hard by the Covid19 pandemic. The burden of infection in the country is heavy among living in informal settlements which has been declining from 1992 to 2018, it is now increasing due to the recent increase caused by the negative impacts of climate change. The lack of access to basic services such as education and health care, poverty, food insecurity and limited access to land are some of the major challenges faced by people living in informal settlements. The heavy rainfall and floods have led to loss of lives and property and destroyed rural roads. To improve the resilience of smallholders, pastoralists and fishers, DEFS will be implemented through three complementary outcomes:

Outcome 1: Strengthened climate resilience of agricultural production systems through enhancement and maintenance of ecosystem services by (i) improving access to efficient water management systems for sustainable agriculture; (ii) increasing the use of agroforestry practices in smallholder farms and pastoralists’ lands, and (iii) through nature-based flood protection and ecosystem-based approaches to restore 5,000 ha of landscape.

### B.1 Climate context (max. 1000 words, approximately 2 pages)

#### Climate baseline
Madagascar's geographical location, marine influence and wind regime result in great climatic diversity. Madagascar has two main seasons: a hot rainy season (November to April), with maximum rainfall received between September and December; and a dry season (May to October). During the wet period, light rainfall is mostly restricted to the southern coastal areas, while heavy rainfall occurs over the northern highlands. The eastern coast receives less than 800 mm of rainfall per year. The highlands enjoy a tropical climate with cold winters and average rainfall ranging from 900–1200 mm annually. The western part of Madagascar has a semi-arid climate, with erratic and low rainfall of less than 800 mm per year. Temperatures vary significantly across the country, due to topography and proximity to the sea. Coastal areas have temperatures between 27°C along the coast and near 16°C–19°C in the highlands. Temperatures are the highest on the west coast, reaching up to 30°C.

#### Temperature trends
The University of Cape Town estimates that the average temperature in Madagascar increased from 21.5°C to 22.4°C from 1961 to 2005. Another estimate by Razafimanantsoa et al. (2018) indicates that the mean annual temperature has increased at a rate of 0.37°C per decade since 1960.

Additionally, as shown in Figure 1, average temperature trends have increased from 22°C to 25°C from 1970 to 2020. The highest increase was observed between the period 1980–1990 and 1990–2000 with an increase in mean annual temperature by 0.72° – 0.84°C between 2000 and 2020 compared to the 1960-1990 period. Finally, over the period 1980–2020, the highest increase was observed in the coastal areas of Madagascar.

In parallel, temperature indicators show an upward trend in extreme heat waves. The highest increases of over 0.4°C per decade were recorded between 1970 and 2020.

## Background on Madagascar's Droughts

Madagascar, a country located off the southeastern coast of Africa, has experienced severe droughts over the past century. These droughts have been exacerbated by climate change and human activities such as deforestation and overgrazing. The most recent drought occurred between 2015-2018, affecting millions of people in Madagascar.

### Causes of Drought

The primary causes of drought in Madagascar are:

* **Climate Change:** Global warming has led to more frequent and severe droughts.
* **Deforestation:** Trees play a crucial role in regulating the water cycle. The loss of trees due to deforestation has contributed to the drying up of rivers and lakes, leading to reduced rainfall.
* **Overgrazing:** Overgrazing by livestock has depleted soil nutrients and increased erosion, making it more difficult for plants to retain moisture.

### Impact on Agriculture

The droughts have had a significant impact on agriculture in Madagascar. Many farmers rely on rain-fed crops such as rice, corn, and beans. The lack of rainfall has led to crop failures, resulting in food shortages and malnutrition among the population. In addition, livestock have also been affected by the drought, leading to increased prices for meat and milk.

### Solutions

To address these challenges, the GCF is proposing several solutions:

* **Restoration of degraded lands:** This involves planting trees and other vegetation to help retain moisture in the soil.
* **Improved water management:** This includes building dams and reservoirs to store rainwater during wet seasons and release it during dry periods.
* **Diversification of crops:** Farmers can be encouraged to grow drought-resistant crops such as sorghum, millet, and cassava.

### Conclusion

The GCF is committed to helping Madagascar adapt to the impacts of climate change. By investing in sustainable agriculture practices and water management systems, we can help ensure food security for millions of people in this beautiful country.

## Typology of cyclones.

Due to its insular nature in the Indian Ocean, Madagascar, and the project area in particular, are frequently affected by tropical cyclones. Not only are tropical cyclones more frequent in this ocean basin, but they also have a higher intensity than those found elsewhere (Figure 12). On average, tropical storms with wind speeds exceeding 63 km/h are reported to form each year; Madagascar has one of the highest cyclone risks among countries in Africa. The frequency and intensity of tropical cyclones is expected to increase as climate change is associated with a higher frequency of cyclones, resulting in lower rainfall in the project area. However, the intensity of tropical storms will also increase, leading to more intense rainfall events.

### Impact on the project areas

Due to the negative impact of climate change, the project areas and central regions (Antananarivo, Masoala, Haute Matsiatra) throughout Madagascar are expected to be affected by an increase in the frequency of tropical storms. The wet season will also be subject to more frequent flooding.

### Impact on the dry season

On the other hand, during the dry season, these same regions may experience a decrease in rainfall and increased drought conditions. These changes could lead to significant and damaging floods across the country and a high number of human and animal deaths. Floods represent one of the most common natural disasters affecting Madagascar.

Over 30 floods or heavy rainfall events have affected Madagascar in the past 30 years, killing hundreds of people and affecting thousands more. The intensity of these storms has increased over time (Figure 12). For example, the Malagasy River in Volohyane increased by 5 metres and the Mananara River in Farafangana increased by 4 metres between 1980-2007 and 2007-2017. The Vatovavy and Fenoarivo regions appear to be the most threatened by floods. The intensity of these storms has also increased over time, with an increase in the average number of tropical cyclones per year, the average intensity of tropical cyclones, the amplitude of storm surges and the associated rainfall, particularly if precipitation occurs during the dry season.

### Climate impacts and vulnerabilities:
Madagascar has been ranked the fifth most vulnerable country to climate change in the world[1]. ND-Gain measured Madagascar's exposure and sensitivity to climate change as high and stable over time, with a score of 0.85 (out of 1). This makes it one of the top five most vulnerable countries[2]. Being an island nation by virtue of its geographical position, Madagascar is exposed to recurrent climatic events such as cyclones, droughts, floods, and landslides. The country's vulnerability stems from sectors such as agriculture, water resources, livestock, human health, infrastructure, terrestrial and marine biodiversity, and the environment[3]. In addition, the majority of its population lives in rural areas where infrastructures that have been destroyed (including schools, basic health centres, and dispensaries), agricultural infrastructure has been damaged or lost, and livelihoods have been disrupted. The country is also heavily impacted by extreme weather events, particularly cyclones, all of which are directly linked with the negative impact of climate change.

### Agriculture and Climate Change:
Agriculture, the backbone of Madagascar's economy, accounts for 20% of its GDP and employs 80% of the population, with women and youth representing an important proportion. The agricultural sector is highly vulnerable to climate change due to a number of factors including poor farming practices and technologies, such as slash and burn, which contribute to deforestation and soil erosion, further aggravating the effects of climate change[4]. Madagascar's dependence on rain-fed agriculture for growing its staple crops and their over-reliance on single commodities (most often rice) make them extremely vulnerable to climate change. Climate-related extreme events such as increased rainfall during the rainy season lead to increased sedimentation in water bodies, which can cause soil erosion and landslides[5]. This can also increase vector-borne diseases. On the other hand, insufficient rainfall and increased drought conditions will result in(i) lower agricultural production leading to food insecurity; (ii) reduced livestock productivity; (iii) increased vulnerability of crops to increased soil degradation; (iv) more difficult access to drinking water; increased climate-sensitive diseases.

### Output 2.1: Reinforced rural access roads

To minimise damage, water will be systematically directed to the land rather than running along the road surface. To direct runoff away from the road, several parameters will be considered, such as regular slope breaks, minimising avoidance of sunken roads and inclusion of basic drainage. The activities planned under this Output are the following:

* **Activity 2.1.1: Reinforcement of unpaved rural access roads to reduce climate change impacts and water collection efficiency**

In the context of DEFIS+, the following GR4W techniques will be explored while rehabilitation works are performed through structured labour-intensive work.

* Using road surface structures, a system of piping slugs (small openings with a large bulb or water reservoir) will divert water from the road surface to the land for productive use. These structures have two purposes: (i) prevent water from flowing beside the road surface by diverting it from the road; and/or (ii) collect runoff in order to provide irrigation water, recharge groundwater, feeding a spring or directly flowing into an on-farm pond to be used for irrigation.
* The construction of small dams is another GR4W technique that can be used. These are called aquifers. This is a typical issue on many unpaved roads where road runoff builds up, causing erosion and sedimentation. It is best to construct the dam at the lowest point in the slope, as this will allow water to flow downhill, particularly if no side drain is provided and the road template is out sloped or crowned.
* Water Level Dips Meters. DEFIS+ will use Water Level Dip Meters to determine the water level within a borehole, piezometer pipe or other water source. The information gathered from these measurements can be used for the reduction of peak discharge with the proposed structures for underground water systems, are well captured in the DEFIS+ ESMP’s section related to the construction of water tanks – including measures of mitigation and indicators of success.

* Complementing a range of bioengineering methods to revitalise vegetation in the road adjacency. Bioengineering techniques include the use of live stakes, which are cuttings or stems that are planted into soil cuts, rootings and stems as a cost-effective and locally adaptable means of erosion control. It ranges from planting trees and shrubs to using grasses and other plants. The technique is particularly useful for preserving the slopes, in addition, it buffers the water through higher infiltration capacity.

## Background and Context

The Green Climate Fund (GCF) is a global mechanism for financing climate change mitigation and adaptation projects in developing countries. The GCF aims to support the implementation of nationally determined contributions (NDCs) under the Paris Agreement, which are voluntary commitments by countries to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and adapt to the impacts of climate change.

## Objectives

The main objective of this proposal is to support the implementation of climate change adaptation activities in Madagascar. The specific objectives include:

1. Strengthening the capacity of local communities and government institutions to implement climate change adaptation measures.
2. Enhancing the resilience of vulnerable populations, particularly women and youth, to the impacts of climate change.
3. Promoting the use of sustainable land management practices and renewable energy technologies in rural areas.

## Implementation Approach

The implementation approach will be based on a multi-stakeholder partnership model that involves government agencies, civil society organizations, private sector entities, and local communities. The following steps will be taken to achieve the objectives:

1. Conduct a needs assessment to identify the most pressing climate change adaptation challenges in Madagascar.
2. Develop a project proposal that outlines the proposed activities and their expected outcomes.
3. Secure funding from the GCF and other donors for the implementation of the project.
4. Implement the project activities, including capacity building, awareness raising, and technical assistance.
5. Monitor and evaluate the progress of the project to ensure that it is on track to achieve its objectives.

## Monitoring and Evaluation

The monitoring and evaluation (M&E) framework will be based on a combination of quantitative and qualitative indicators. The M&E plan will include:

1. Key performance indicators (KPIs) for each activity, such as the number of people trained in climate change adaptation techniques.
2. Regular reporting to the GCF on progress towards achieving the project objectives.
3. End-of-project evaluation to assess the overall impact of the project and identify lessons learned.

## Conclusion

The implementation of this proposal will contribute to the achievement of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and the Paris Agreement targets in Madagascar. The project is expected to have a positive impact on the livelihoods of vulnerable populations, particularly women and youth, by reducing their vulnerability to climate change impacts and promoting sustainable development.

DEFIS proposes a comprehensive approach to sustainably improving resilience to climate shocks and enhancing productivity on smallholder farms in the six regions of the project. This requires modernizing the present agricultural production systems, which are reliant on conventional irrigation methods and other technologies that are not resilient to climate change.

DEFIS proposes to reduce smallholder farmer’s exposure to climate and economic shocks by climate-protection irrigation methods such as drip irrigation, rainwater harvesting, and integrated pest management (IPM). These will mitigate the adverse effects of climate change caused by extreme climate events and promote and disseminate climate smart agricultural practices. DEFIS will support these efforts through a combination of technical assistance, capacity building, and financial services.

At the field level, the diffusion of water-saving technology such as the implementation of drip irrigation systems and the use of rainwater harvesting techniques will increase the crop yield per hectare by 20% to 30%, which is equivalent to an additional 1.5–2 tons of wheat or maize per hectare. This will increase the recoverable crop area that contributes to high levels of production, thus increasing the overall productivity and income for smallholder farmers.

The project will also support the adoption of integrated pest management methods and other improved agronomic practices will contribute to further reducing production costs and improving yields. The use of drip irrigation systems in combination with rainwater harvesting will be strengthened by investing in ecosystem restoration, including the promotion of an integrated landscape approach that will improve soil fertility and water retention capacity, which will increase the overall productivity of farms through the retention of fertile topsoils. Some activities such as the dissemination of drip irrigation technology and rainwater harvesting techniques are expected to have a direct impact on the nutritional improvement of households. Investments in infrastructure reinforcement are aimed at the rapid expansion of smallholder farmers’ access to markets, which will reduce post-harvest losses due to climate change degradation, thus ensuring sustainability and reducing maintenance costs in the future. Finally, DEFIS will promote waste recycling through the production of biochar from agricultural residues such as rice husks, palm leaves, and handmade briquettes, by using local materials, thus reducing deforestation and contributing to carbon sequestration.

The monitoring, reporting and evaluation (M&E) system of DEFIS will focus on the results and outcomes identified in the project proposal (Annex I, pages 3-8). The M&E system is based on the framework described in the third edition. This system is also used to assess the work of implementing partners in order to guarantee regular, systematic monitoring and evaluation of the implementation progress of the project activities and reporting as well as for knowledge management to evaluate knowledge capital and identify good practices.

A participatory M&E process will be used to ensure the establishment of a good understanding of stakeholders' expectations and their approaches since beginning. This participative approach will also allow all stakeholders to establish a deeper, common comprehension of the objectives, strategies and appropriate information needs; provide them with tools for monitoring progress and reporting on results; and support them in using monitoring data as tools to support management and decision-making.

## Summary of the Secretariat’s assessment

### Project background

1. Madagascar has been ranked the fifth most vulnerable country to climate change in the world and the Notre Dame Global Adaptation Initiative Index, which ranks the climate adaptation capacity of countries, places it among the least adapted countries.
2. The country is one of 35 Least Developed Countries (LDCs) as well as one of the countries least adapted to cope with climate events (ranking 172 out of 181 countries).
3. Madagascar belongs to the group of countries most affected by climate change, with an average temperature increase above global averages and a decrease in rainfall.
4. The country has more than 50 per cent of its population living below the poverty level (USD 2 per day) and 80 per cent of the population living in rural areas, most of whom are smallholder farmers.
5. The number of people living in extreme poverty was 74.3 per cent of the population in 2019, corresponding to an increase of 1.38 million people in just one year (2020), and it is expected to continue to rise.
6. Madagascar experienced unprecedented droughts between 2015 and 2017, which had a negative impact on crop production, food and nutrition security across the country.
7. The south-west and the extreme north are particularly vulnerable due to their high rainfall intensity (more than 800 mm per year).

# Reporting procedures for gender-based violence, GBV and violence against children

All staff, volunteers, consultants and contractors are encouraged and required to report suspected or actual cases of GBV as they are responsible for complying with the company's commitments and hold their direct reports accountable for compliance with the individual Code of Conduct.

The client, contractor and consultant must establish a working relationship with the service provider in advance of any incidents or complaints related to GBV. - They will be assisted by the service provider where appropriate.
- All reports must be kept confidential in accordance with local laws and regulations.
