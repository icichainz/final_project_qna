**GCF's Request for Proposals for Piloting Enhanced Direct Access**

In 2016, the Green Climate Fund announced a pilot initiative for Enhancing Direct Access with the objective of allowing the GCF to effectively operationalize its enhanced direct access modality at the sub-national and national regional level. This includes developed decision-making and stronger local multi-stakeholder engagement.

This project is in response to the request for proposals (RFP) issued by the GCF in July 2016, and it is designed to meet the stated objectives of the RFP, namely: to enhance country ownership of projects and programmes by devolving decision-making at county level. Thereby allowing greater involvement and input from local stakeholders.

A table of how this EDA proposal meets the criteria set out in the GCF Enhancing Direct Access (EDA) Request for Proposals is provided below.

Integrated physical adaptation and community resilience through an enhanced direct access pilot in the public, private, and civil society sectors of three Eastern Caribbean small island developing states

The proposed project is designed to enhance country ownership of adaptation in three small island states by devolving decision-making at county level. The problem that this project seeks to address is that the Eastern Caribbean small island states are particularly vulnerable to climate change.

In September 2017, according to preliminary estimates, Antigua and Barbuda has experienced damages totaling over USD $102 million; changes in flow (losses) over USD 20 million; and its estimated recovery needs are USD 52.5 million or 13% of GDP.

In the case of Dominica, estimates of the total damage of Hurricane Maria can reach as excess of 200 percent of GDP or approximately USD $1.3 billion. Dominica's GDP in 2016 is USD 928 million. Dominica is among the lowest GDP per capita ranking in the Caribbean.

Climate change for the Eastern Caribbean sub-region is: between 1°C and 4°C warmer by the end of the century; average annual rainfall is projected to decrease by the end of the century, however rainfall variability is expected to increase; sea level rise will be significant in some coastal conditions; and hurricane intensity is likely to increase, with estimates of 5–13%, increased intensity[2].

This vulnerability arises from recent disaster costs, coupled with the ongoing effects of climate change, have greatly contributed to unsustainable budgetary constraints. The Eastern Caribbean pilot countries are vulnerable to climate change because the adaptation infrastructural works, which are required for climate resilience, are often not proportionate in proportion to population size of these small islands. This is a major socioeconomic challenge that requires significant financial resources and proportion of adaptation. Moreover, the relative impact of an extreme event such as hurricane or earthquake on a small island country can have a disproportionate impact on gross domestic product, compared to a larger country with similar exposure. Adaptation generally affects a small proportion of its total land territory[3].

The result is relatively higher adaptation and disaster risk reduction costs per capita than for other Caribbean islands and areas—especially those that are also geographically isolated, have a poor road network, and have high transport costs.

The objective of this project is to strengthen institutional capacities and increase the resilience of 50% of the population in the Eastern Caribbean pilot countries to climate variability and change; of which 50% are women. This will be achieved through infrastructure, strengthened buildings, and enhanced ecosystem services.

- **Output 2**: Government-commissioned climate adaptation mainstreaming and innovative approaches where appropriate. This will demonstrate enhanced direct access in the public sector for the Eastern Caribbean pilot countries to GCF-financed concrete local area adaptation projects to climate-proof infrastructure investments and co-financing from the Government

**Rationale for grant financial instrument**

This project's choice of financial instrument, a full grant request, is necessary for the project to overcome barriers confronting small island states and achieve the project’s objectives by increasing resilience in vulnerable communities. The project is designed as a Revolving Fund (RF) that will provide concessional loans to a Reserve Fund, which will then extend credit to the private sector. The private sector targeted in this proposal are homeowners and small business owners whose property or livelihoods are most at risk from climate change. The RF will provide them with access to financing to protect their property from climate extremes.

The Revolving Fund, an unsecured, concessional debt fund targeting vulnerable populations, is a quasi-debt/quasi-grant facility. The debt structure of the facility is primarily driven by the creation of new concessional financing by the recipient country’s central bank. This is achieved through the issuance of bonds and/or other securities that are “revolved.” The “grant” funding occurs through discretionary forgiveness and payment flexibility to certain categories of borrowers, including those who have fallen behind on their payments, even if concessional rates may be difficult, instead of pursuing legal recourse against such defaulted borrowers.

The Revolving Fund will not be increasing its capital base or seeking to increase returns. At the same time, natural shock such as extreme weather events and other disasters can aggressively “kick” up interest payment structures and reduce funding to home and business owners. The Revolving Fund will complement debt from the private sector, such as local banks, in order to provide a more comprehensive package of financing for homeowners and small businesses. In this way it met through the Revolving Fund, beneficiaries will be in a better position to increase productivity and borrow from local banks. For more information, see Section E.6: Efficiency and effectiveness.

**Market demand for concessional loan financing – home and business owners**

The market demand for the Revolving Fund in the three ESA countries is estimated at USD 213 million, which exceeds the USD 80 million that will be available in the EDA project. The analysis is provided in Section C.5 Market Overview.

# C.1: Strategic Context

Please describe relevant national, sub-national, regional, global, political, and/or economic factors that help to contextualize the proposal, including existing national and sector policies and strategies.

## Climate change is a serious and substantial threat to the economies of Caribbean nations, the livelihoods of communities and the environment. The region's small island states are particularly vulnerable to climate change; they are small and remote (<120,000 people per country), have limited natural resource base (48% of land area is forested) and are highly dependent on tourism. The Caribbean is already experiencing the effects of climate variability and change through damage from severe weather systems such as hurricanes and tropical storms, increased rainfall intensity, more frequent droughts, and rising sea levels. These effects are evident in the decline of some coastal tourism resources, but also in the socioeconomic sectors which support tourism (e.g., water supply, food resources, health and biodiversity). These effects will be magnified under projected climate scenarios for the sub-region.

### Climate trends in the Eastern Caribbean include increased temperature, rainfall extremes (both drought and high intensity downpours) and more severe hurricanes. The Hadley Centre PRECS (Providing Regional Climatic Models for Impact Studies) regional model simulations indicate that the Caribbean will be warmer by 2050 under the A1 scenario. For the eastern Caribbean SS2A (higher emissions) and B2 (lower emissions) scenarios are:

- **A1 Scenario:** The average temperature in the Caribbean during the 20th century exceeded the global average!
- **SS2A Scenario:** The average temperature is projected to increase by 3°C at the end of this century.
- **B2 Scenario:** Rainfall variability is projected to increase, with more intense downpours and extended drought conditions.
- **Hurricane intensity is likely to increase. Increases in hurricane frequency are uncertain.**

### Higher Temperatures: Health impacts, Risks and Adaptation measures

Recent epidemics in Latin America and the Caribbean underscore the risks of higher temperatures to human health, as transmission of vector-borne diseases increases with temperature. Epizootiology research has linked dengue fever transmission to temperature, where warmer temperatures can shorten incubation periods from 12 days at 30°C or only 7 days at 35°C. #### Financing Adaptation in a "4 Degree World"

On September 6, 2017, the second most powerful hurricane on record made direct landfall on Barbuda, the northern island of the twin island state of Antigua and Barbuda. This is one of the projections of the Regional Climate Model for the Caribbean region under a high emissions scenario (RCP8.5) that shows an increase in the frequency and intensity of hurricanes. The hurricane destroyed 90% of the building stock in Barbuda, and all communications we destroyed, leaving the community without access to basic services such as water, electricity, and telecommunications for over two months.

The sand spit which formed the protection barriers for the Codington lagoon national park (the second largest frigate bird sanctuary in the Caribbean) was completely washed away by the hurricane. The island is now vulnerable to storm surges and flooding.

In August of 2015, Tropical Storm Erika impacted the island of Dominica. In just a few hours, the strong winds and rains destroyed critical infrastructure that eliminated an estimated five years of normal investment for the country. According to the World Bank, the total damage and loss was estimated at US$483 million, equivalent to 90 percent of Dominica’s Domestic Product.

The devastating losses borne by Grenada’s economy in 2004 and 2005 from the passage of Hurricanes Ivan and Emily respectively put the country’s inherent vulnerability in stark relief, with some of the impacted industries still in recovery 10 years later. The total damage to the island was estimated at US$387 million or approximately 69% of Grenada’s Gross Domestic Product (GDP) [DECS, 2004]. Direct and secondary losses were experienced in virtually every sector and these damages were compounded by the passage of Hurricane Emily just 10 months later.

## Case Study 5: Why is the cost of adaptation to climate change so high in small islands?

### Background

The island nation of Antigua and Barbuda, located in the Eastern Caribbean, has been severely impacted by Hurricane Irma. The hurricane caused significant damage to the main village in Barbuda, where 90% of buildings were damaged or destroyed.

### Impact on Adaptation Costs

Adaptation to climate change that involves infrastructure works requires large up-front overhead costs, which in the case of small islands cannot be downscaled in proportion to the population's size. This is a major socioeconomic reality for small island developing states (SIDS).

Moreover, the relative impact of an extreme event such as a hurricane that can affect most of a small island’s territory has a disproportionate impact on that state’s gross domestic product, compared to a larger country where an individual hurricane may only impact one or two regions. This is especially true for SIDS with higher adaptation and disaster risk reduction costs per capita in countries with small populations and areas—especially those that are highly exposed.

### Economic Declines

Economic declines as well as demands from sectors such as health, education and debt servicing has made it extremely challenging for Antigua and Barbuda to continue to pursue self-financing the cost of adapting to climate change. The nature and size of the problem cannot be addressed with the current flow of donor resources, which, while important, are insufficient in scale and scope for the task at hand.

# G.1 Risk Assessment Summary

## Assumptions underlying the project Proposal

The project is conceived based on the following main assumptions: firstly, that OECS countries would partner with Antigua and Barbuda to implement the project; secondly, the countries will have sufficient resources for their contributions on climate change set forth in their National Determined Contributions (NDCs); and finally, that the OECS Secretariat will have the requisite capacity to successfully provide oversight for the effective and implementation of the project.

## Risk register and methodology

A project risk register was developed to list all identified risks that may affect the project. The register was compiled using the following baseline documents and records:

- Antigua and Barbuda National Adaptation Plan (NAP) consultation in Antigua and Barbuda, Dominica and Grenada, and the OECS Commission M&E Unit
- Consultations with OECS countries between April 2016 and June 2017 (four in-person consultations, several phone calls, and circulation of project update briefs)
- Environmental and Social Impact Assessments for similar activities, including the seed pilot in Antigua and Barbuda
- Financial model and feasibility analysis of the Revolving Fund Programme for Adaptation in Antigua and Barbuda
- IPCC AR5 (2014) and its recommendations for Small Island Developing States

The register is also based on the historical knowledge of the culture and socio-political history of the pilot countries. The risk register identifies risks in the following categories: Strategic, Regulatory, Operational, ESS and Gender, Legal, Compliance, Performance, Funding, and Market. The risks list risk mitigation measures, and ranks the resulting probability of occurrence.

## Project staff, consultants, and project partners

The project staff, consultants, the Accredited Entity and project partners will maintain continued monitoring and evaluation of the implementation of the project to identify new or latent risks. Further, the audit plan for the project will include consideration of the risk register as part of the overall audit process.

The Legal Unit of the Accredited Entity and the respective national decision-making committees will provide necessary technical support to ensure that the project is implemented in accordance with the approved project document. ## A robust M&E framework for enhancing direct access

A robust monitoring, reporting, evaluation and ultimately a learning framework will be established via the OECS (representing small countries) and/or regional/international research institutions. This is a key feature of the EDA project as a new pilot modality for the GCF, and a new approach to programming climate financing for small island developing states.

The overall theory of change for the EDA project is that on-granting and concessional-on-lending directly to beneficiaries will provide resources to communities, home owners and building owners, and that these beneficiaries will implement changes in their buildings and infrastructure. This will lead to increased adaptation action by the target population in the overall theory of change. Critically, it assumes that i) the target group (at risk communities, home and building owners) decides on how they want to use the resources provided; ii) the EDA project is able to identify and engage a robust EDA modality, and help other island nations and vulnerable communities adopt impactful EDA approaches.

Through the implementation arrangements below, and through the development of partnerships with regional and international research institutions, the following questions will be addressed:

- Are the EDA project decision-making processes resulting in funding awards that target the vulnerable section of the population?
- Are the EDA project’s on-granting and on-lending awards leading to increased adaptation action of the target population? If yes, by how much?
- Are people becoming more resilient as a consequence of the on-granting and on-lending awards?
- What kinds of climate change actions are being pursued by the target population through the EDA’s on-granting—lending approaches (e.g., economic ecological adaptations; social vulnerability approaches aimed at addressing poverty, food security, etc.); and how is this impacting or enhancing its systems resilience/adaptation approaches which target action to specific climate change risks?
- What knowledge has been gathered, what are the lessons learned and what is the scope for replication?

**I. Background**

The Green Climate Fund (GCF) was established with the purpose of making a significant and ambitious contribution to efforts towards attaining the goals set by the international community in the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). The GCF will promote a paradigm shift toward low-emission and climate-resilient development pathways by providing financial resources for projects, programs, and activities that are consistent with and to adapt to the impacts of climate change.

The GCF is an independent entity within the framework of the financial mechanism of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). It is governed and supervised by a Board consisting of 30 members elected from among the parties to the UNFCCC. The GCF Board is the Green Climate Fund. It is supported by an independent Secretariat, accountable to the Board for carrying out its functions in accordance with the rules of procedure governing administrative, legal and financial expertise. The Fund's headquarters are located in Songdo, Incheon, Republic of Korea.

The Governing Instrument of the Fund establishes the direct access modality for requesting funding from the GCF. This modality is intended to provide a streamlined process for implementing entities accredited by the Fund. The GCF states that the Board will consider implementing entities on an ad hoc basis as they are accredited and approved.

In this framework, the objective of Enhancing Direct Access (EDA) is to enhance country ownership and accountability in the design and implementation of projects and programs. Specifically allowing greater involvement and input from impacted stakeholders. EDA is designed to provide a more streamlined process for requesting funding from the Fund by aggregating individual, bespoke projects towards a more comprehensive, stakeholder-driven programmatic approach. This will allow for a more efficient use of resources through improved management criteria and result measurement.

In March 2016, the Board agreed to initially allocate USD 50 million per year at least 10 pilot projects, including at least four pilot sites to be implemented in small island developing States, the least developed countries (LDCs), and landlocked developing countries (LLDCs).

The pilot phase will offer the Fund an opportunity to gain experience and additional insights through such an approach.
