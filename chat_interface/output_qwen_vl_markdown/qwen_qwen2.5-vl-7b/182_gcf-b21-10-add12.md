### A.2. Project / Programme Executive Summary

Please provide a summary of this project, including the objectives and primary measurable benefits (see evaluation criteria in section E). The detailed description can be described in section C.

Burkina is a Sahelian country hard hit by the effects of climate change, which includes a steady decline in the volume of rainfall. This has led to an increase in desertification and land degradation, with 70% of Burkina Faso's territory now represented only 61% of the standard living of an average inhabitant in Sub-Saharan African developing countries. This has resulted in a significant decrease in agricultural productivity, which is the main source of income for the economy, providing livelihood to more than 80% of the population and at the same time it is most vulnerable to the effects of climate change. Droughts and famines are factors contributing to a vicious circle leading to one out of every three people living in rural areas.

The country has only 3% access to electricity in rural areas mostly through diesel generators. The area county development strategy (SDA) for Burkina Faso, which was adopted by the government in 2014, aims at increasing this figure to 5%. This is a very ambitious target and will require innovative solutions. The project seeks to introduce a paradigm shift toward low emission electricity access through innovative models, essentially green mini grids which would be financed by private sector investors.

The country has one of the highest electricity tariffs, which range from USD 0.23 to 0.72 kWh (€/kWh: EUR 0.19 to 0.53 kWh) in urban areas. The industrial tariff for electricity is USD 0.48/kWh (€/kWh: EUR 0.34-0.64 kWh). The government currently supports a policy of universal access to electricity through the creation of rural mini grids, which are financed by rural electric cooperatives (COOPÉL). Such policy has not proved very successful in terms of rural energy access.

The total cost of the project is EUR 50 M.

The project includes 3 components:

1. A review and restructuring of the legal, regulatory and institutional framework for a rural electrification model based on private sector investment to establish sustainable rural energy access financed by private sector developers.
2. The installation of 100 mini-grids powered by an aggregate solar PV capacity estimated to 1.4 MWp to achieve universal access in the rural areas of Burkina Faso.
3. The provision of productive use equipment to support economic activity in the target area.

Mini-grid projects will be financed through a combination of public and private capital, with the government providing a grant to carry out detailed surveys and design and an on post capital grant per connection successively contributing to the development of the mini grid. A 10-year concessional loan from the World Bank will be provided for 3 access levels and above.

The tariff structure will be designed to provide flexibility to the mini-grid developer to provide flexible tariffs during the day to incentivize production use during broad daylight. This will be reflected in the tender documents and mini-grid developers will be informed before they submit their response proposal to the RFP.

### Additional information on the disbursement of the RBP Grant

The result based payment (RBP) proposal is to be structured around a strong control mechanism. As an example, for the type of contract that could be implemented, we would like to present one existing platform ("the Platform") ADB has been working with in the context of its Africa Green Minigrid Market Development Programme.

It is a web-based platform that is unlocking the energy access market and facilitating rapid deployment of mini-grids and distribution networks through a competitive market. The RBP will be based payment model used by streamlining evaluation and verification while capturing key data in a sophisticated database with robust analytical tools and an intuitive user interface.

## Quality assurance processes

The Platform centralizes all mini-grid data on one web-based online site, enabling effective management of all mini-grid activities from end to end – from feasibility analysis through system operations:

- An evaluator updates status when a project is pre-approved; funds are set aside for the project, to be disbursed over time as the project progresses.
- Once a mini-grid and its associated distribution infrastructure is implemented, the mini-grid operator reports the required commissioning data to validate that the Quality Assurance Framework (QAF) protocol has been followed and only then will it be ready to connect.
- Then, the mini-grid operator uploads lists of connected customers (via Comma Separated Values, CSV files or mobile app)
- The Platform reviews submitted data and updates the status if the criteria are met
- The mini-grid operator shares the required performance data with the control platform
- It is also possible to integrate a smart meter that can also verify that the Quality Assurance Framework service level agreement (SLA) has been followed.

In the way the project is designed, we would like to use a similar platform selected competitively to monitor the connections. The platform can also provide a transparent communication tool to share site data to potential bidders and be used for optimal design of GMS's. After the selected bidders are allocated concessions, they will report progress to the platform at different stages.

It is expected that the services of the platform will be mobilised for 6 years during the implementation phase of the project. The data accumulated during this phase will constitute a valuable resource for DFIs and Climate finance Institutions to benchmark GMS implementation processes and will be useful in the scaling up of the GMS concept to other regions and countries.

On the basis of verifications made through the chosen platform, the proposed process for RBP payments would be as follows:

- 50% of the RBP payment upon the successful completion of the mini-grid power generation infrastructure and distribution network by the selected mini-grid operator as reported to the platform and validated by the platform.
- 50% of the RBP on a quarterly basis for the number of successful connections (i.e. wiring and user's premise connection) realised during that quarter, and upon validation of the QAF protocol (based on the successful monitoring of the connections) by the control platform.

# C.1. Context Strategic

Please describe relevant national, sub-national, regional, global, political, and/or economic factors that help to constitute the project context, including existing national and sector policies and strategies.

## Economic situation:

After a period of sluggishness, Burkina Faso's economic activity has resumed in 2017, following the slowdown of the real GDP growth rate to 4% in 2014 and 2015, mainly due to poor rainfall during the crop year and the socio-political upheaval. This recovery is largely driven by agricultural activities in all sectors of the economy, mainly through solid performances in the agricultural and extractive industrial sectors.

### Poverty incidence:

Poverty incidence is in net decline but remains high in 2014 at 40.1% compared to 46.7% in 2009. Poverty is essentially a rural phenomenon (85%), with poverty rates of 30.5% and 40.5%, respectively, for urban areas (13.9%) and rural areas (47.5%). The incidence of poverty is higher among women than men (46.2% vs. 34.5%).

### Rural electrification:

With one of the lowest rate of rural electrification in Sub-Saharan Africa standing at 3%, the challenges and barriers to rural electrification are linked to the prevailing socio-economic conditions. These include: (i) prevalent rural poverty; (ii) excessive population density, which makes it difficult to connect households to the grid; (iii) a lack of infrastructure, which discourages industrialization; (iv) social challenges pertaining to demography, headship and education; and (v) the traditional practices that discourage the use of electricity.

The proposed project is in line with the Country Energy Sector Policy (POSEN 2014-2025). The POSEN articulates that in order for the country to attain universal access on electricity, it should move towards an energy sector that depends on endogenous resources and technologies. This will be achieved by: (i) improving the efficiency of energy production and energy management capacity; (ii) liberalize the electricity sub-sector; (iii) control the cost of energy inputs; (iv) ensure better access to energy services; and (v) promote other sources of energy (renewable energy); and (vi) sensitize the population to more efficient use of energy.

## Energy sector overview:

The electricity sector in Burkina Faso is under the supervision of the Ministry of Energy and Mines. Other key players in the sector are the sector regulator (ARIDE), The National Electricity Company (SONABEL) and the Fonds de Développement de l'Electricité Rurale (FDER). The Rural Electrification Agency of Burkina Faso (AREB) is also involved. There is neither an oil producer nor an exporter.

Therefore, the country is heavily dependent on imported fuel/oil/capacities from neighboring countries, particularly Côte d'Ivoire.

The country is divided into two distribution segments, with distinct regulations and actors. The first segment includes the area served by SONABEL and covers the main population centers. SONABEL has a monopoly over transmission and distribution in this segment, which is heavily regulated and has been successful (although not perfectly so) because SONABEL is perceived as a financially sound off-taker.

The second segment includes rural areas, which are the responsibility of FDE/ABER. These areas have no electricity generation systems; instead, they rely on small-scale renewable energy sources such as solar panels or wind turbines. The main actors in this segment include local cooperatives called COOPELs, generally managed by small volunteer teams, who receive technical and financial support from FDE/ABER.

Currently, there are more than 180 local cooperatives with an authorization to manage local distribution networks in the second segment. 77% of them (in 2015) were connected to the grid and purchased power in bulk from SONABEL, but the remaining 23% generated their own electricity using solar panels or wind turbines. In 2015, 16% of the COOPEL generation systems were estimated to include photovoltaic (PV) components.

Hurdles faced by these cooperatives include: (i) lack of technical and financial resources; (ii) theft and destruction of equipment; (iii) use of unscrupulous payments; (iv) lack of sound financial management. In addition to this, the COOPELs are also tasked with the repayment of the generation systems they invested in after three years of operation, and by 2015, only half of them had not reached a critical number of connections needed to make the investment viable. Finally yet importantly, COOPEL management is supposed to work on a voluntary basis without any remuneration for their efforts.

Most COOPELs lack the necessary technical capabilities, so they must rely on external technical providers (so called "Fermiers" in French) to manage the distribution network. The tasks assumed by the "Fermiers" varies from one COOPEL to another, but they are generally responsible for maintenance and repair of equipment.

The relationship between "Fermiers" and COOPELs varies drastically from case to case and may be regulated by contracts or agreements. In some cases, the COOPELs fund their capacity to pay its "Fermier", and reimburses the concessional loans provided by FDE/ABER.

In addition to these challenges, the cooperatives were aiming at a single electricity tariff across the country irrespective of the production source and thus highly subsidized.

Consequently, the cooperatives are not financially sound, and have limited capacity to connect more households to electricity. As of 2015, there were 347 communities having access to electricity (serving approximately 68,000 people), 229 communities with access to electricity, 3242 households connected (230,000 individuals), 3 MW capacity installed with fuel, and 405 kWs in solar PV.

Proposed mitigation solutions include: (i) payment measures; (ii) using payment systems that will ensure bill collection; and (iii) use remote management services such as online platforms to avoid fraud and electricity theft.

### To help improve the rural electrification rate from 3% currently to 10% by 2025, the authorities have now decided the following steps:
- (i) Implement a new regulatory framework in favor of private sector operations,
- (ii) Enable a financially sustainable operating model for electricity production in rural areas in order to ease the process of obtaining financial and development financing and allow the sector to develop, and therefore more households to have access to electricity.

### Trend in electricity generation
Power generation has increased drastically over the last decade. SOBELMEX's customer base increased from 223,312 in 2006 to 544,879 in 2015. Power consumption doubled from 550 GWh to 1,125 GWh during the same period. Nevertheless, there has been a significant increase of power generation in Côte d'Ivoire since 2013. At a recent census, electricity supply is only enough to serve the non-peak periods. Load shedding has increased significantly reaching a record average of 340 hours in 2015, compared to 180 hours in 2009.

### Challenges
The country's limited supply of electricity is one of the most striking situations on the continent, with an access rate of 18% as compared to the African average of 46%. The cost per kWh of electricity ranges from €3.25 to €7.02 USD /kWh and remains one of the highest in West Africa (10 to 13 times more expensive for Côte d'Ivoire). Since over 65% of national electricity is generated by thermal power plants, oil prices have a significant impact on electricity production costs. Furthermore, the country imports, on average, more than 30% of its energy consumption from Côte d'Ivoire where access conditions are better and the cost per kWh lower (€1.25 USD /kWh). ### Mini-grid development:
The concept of mini grid is not new in Burkina Faso. ## Regulatory regime:

The country has been divided into five distribution segments, with distinct regulations and actors. The first segment includes the area covered by SONAMEL, the national utility, which covers the main population centers. SONAMEL has a monopoly over transmission and distribution, but generation is open to other parties and independent power producers.

A regulatory body, the National Energy Regulatory Commission (CNRE), was established in 2013 with the mandate to regulate operators, arbitrate disputes and protect consumer rights. The government is currently aiming to strengthen CNRE's powers by increasing its budget and giving it more power over issuance of acts, pricing, control and arbitration.

Power distribution is the responsibility of local cooperatives (COOPELLS) in the country’s second distribution segment.

An EU funded study* documented the problems associated with the COOPELLS which led to the conclusion that the COOPELL model in its current state is not appropriate for rural Burkina. Providing a more robust and sustainable model for rural electrification was recommended, and several action plans were drafted to support renewable energies (PANER) and drive implementation of the Sustainable Energy for All initiative (SE4ALL). These action plans also target long-term access to clean and renewable energies. The target is for the national electrification rate to reach 36% by 2015, 55% in 2020.

The government has been working on a new law that will create a framework for energy efficiency leading to the creation of a new agency (NANEECRE) created to promote renewable energies and energy efficiency.

##### Productive use

To be most profitable, solar mini-grids need to sell the maximum amount of electricity during the day – when the sun is shining. In the evenings, the network relies on batteries, which are the most expensive parts of the system. Therefore efficiently managing power consumption in the evenings help reduce the CAPEX requirements of the mini-grid.

Unfortunately, this ideal consumption pattern is the exact opposite of the current situation as what appears in most cases is that the electricity is used during the day for lighting and other household uses, while in the evening customers sales, mini-grids developers promote productive uses of electricity during the day, essentially small and medium enterprises (SMEs).

Best practice relative to promoting productive use involves community capacity building and micro-finance. Rural communities need support to identify business opportunities. Technical assistance is then required to help them simulate their business ideas and develop a business plan. This can be provided by organizations with close ties to the target communities. At this stage, the idea is to request in the RFP that mini-grid developers provide technical assistance to the local communities. The second step involves providing micro-finance to support the local economic activity in order to assume the business incubator role that will require to support local economic activity.

In parallel, there is additionally a need for financing of the small business ideas developed. A local microfinance institution can provide credit to the fledgling entrepreneurs. Additional options would be credit provided by a local commercial bank or directly by the mini-grids developer (this is subject to regulation). Under this alternative framework, the mini-grid developer will have an incentive to provide a loan to the entrepreneur and make a business contribution that he or she uses to finance the necessary CAPEX for the new micro enterprises, while recuperating progressively over time the cost of the loan.

There could also be a partnership between the mini-grid developer and a local bank or a local micro-finance institution so the mini-grid developer will direct the entrepreneurs to the financial institution when they require productive equipment or to apply for a loan. This is the preferred solution at this stage.

F.2. Technical Evaluation

Please provide an assessment from the technical perspective. If a particular technological solution has been chosen, describe how it would be appropriate for this project.

There is a design compromise in mini-grids between the sizing of batteries for storage and the provision of a diesel backup that tends to reduce the battery capacity needed but introduces a fossil fuel component. To avoid the issues of reliance on diesel fuel, the proposed solution is to use solar PV as the primary energy source.

The solution proposed to achieve acceptable battery capacity while providing adequate night service is to opt for an all-grid mini-grid with a 10 kWp grid connected system and a 50 kWh battery bank. This will provide power at or above their subscribed capacity during night time.

Decentralized solar PV systems in use will enable efficient charging and limiting of night time uses so that in an average mini-grid circuit (110 kWe) serving 500 users, the available solar energy during the day is fully used for serving daytime productive loads. The system will be designed to provide a minimum of 2 hours of power supply to Households which will mandatorily be LED and controlled through the allowed power rating for each household. Due to the very low number of houses restricted to the small number of high-end households, the use of battery backup-only is feasible.

This rural model through its energy autonomy is well suited to the remoteness of the target rural regions. The smart mini-grid concept proposed in this project will be implemented by a local team of technicians trained and supervised by Bobo Diloasua with a team of local technicians trained for technical support. This addresses the issue of lack of technical expertise in the region, which is a major challenge to the implementation of mini-grids.

The smart mini-grid concept proposed is compatible for a grid interconnection in the future. However, this will be through the original bulk import from the grid at night and the bulk export to the grid during day light. There are also a bulk import from grid in the right and the left.

# G.1 Risk Assessment Summary

Please provide a summary of main risk factors. Detailed description of risk factors and mitigation measures can be elaborated in G.2.

There are 7 key risks which could delay implementation:

* **1. Political will:** This is mitigated by the high degree of political willingness and a sense of urgency to implement the rural electrification program.
* **2. Security risk:** This is mitigated by the fact that proposed region for the implementation are in low security risk areas.
* **3. Lack of financing risk:** This is mitigated by the fact that ADB and EU have already reserved funding for this project. GCF funding will be critical to ensure timely completion. Selected private sector bidders need to achieve financial closure after being selected. The risk is that the innovative nature of the project and country risk does not attract players with sufficient equity and debt capacity to achieve financial close. The funding brought in by project by GCF, ADB and EU is likely to attract commercial lenders.
* **4. Regulatory framework risk:** The bulk of regulatory environment includes changes in regulatory framework. The risk here is that delay in setting up this environment will delay the launching of the final selection process for mini-grid developers as well as legal and regulatory issues which could arise during the implementation phase of the project. The level of capacity at FDE/ABERI is also a constraint, which is being addressed through the SEFA grant.
* **5. Delays in implementation risk:** There may be delays in the implementation of mini-grids once the regulatory and technical aspects are out of the way. The RBP should give enough incentive to private developers to move on at the best pace possible with the implementation of mini-grids and the connection of the households.
* **6. Willingness of private sector operators to participate:** The choice of a public-private partnership model or a financially sound tariff would mitigate that risk.
* **7. Effectiveness of technology used in mini-grid:** This is mitigated by the fact that solar powered mini-grids have been implemented in other African countries.
