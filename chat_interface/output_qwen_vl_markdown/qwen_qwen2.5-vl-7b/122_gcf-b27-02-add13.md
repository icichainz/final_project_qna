A.21. Executive summary (max. 750 words, approximately 1-3 pages)

Mongolia lacks the necessary resources to finance its planned transition to a low-carbon and resource-efficient economy and achieve its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) targets. The financing needed is estimated at US$4 billion over five years.

Despite an ambitious agenda, both the Government of Mongolia (GOM) and the domestic financial sector are unable to deliver finance at scale to support transition to low-emission technologies. The economy is also constrained by a lack of access to international capital markets due to its membership in the Low-Cost Facility of the International Monetary Fund (IMF) with a restricted ability to borrow. The financial sector is being hampered by high interest rates and limited access to foreign currency, which limits investment opportunities. The main barrier to the adoption of advanced technologies is the cost of financing them, especially where key adopters are low-income households and businesses.

High lending interest rates and the lack of affordable credit create a significant challenge for the financing of low-carbon and resource-efficient measures, which require longer-term, affordable credit due to the cost of technology and equipment. The current financial instruments available in Mongolia do not meet the needs of beneficiaries. Existing green financing facilities do not match the scale needed to induce transformational impact.

The Mongolia Green Finance Corporation (MGFC) is a joint public-private sector effort to create a national financing vehicle (NFV) to overcome the existing challenges and constraints of climate change mitigation. The MGFC will provide long-term, affordable credit for low-carbon technologies by supporting households and businesses to switch to low-carbon technologies; and to create an improved policy environment and build the capacity and awareness of stakeholders in support of this mission.

## Climate vulnerability baseline

Despite Mongolia's marginal historical contribution to global climate change, the country is characterized by increasing temperatures and precipitation extremes. The frequency of extreme weather events and their impacts from climate change, Mongolia’s recorded temperatures have already increased by 2.4 degrees Celsius since 1960. According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change anticipates annual temperatures in Mongolia of between 2.5-5.5 degrees Celsius, along with increases in drought and desertification, making it one of the most vulnerable countries.

The already severe effects of climate change have accelerated rural-to-urban migration, resulting in even further climate impacts. As weather and temperature swings become more severe, large-scale livestock mortality is expected to increase. The combination of these factors will likely lead to increased human forcing herders to move to the areas around Ulaanbaatar. These peri-urban settlements primarily use poor-quality coal for heating and cooking, with 90% of Ulaanbaatar’s winter air pollution and has serious health implications – particularly for children.

While the Mongolian Government has committed to the Paris Agreement, as well as establishing ambitious Mongolian NDC targets, the abundance of cheap domestic coal means that Mongolia will not likely meet its climate change commitments. The only mechanism which the NDCs are necessary to promote is to educate, finance and make more sustainable technologies.

## B.2. Theory of change (max. 1000 words, approximately 2 pages plus diagram)

The GCM has made strong commitments and ambitious policies for reducing air pollution and GHG emissions in Mongolia. However, the implementation of these commitments will require a significant amount of capital investment to be implemented. The MGFC presents an optimal public-private partnership model that will significantly contribute to the removal of financial, institutional, technical and social barriers that hamper the achievement of the GCM’s objectives.

Financial institutions in Mongolia exhibit significant interest in green investment; however, they face a number of internal and external barriers towards wide-scale adoption of green financing that the MGFC can help overcome. The MGCF was formally established with the overall objective to contribute to GHG emissions reduction and climate change adaptation in Mongolia by promoting the mainstreaming of green, affordable, and gender inclusive financial products to allow households and energy intensive users to switch to low carbon technologies (Outcome 1) and to contribute to create an improved policy environment, as well as build the capacity of key stakeholders for the implementation of the GCF (Outcome 2).

D.4. Needs of recipient (max. 500 words, approximately 1 page)

Vulnerability to climate change

Mongolia's unique climate and geography lends to diversity in extreme events, such as dzuds, dust storms, flash floods, and severe forest fires. The number of extreme weather events has doubled in the last 20 years and is expected to increase. This, along with other drivers, contributes to Mongolia’s ranking on the Global Climate Risk Index (GCR) for 2016. These climate-related risks have important implications for rural households with traditional livelihoods and driving haphazard settlement in peri-urban areas. Such areas are more vulnerable to extreme weather such as flash floods, and rely on raw coal for heat, driving severe air pollution within communities and public health.

Economic and social development level of the country and the affected population

The World Bank classifies Mongolia as a lower-middle income country, with a GNI per capita at USD 3,590. Real GDP growth forecasts increase from 6.0% to 6.3% in 2017, reflecting a positive outlook on the Mongolian economy. The highest share of total household expenditure is spent on food and non-food items; food income in Ulaanbaatar’s ger areas is estimated at MNT 550,000-800,000 (USD 227–255*) per month and the average monthly salary for a skilled worker is USD 134. The average rent paid by households is USD 69/month, indicating a limited ability to invest in improvements of energy efficiency of housing and/or living conditions.

Absence of alternative sources of financing

Public financing: Mongolia’s near financial crisis in 2016 led the GOM to receive a combination of USD 5.5 billion in concessional loans from China, Russia, and Japan, and USD 3.7 billion in grants from the World Bank. This results with an increase in foreign exchange reserves, a reduction in government debt in percentage to GDP, and a decrease in inflation rate. Nevertheless, the growth outlook is subject to a fall in external demand for commodities and higher fuel prices, while domestic fiscal policies will remain prudent.

Private sector financing: The banking sector remains the dominant player in the financial system. Non-banking institutions, such as insurance companies, micro-finance institutions and savings and credit cooperatives, remain small in terms of asset size. There is no corporate bond market. The equity market is also underdeveloped. Mongolia’s capital markets are dominated by foreign banks, which have limited financing sources requiring substantial institutional investors to invest in long-term financial products. The cost of debt for the private sector is 10% and 9%, respectively, compared with 7% and 6% for public sector* and the average deposit and lending rates are at 13.0% and 18.8%, respectively.*

1. Introduction

### 1.1 Project Background

Mongolia is a land-locked country located in Central Asia between China and Russia. It is the most sparsely populated country in the world, and nearly half of its total population of 3.1 million people are living in its capital city, Ulaanbaatar (UB), which is known to be the coldest capital in the world. Mongolia has a low carbon economy with a GDP per capita of US$2054. Despite this economic growth, however, Mongolia lacks necessary resources to finance its planned transition to low-carbon technologies.

The Government of Mongolia (GoM) has developed supportive policy environment to promote energy efficiency. However, the domestic financial sector is not able to deliver at scale to support the transition to low-carbon technologies, especially as the financial sector is burdened by the high cost of capital and limited access to international financing for climate change mitigation projects. The adoption of advanced and low-carbon technologies is the cost of financing those, specifically when adopters are low-income households and domestic businesses with income and budget constraints.

The Mongolia Green Finance Corporation (MGFC) sets out to change this landscape. The MGFC is a joint public-private initiative between the GoM and the World Bank Group (WBG). Its purpose is to remove challenges and constraints for shifting to low-carbon and green economy. It specifically targets the mainstreaming of green, energy efficient technologies and the adoption of advanced and low-carbon technologies and acts as an instrument to create an improved policy environment, as well as to build the capacity and awareness among stakeholders in Mongolia on climate change mitigation policies and projects.

The MGFC is expected to be a major vehicle for effective and strategic financing of climate change mitigation policies and projects, resulting in improved energy efficiency of households and businesses.

The MGFC's main beneficiaries are households, especially women-headed households, living in peri-urban (or semi-urban) areas. The MGFC will support the adoption of green and energy efficient practices, as well as Mongolia’s major commercial banks that will act as participating financial institutions to provide financing for the implementation of the MGFC’s climate change mitigation priority programs. The initial target markets of the MGFC green credit products focuses on: (i) the reduction of GHG emissions through the promotion of alternative heating and energy efficient insulation materials, (ii) the adoption of energy efficiency measures in large industries, and (iii) green and affordable housing mortgage.

The following outcomes are expected through the operation of the MGFC:
- Promotion of innovative and effective institutional financing for low-carbon, resource-efficient technologies and livelihoods;
- Contribution to GoM objectives for its national climate development commitments; and
- Development of institutional and human capacity for a paradigm shift towards mitigation of climate change.

In sum, the MGFC is expected to support the GoM achieve the targets set in its Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) and the National Green Development Policy (NGDP).

### 1.2 Objectives of the Gender Assessment and Action Plan

The key objective of the gender assessment is to provide an overview of the current gender equality situation within the MGFC, as well as to identify areas where the MGFC needs to be aware of and promoting opportunities for enhancing gender equality through its operations. The gender assessment outlines the legal framework in Mongolia that governs gender equality and identifies the key gender issues that needs to be taken into account, especially as they relate to MGFC's operations, products and services.

#2.1 National Gender and Development Policies, Laws and Programs

Gender equality has been one of the primary thrusts of the Government of Mongolia, and Mongolia is a signatory to all major international instruments pertaining to women’s rights and gender equality.

The Mongolian Constitution guarantees that “every person shall be equal before the law and the courts.” Further, it features an explicit ban of discrimination, starting in Article 12: “No one may be discriminated against on account of their nationality, language, race, age, sex, social origin or status, property and assets, employment occupation and official position, religion and conscience, conviction and opinion, and education.” Mongolia was among the first countries to ratify the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW) in July 1981. As such, Mongolian’s relatively strong regulatory framework and international commitments to gender equality provides a solid foundation for mainstreaming gender into development policies and programs.

"The Mongolia's Sustainable Development Vision (MSDV) – 2030" was adopted by the Parliament of Mongolia on 5 February 2016 and it sets out the objectives for ending all forms of poverty (phase I: 2016-2020; phase II: 2021-2030). The MSDV 2030 highlights the importance of ensuring gender equality and promoting inclusive growth as key priorities for enabling sustained social development and achieving the SDGs. Priorities include ensuring equal access to education, health care, employment opportunities, and economic resources for populations in the labour market, creating “a pleasant environment for equal participation in social welfare,” and reducing material and child mortality.

In 1996 a National Program on the Advancement of Women was drawn up, based on consultations with women’s organizations. In 2001 it was replaced as the National Program on Gender Equality (NPGE) 2001-2015, with the aim of “removing barriers to equal participation of men and women in economic and social development” achieving development goals through the active involvement of both men and women, while also at a priority for the government.

In 2011, the Law on the Promotion of Gender Equality (LPGE) was passed. The law promotes gender equality and sets out obligations for government and other key actors in the spheres of politics, employment and labour relations, civil service, education, health care and family relations. However, there has been limited progress towards achieving these goals due to a lack of political will, a lack of financial and human resources, and the absence of a stable, long-term government

2.5 Ger community

Ger inhabitants, characterized by lack of access to basic utilities and most basic services, now cover an area of about 350 km² while, in comparison, the apartment areas cover only about 130 km² in Ulaanbaatar (UB). Out of total 380.6 thousand households living in UB, 57% (216 thousand) live in an area which is not connected to central heating and water supply systems. In contrast, the majority of 216,000 ger area households remain unconnected to central heating supply. During long, harsh winters, the burning of raw coal serves as a primary source of heating for ger dwellers, a practice that contributes to severe air pollution with grave environmental and public health implications.

Air pollution borne diseases including pneumonia and acute lower respiratory infections (AIS) people per year) are more prevalent in ger areas than in apartment areas. Children living in ger areas (10 per year) caused most deaths, with children from ger areas being disproportionately affected. Ger area households have very limited access to finance to upgrade their housing conditions and remain highly vulnerable to the effects of climate change such as flooding, lack of access to piped drinking water, sanitation services and waste management services.

The average income of households in the ger areas is 1.1 times lower than the average household income of Ulaanbaatar city. Ger area households are constrained by the affordability of insulation technologies and heating systems. The majority of ger area households have access to only limited solutions available on the market. Those constrained with income and/or budget use inefficient and environmentally damaging heating technologies throughout the cold season which continues for 8-9 months.

As stated in a World Bank report [1], while both poor women and poor men suffer from lack of access to basic utilities, women are disproportionately affected. As a result of time consuming and physically draining tasks such as fetching water and firewood, women have limited opportunities to engage in other activities that could improve their economic status or optimize household resources. Furthermore, minimal opportunities to improve the labor productivity are low, and the options for social and political interactions outside the household are retracted.

2.6 Rural Areas and Herding

Despite external influences and pressure due to globalization and severe threats like climate change, mobile pastoralism still plays a significant role for the Mongolian society, its culture and people. According to the 2017 census, 40% (953,290) of the country's population are nomadic pastoralists competing with sedentary farmers in the same areas and living in fragile ecosystems and semi-arid landscapes [2]. Due to the importance of nomadism for the Mongolian society, a gender analysis focusing on pastoral households is essential.

A study published by the SDC in 2015, conducted interviews with 366 persons from different herding households. The results are showing that contribution and roles of women do appear not to be adequately recognized in other five aspects for herding households like in terms of decision-making involved in livestock management, income generation, household food security, child care and education.

The daily workload of women in the country is significantly higher than men's for the entire year. The average time spent by women on domestic chores such as cooking, cleaning and childcare is 10 hours per day. Furthermore, in stark contrast to assets owned by women living in urban areas, family properties or animals are usually owned by their husbands or other male members of the household. Thus, this imbalance may negatively impact women's bargaining power and vulnerability in their human and communities.

# 2.7 Leadership and Decision-Making

In 2011, Mongolia established binding quotas of 20% for women candidates by party or coalition for the Parliament elections[1]. Despite this measure, only 13 of the 76 members of the current parliament are women (17%). Women hold just over one-third of ministerial positions in the judiciary (13 out of 24 justices are women)[2] but remain generally underrepresented in management and decision-making roles across all sectors[3]. According to a World Bank study, women lead around 6% of the top 100 listed firms in Mongolia.

In the civil service, a report by UNDP[4] shows just 26.6% of state secretaries are women, while only 30% of middle managers and 15% of senior managers in Mongolia are women. At the Ministry of Finance and Economy, women hold just 8% of senior positions compared to 79% for men; however, they hold 85% of what their male counterparts make[5]. In the recently released 2016 Global Gender Gap Index (GGGI), Mongolia ranks 83th out of 144 countries, slipping from 42nd place in 2014.

### 2.9 Issues facing Men

There are a number of concerns associated with the deteriorating health condition of men given their increasingly high morbidity and mortality rates. ## Other Vulnerable Groups

### People with Disabilities

According to recent data on disability in Mongolia, there are 103,600 people with disabilities in Mongolia (Ministry of Labor and Social Protection, & JICA, 2017, p.8). Among this population, 55.3% were men and 44.7% were women, while 42.9% were genetic disabilities and 57.3% are categorized as acquired disabilities. Roughly one third of these disabled persons are living in Chabuus (rural areas) where they face additional challenges due to the lack of infrastructure that makes their daily lives harder and lead to social exclusion (Ministry of Labor and Social Protection, & JICA, 2017, p.23). The main obstacles challenging the lives of disabled people are in legislation and policy, social protection, and access to services and facilities. These challenges are exacerbated by physical and cultural obstacles.

In terms of legislation and legal frameworks on disability, the country's legislation lacks age and gender that are aimed at meeting the situation for disabled persons in the country. Although the Mongolian government has made significant progress in recent years, research indicates that the implementation of these new frameworks, laws and legislation proceeds very slowly. Lack of political will adds to the very slow and timely implementation process (ADP et al., 2013, p.9). Thus, due to the lack of legal protection, disabled people are often excluded from social life.

Additionally, Mongolian architecture and infrastructure is very disability-unfriendly. Key infrastructure like public transport remains very difficult for disabled persons to access. Furthermore, cultural obstacles exacerbate issues for disabled Ger district residents. The entrance to a ger, the most ubiquitous housing type in Mongolia, is usually at ground level, making it impossible for those with wheelchairs or other limited mobility devices. Due to the traditional cultural meaning of the threshold of a ger, it is hard to find alternate entrance types, and often their only option is to use stairs (Ministry of Labor and Social Protection, & JICA, 2017; Ministry of Education et al., 2015, pp.3-4)). However, there are some local NGO working on solutions to this issue, including building wooden bridge add-ons that can ease the access for wheelchair users.

Elderly

One of the most crucial challenges for elderly people in Mongolia are insufficient social welfare programs. The Mongolian civil welfare funds currently serve over 80,000 people per year (roughly 25% of the total population[1]), and this percentage is increasing as life spans lengthen. However, these programs are not well established enough to adequately meet the needs of a growing elderly population. This situation is further complicated by the lack of awareness among Mongolians about Mongolian legislation and policies. Thus, many elderly people rely on their children to take care of them. Consequently, there is increasing pressure on young families to take care of the elderly generation. In urban areas, where the majority of the population lives, this burden falls primarily upon women who have a disproportionate burden of taking care of children, grandparental care, and finances. In rural areas, often inhabited by older generations, the situation is even more challenging.

Youth

The main issue faced by Mongolian youth is the deep inequality between children of high and low socioeconomic backgrounds. In Ulaanbaatar, there is a stark divide between outcomes for children raised in the poor districts versus the rest of the city. These divides lead to persistent inequality in terms of life expectancy, education, employment opportunities, and access to healthcare services. For instance, youth unemployment is 2.3 times higher than the national average of 8.8%, with youth from low socioeconomic backgrounds being disproportionately affected. Children aged 15-19 are especially hard hit, experiencing the highest unemployment rates with 18.7%.

Therefore, it remains a top priority for the government to promote a socially inclusive development agenda that helps the Mongolian youth to engage on an equal basis, regardless of health and education status (Government of Mongolia & Prime Minister of Mongolia, 2019, p.227).

There is also a significant divide between urban and rural areas in terms of access to services and educational opportunities for adolescents. The quality of educational systems is often a term of curricula facilities offered to the youth across the country varies significantly from one district to another. Much of the learning facilities provided in very remote areas lack sanitation systems and any kind of advanced teaching equipment (Mongolian Ministry of Education, 2019). Furthermore, many of these facilities are not updated or maintained due to a lack of resources. The majority of these educational programmes date back to the 1930s during Mongolian socialism. Furthermore, there is a chronic lack of health programmes, specifically in terms of mental health for young children (ADB et al., 2019, p.57).
