1. The Programme "Greening Financial Systems: Delivering Climate Finance for All" (the “GFIS” or the “Programme”), in its first Phase, will directly support 307 thousand and beneficiaries to reduce vulnerabilities to the impacts of climate change and accelerate transition towards low-carbon development pathways ("the target countries"): Armenia, Egypt, Jordan, Georgia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyz Republic, Moldova, Mongolia, Morocco, North Macedonia, Serbia, Tajikistan, and Uzbekistan.

2. The Programme targets financial institutions (FIs) and financial market stakeholders as its main beneficiaries. It will also support the development of national strategies for this programme which include especially underserved groups, including the micro and small-medium sized enterprises (MSMEs) that are the backbone of emerging economies, women entrepreneurs, and women in rural areas who are at the real frontlines of climate change, and women alongside midcaps that are drivers of employment and climate impact in emerging markets.

3. In addition to delivering USD 620 million in green finance and 12.8 million tonnes in reduced CO2 equivalent emissions directly, the Programme represents a significant step up in ambition and enhancing the financial sector's resilience: it puts forward a unique catalytic approach that will help countries transition towards Paris-aligned pathways, ultimately catalysing the target countries' financial sectors’ alignment with the Paris Agreement’s long-term goals and reducing exposure to climate shocks. This will unlock tens of billions of dollars in climate finance to benefit millions of businesses and households, and initiate a shift in thinking in the financial sector that will help them better manage their risks against the imminent impact of physical and transition-related risks.

4. Partnership with the GCF on this Programme offers a vital opportunity to impact and leverage additional resources for the target countries, in line with the GCF’s Strategic Plan 2024-2027 and Private Sector Strategy, as well as aligning with targeted countries currently under-represented in climate finance support programming.

5. Without systemic transformation of the financial sector, the world will not meet the objectives of the Paris Agreement. Although currently available technologies and solutions are adequate to achieve this objective, they need to be scaled up significantly for their full potential to be realised for beneficiaries on the ground if the private sector is willing and able to substantially accelerate and scale up climate investments. On the other hand, climate-related risks present additional challenges that require a more comprehensive approach in order to understand how to mitigate the impact of these risks on their assets. In light of the recent state of climate regulations and the need for financial institutions to be better prepared to manage climate risk, support partner FIs (“Partners”) to proactively become first-movers to adopt Paris-aligned transitions and enhance their resilience against vulnerabilities resulting from climate-related risks.

6. Aligning banking practices with climate transition goals necessitates banks acquiring the necessary skills and knowledge to effectively manage these risks. However, many Banks have numerous business and regulatory priorities, and most do not have the capacity to also plan how to smoothly transition their portfolios towards the acceleration of low greenhouse gas emissions pathways.

7. Without transition planning, banks will be unprepared to manage climate-related risks within their portfolios. This could lead to a more risk-averse approach where banks reduce their exposure to sectors deemed high-risk in terms of climate impact, potentially leaving those sectors without the financial support they need.

8. Unfortunately, these high-risk sectors are often the ones most in need of investment to adopt more sustainable technologies and practices. This highlights the critical role of banks to help avoid a disorderly transition that could disproportionately affect vital sectors of the economy and established livelihoods.

9. Given that transition planning is not expected to become mandatory any time soon, incentivising the prioritisation of transition planning can help banks acquire the necessary knowledge and expertise to manage climate-related risks. This also accelerates the shift towards low greenhouse gas emissions and climate-resilient development.

10. The Programme adopts a multi-pronged approach which aims to considerably scale climate finance across the three countries and promote FII transition planning as well as ambitious climate action plans. The Programme will work in close collaboration with PFIs established by the GCF-EBRD Green Economy Financing Facilities (“GCF-EFFs”) in the nine overlapping countries, identifying complementarities with existing PFIs to scale the climate finance landscape in the three target countries. In addition to leveraging on the successful momentum under the GCF EFF engagements, while also establishing opportunities with new PFIs and other financial institutions, the Programme will support PFIs that are nascent in their climate financing journeys, will entail some overlapping activities with the GCF EFFs, and will provide a platform for PFIs to share lessons learned. The Programme offers opportunities to replicate the GCF EFF success with new PFIs and across the nine additional five countries, as well as ensuring continuity and maintaining the momentum with existing PFIs.

11. These overlapping activities are considered to be stack building blocks for climate finance and will help to scale up climate finance in the three target countries by supporting their climate finance journey and will require identifying sub-loan opportunities for PFIs, reducing their perceived risk and establishing their commercial viability, supporting PFIs to see the climate finance landscape as a whole. The Programme will also support PFIs to develop climate finance plans that will build on the GCF EFF and target existing or more advanced PFIs within their climate financing journey. These PFIs will be supported in developing their climate finance plans to assess the impact of climate risks on their assets and shift their financing flows towards AEB-aligned projects, supporting PFIs to identify opportunities within the larger realm of climate finance opportunities (i.e., non-climate).

14. When it comes to financial regulators, there is, indeed, a widespread perception that the risks associated with climate change can affect the stability of the financial system and the macroeconomy. 15. They identified a trend of closer collaboration with central banks and other regulators to better understand and manage climate-related risk, if necessary, by reducing tenors and increasing collateral requirements, among other responses.

B.1. Climate context (max. 1000 words, approximately two pages)

Global climate context

22. The world is set to get hotter by 2-3 °C even if countries deliver their current, unconditional nationally determined contributions (UNEP Emissions Gap Report, “NDC scenarios in NGFS climate scenarios). Continued emissions of greenhouse gases have already led to about 1.1°C of global temperature rise since the industrial revolution and is projected to reach 1.5°C by mid-century. The heat will be felt unevenly across various types of physical climate risks, and those in the emerging economies like the least developed countries are particularly vulnerable in the absence of adequate access to technologies and finance and knowledge, most notably acutely underserved groups such as smallholder farmers, medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), women, households, farmers and those that are based outside of main cities.

23. The world can get back on track to 1.5°C pathways with currently available technologies and solutions only if the private sector globally accelerates and scales up climate investment; adding to limited public finance available for climate action. In this context, it is critical that governments, development banks, and other financial sectors play a key role in enabling the urgent and system-wide transformation required to achieve a Paris Agreement-aligned pathway. This will require significant investments across all sectors of the economy, including a transition across all sectors of the economy, with substantial financing needs for such transition – for example, decarbonization of energy supply, replacing fossil fuels with clean renewable energy, increasing energy efficiency, and introduction of technologies and practices to tackle climate change impacts on agriculture. Business and consumer preferences and higher emission risks locally can pose significant transition risks to those in developing economies that are not adequately prepared or have limited access to the financial sector in the absence of commensurate local regulatory requirements, policies and guidance.

25. Most leading financial institutions in advanced countries have set net zero targets and are publishing plans on how they aim to meet these targets. These plans, often referred to as transition plans, set out the steps through which a financial institution will reduce its greenhouse gas emissions over time and identify opportunities. For many banks, this includes setting near-term emissions targets for GHG-emitting sectors2, such as coal-fired power generation or thermal coal mining3, and identifying how they plan to manage climate-related risks in their sectors to enhance their resilience. On the other hand, financial regulators have a key role to play in ensuring the resilience of the financial sector against the impact of physical and transition climate risks. This can be achieved by requiring financial institutions to identify and disclose their exposure to unsustainable finance practices through the development of guidelines, corporate governance, taxonomies and regulatory requirements among others, as well as to ensure the financial sector’s alignment with the countries’ NDCs and LTSS.

26. Transition planning has gained the interest of regulators and is supported by leading international initiatives on climate change for financial institutions. Standards from the International Sustainability Board (ISSB) and the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) provide guidance on transition planning. Financial institutions in emerging economies however show low adoption of climate target setting, transition planning and climate risk management practices due to less stringent regulatory requirements, limited access to international best practice guidelines and a lack of understanding of how to apply these standards to their market needs. For example, the EBRD’s survey Readiness of the Financial Sector for the Impacts of Climate Change in Central Asia (2019) found that 85% of respondents were not aware of any climate risk management or transition planning guidance and only 37% had yet to dip their toe sufficiently into the knowledge offered by international best practice initiatives. 90% of respondents indicated they are focusing on climate change relevant to their operations, but few have a clear view of how to operationalise climate risk management in their operations. The EBRD’s survey also found that only 25% of respondents in these countries stated there was no specific guidance or framework for transition planning and climate risk management. Furthermore, 80% of respondents in these countries did not anyhow take into account the impacts of climate change in their operations and business strategies. This suggests that many financial institutions in emerging economies have yet to make their climate risk management robust enough to not require improvements within the coming two years. And when asked what the main obstacles to improving climate risk management were, 65% mentioned the lack of regulatory guidance or framework for transition planning and climate risk management, while only 10% cited a lack of resources and management practices; only 10% of respondents already do at least some disclosure in the Central Asian region. The remaining 25% of respondents who are not yet disclosing their exposure to unsustainable finance practices are either zero, and voluntary and resource-consuming. The ability of an FIs to embark on non-mandatory transition planning requires a significant amount of in-house resources to be prioritised above other business needs and regulatory requirements. This is particularly challenging for financial institutions with limited capacity and where demand is low. Given this absence of regulatory guidance, coupled with lacking capacities and knowledge among FIs, transition planning is at nascent stages in emerging markets (including the targeted 13 countries).

27. Aligning banking practices with climate transition goals necessitates banks acquiring the necessary knowledge and governance policies to better manage climate-related risks. Banks have numerous business and regulatory priorities, and most do not have the capacity to also map how to smoothly transition their portfolios towards the acceleration of low greenhouse gas emissions and climate-resilient development. Without transition plans, banks may be forced into a reactive mode, which could lead them to adopt an overly conservative or adverse approach to high-risk sectors, potentially leaving those sectors without the critical financial support needed for their transformation.

28. As the market demand studies in Annex 2 illustrate, there are already viable technical and behavioral changes for climate adaptation and mitigation across priority sectors specified in NDCs. But available climate mitigation and adaptation technologies and solutions are out of reach for many in emerging markets due to high costs. Many adaptation solutions identified are not commercially viable at present and hence face systemic barriers. In addition, the transition risks associated with these solutions are often underestimated despite their significant long-term benefits due to their higher upfront costs compared to current prevailing market practices. The lack of awareness among financial institutions about climate change-related risks and vulnerabilities in particular sectors can also limit awareness of their own exposure to physical climate and transition risks and viable solutions, which in turn can also affect the stability of financial systems and the economy as a whole.

## Country regulatory environment overview

The target countries are progressing towards developing sustainable financial systems, albeit at different stages and with varying approaches as outlined in brief below and in more detail in Annex 2. Common challenges include the need for capacity building, standardizing methodologies for evaluating green projects, and integrating green finance into existing financial regulations. The countries that have already developed frameworks, such as Kyrgyz Republic and Mongolia, are better positioned to overcome these challenges compared to those still developing their frameworks or without a clear body providing centralized leadership in this area. Given the emerging market context of these countries, there is also a need for capacity building on green finance concepts and tools. This includes a lack of unified approach and a comprehensive framework for promoting green financing. Moreover, where regulatory frameworks exist, they are often fragmented and not aligned with international standards. The GCFs to meet such disclosure requirements, such as limited technical expertise, a large financing gap, and a lack of a common methodology and reporting.

### Kazakhstan

Kazakhstan is in the development regulatory framework for sustainable finance, aligned with the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and the Paris Agreement. Key institutions include the National Bank of Kazakhstan and the Ministry of Finance of the Republic of Kazakhstan. The country has established the Financial Market (AFRM), and the Association of Financial Centers (AFC). Green Finance Centre (GFC).

### Kyrgyz Republic

The Kyrgyz Republic is in the nascent stages of a developing sustainable finance framework, with recent developments including the adoption of a green projects taxonomy and a sustainable finance center in Bishkek.

### Mongolia

Mongolia has established a robust basis for green finance, driven by the Mongolian Banks Association and the Sustainable Finance Association (SFA). Key initiatives include the Sustainable Finance Principles (SFPs) and the Green Bond Framework. The country's favorable regulatory environment, green loans remain a small percentage of total loans, highlighting the need for further growth.

### Uzbekistan

Uzbekistan’s sustainable finance framework is emerging, with the Ministry of Economic Development and the Central Bank of Uzbekistan playing a significant role in developing ESG factors and developing green financial instruments. The country has taken concrete steps such as issuing green bonds but are still in the early stages.

### Tajikistan

Tajikistan is at the preliminary stage of integrating sustainable finance. Efforts include developing a green finance strategy and collaborating with international partners. However, a lack of infrastructure and financial resources poses significant challenges.

### Armenia

Armenia's progress is in its early stages, primarily through the Central Bank of Armenia’s initiatives. The country has issued green bonds but developed frameworks for ESG integration, though challenges remain in scaling these efforts.

### Georgia

Georgia's approach to green finance includes the adoption of green bonds and the integration of ESG factors into financial regulations. The National Bank of Georgia plays a crucial role in these initiatives, aiming to enhance the financial sector’s resilience to climate risks.

### Moldova

Moldova is in the early stages of developing its finance development efforts. Efforts include integrating ESG factors into financial regulations and working with international partners to enhance sustainable finance practices.

### North Macedonia

North Macedonia has taken steps to align with regional and international environmental strategies, such as the Western Balkans Green Agenda. The National Bank has implemented measures to encourage green financing through the issuance of green bonds.

## Serbia
Serbia has made recent advancements in sustainable finance, particularly with the adoption of green bonds and the integration of ESG criteria into its financial sector. The National Bank of Serbia and the Ministry of Finance have been key players in these developments. Initiatives include issuing the first green bond in 2021 aimed at financing renewable energy projects, developing a national green finance strategy to encourage sustainable development, and promoting Green Bond Principles (GBP) and Sustainability-Linked Bonds (SLB). Despite these efforts, Serbia faces challenges in implementing a comprehensive green taxonomy and increasing public awareness.

## Jordan
Jordan has taken significant steps towards integrating sustainable finance into its national agenda, supported by institutions like the Central Bank of Jordan and the Ministry of Environment. Key initiatives include establishing a Green Bond Framework, developing guidelines for green projects, promoting waste management, collaborating with international organizations to develop green bonds and sustainability-linked bonds (SLB), and enhancing capacity building for sustainable finance. Challenges in Jordan include the need for greater regulatory support and the development of a comprehensive green taxonomy to classify eligible green projects.

## Morocco
Morocco has made substantial progress in sustainable finance, focusing on energy efficiency and renewable energy projects. Public institutions and development finance institutions (DFIs) play a crucial role in providing green finance options, although there is a need to expand these to include other green projects.

## Egypt
Egypt has taken significant steps towards sustainable development strategies. The Egyptian Financial Regulatory Authority (FRA) has been instrumental in this progress, establishing guidelines and frameworks to support green finance. Key initiatives include developing a Green Bond Framework, promoting ESG disclosure among publicly listed companies, and enhancing capacity building for sustainable finance. Challenges in scaling these initiatives but benefits from strong regulatory support and international collaboration.

67. The Programme, through its transformative approach to greening the FY system, will contribute to the achievement of NDC mitigation and adaptation goals in the targeted countries. The introduction of climate-related financial disclosures by FIs in developing countries has been delayed compared with developed markets (by the EBRD Survey) by far the most important external factor motivating FIs to strengthen their climate risk management[1]. - The lack of regulatory environment is a major obstacle to the greening of the financial systems;

# D. Risk Assessment and Management

## F.1. Risk factors and mitigations measures

### Selected Risk Factor 1: Political risk

| Category | Probability | Impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Other | Medium | Medium |

#### Description

There is potential political instability at the macro-level in many of target countries as well as localized political risks to the Programme. Specifically, some of the targeted countries in the Neighbourhood East have significant geo-political uncertainties stemming from the ongoing conflict in Ukraine, in particular Moldova, which has been a focal point for the conflict and is currently experiencing an influx of refugees. This results in high levels of displaced people, thereby intensifying fiscal pressures and negatively impacting growth and infrastructure development. The political instability could also lead to delays in the implementation of the Programme, disruption to countries' governance, banking system and other implications of the conflict on the country's economy. Such risks are considered significant for all countries that have received loans from EBRD borrowers and therefore lower deployment of the Programme in the affected country. This has been considered a risk for many countries in the Neighbourhood East region including Moldova, Ukraine and Georgia (e.g., Jordan) and is considered Medium. Furthermore, this could lead to potential legal and administrative restrictions that may be imposed on the implementation and financing conditions for the private sector.

### Mitigation Measures

Political risks vary considerably between countries and the Programme contexts. The EBRD is primarily driven by sound banking principles, with thorough due diligence processes covering angles related to political, integrity and policy risks. The EBRD has local presence and trusted relationships and maintains a close working relationship with governments in the Neighbourhood East region. The EBRD would also build on its long relationships and policy support activities with the governments of the countries it finances. It is important that the GCF supports the EBRD in maintaining these relationships, as the Programme can be a crucial tool in enabling financing even in crisis situations where sound banking principles may not apply. This will help to ensure that the risk of political instability is reflected in higher financing premiums. The GCF's support through the Programme will be an important risk mitigation measure for EBRD and its clients. In addition, the GCF can also contribute to the overall cost of the Programme by providing a lower interest rate and then margins should be structured to improve. The Programme also includes a diverse set of countries across three continents, thereby diversifying this risk; hence the residual impact of this risk is considered Medium.

# G.2 Gender assessment and action plan

159. Gender inequalities in social systems and access persist across the thirteen countries targeted by the EBRD. These are especially prevalent in the context of women's unequal access to finance and in particular climate finance. Significant gaps also are prevalent with regards to women’s access to green skills and opportunities for employment, as well as their participation in the sector (horizontal segregation), as well as in related managerial roles (vertical segregation).

The EBRD Strategy for the Promotion of Gender Equality (2017-2023) has set ambitious organizational-level targets with respect to women’s representation at all levels and across all functions, including gender-focused activities within the Programme and sub-projects. This would include supporting financial institutions in their efforts to promote gender equality as well as ensuring that women are aware of the benefits of investing in climate technologies both for commercial and residential purposes. ### Background and Context

The Green Climate Fund (GCF) is committed to supporting countries in their efforts to address climate change, with a focus on adaptation and mitigation. This document outlines the proposed program submitted by the GCF, which aims to facilitate and support investments for climate adaptation and mitigation projects.

(a) **Component 1 - Greening Financial Systems:** This component focuses on equipping FIs in the target region with tools and knowledge to adopt and implement robust, sustainable climate finance strategies. (b) **Component 2 - Expanding Access to Climate Finance:** This component focuses on increasing access to climate finance across multiple sectors in the target region. The AE will offer concessional credit lines to FIs, designed to mobilize and expand access to climate finance.

## Gender Action Plan

Climate change risks are projected to disproportionately affect women due to entrenched gender inequalities in asset ownership and access to information, jobs, finance, and markets. To tackle these persistent gender inequities, EBRD proposes to integrate gender activities into the Project Design Stage (PDS) of all projects.

The proposed Gender Action Plan builds on the EBRD’s unique private-sector approach and experience working with the GCF on previous GCF-EBRD programmes such as the Green Cities Facility (FG25), the Green Cities Fund, and the Green Economic Financing Facilities programme (“GEFF Programme”, Funded Activity FG26).

In line with EBRD’s Gender SMART methodology™, gender activities will be integrated into investments through three key actions: (1) introducing gender considerations early in project design stages; (2) creating a standardized and systematic approach to mainstreaming gender in projects; and (3) reflecting appropriate incentives attached to gender mainstreaming into investments.

### Activities enabling climate-compatible financial systems and benefiting end beneficiaries

Among the main demand-side barriers for women to access climate finance is limited awareness of the benefits of adaptation and mitigation measures for their homes and businesses, limited ability to present business cases, and lack of collateral. To address these issues, EBRD will support the Green Economic Financing Facilities Programme, building on the experience under the GEFF Programme. EBRD will support PFIs in the development of a gender-sensitive financial inclusion strategy and capacity-building for PFIs to provide quality banking to end beneficiaries. This is aimed at closing the gender skills and information gap to enhance the female end beneficiary’s resilience to specific impacts of climate change.

### An investment approach to mainstreaming gender into private capital

EBRD aims to create climate-responsive PFIs that are also gender responsive, and thereby deliver sustained transformation of the financial sector. Under this Programme, the EBRD will support women's increased access to finance through PFIs though concessional financing, to ensure women are well represented among the sub-borrowers.

The EBRD’s approach to promoting gender equality into the programme is aligned with the strategic goals of the Green Climate Fund’s updated Gender Policy and Action Plan and with the EBRD’s Strategy for the Private Sector in the Context of Climate Change. The proposed activities will be integrated across all projects that benefit women and men. The gender activities specified under this programme will be overseen and led by the EBRD's dedicated expert team working on Gender & Economic Inclusion. The EBRD will also work with the GCF to ensure alignment between the two programmes, including through mainstreaming gender into the GEFF and other climate focused programmes over the past 5 years. Building on this experience, the EBRD will support the GCF in developing a gender action plan for the implementation and monitoring of the GAP across the 13 GFS programme countries. Gaps may arise due to the lack of dedicated gender expertise at the country level; the EBRD can be addressed by applying for additional donor funds from other resources.

### References

EBRD’s Gender SMART process represents a standardized approach to systematically address gender gaps in investments. As outlined in its Briefing on the Promotion of Gender Equality (GEQ), it is a support banking teams to: (1) Introduce gender considerations early in the project design stages; (2) Create a standardized and systematic approach to mainstreaming gender in projects, and (3) Reflect appropriate incentives attached to gender mainstreaming into investments.
