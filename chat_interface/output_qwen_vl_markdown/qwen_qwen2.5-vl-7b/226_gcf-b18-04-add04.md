### Narrative

Given the project's objective of public goods development, an economic cost benefit analysis was conducted to evaluate its financial viability from the perspective of the state in both the project timeframe (four years) and longer (ten year) terms. Details of the analysis are summarized below:

#### Cost structure
The cost structure for the first four years is exclusively derived from the budgeted costs of the project which include all direct and indirect costs. From year five onwards, a steady state in the unit investment and ongoing costs is assumed with annual change by the projected inflation rate. The sources of fund for this period are assumed to be derived from both GoG investments in their safety nets as well as from the project beneficiaries.

#### Project benefits
In line with the theory of change (Annex 10) of the project, all benefits are assumed to occur at the beneficiary household level through the means of increased income generation and asset base creation. Risk reduction activities include the provision of insurance against crop failure and livestock mortality. The project aims to increase agricultural yield of main crops such as rice, beans, millet, groundnuts, maize and sorghum and vegetables. In drought years, the project will provide access to irrigation water through the use of improved irrigation systems (IIS) and the prudent risk taking tool enables participants to make the necessary investments for increased agriculture yield while the risk reduction activities are provided by the project. The project also aims at increasing the income generating assets (IGA). A secondary benefit of the project occurs through an increase in the household asset base, which is mainly in the form of livestock. Taking a very conservative approach, benefits per unit are assumed to remain constant throughout the life of the investment. It should be noted that the model does not consider any generated benefits beyond year five and a lower bound of the total benefits, as the true value of such as insurance becomes tangible only during adverse climate years when the project is in operation.

#### Viability
Assuming a standard discount rate of ten percent, the project has a positive net present value across the projected period. A seven-year internal rate of return of seventeen percent is generated which is higher than the discount rate, thus making the project economically viable. The project results cumulative net positive spill over in the seventh year of its operation.

# FINANCING / COST INFORMATION

## GREEN CLIMATE FUND FUNDING PROPOSAL | PAGE 6 OF B

A grant-financing instrument is used for this project with WFP seeking maximum concessionality to undertake the proposed adaptation investments. Without grant resources, the proposed interventions would not be financially sustainable.

- First, as a Least Developed Country and a Low Income Economy, there is limited capacity in the country for concessional debt financing for its adaptation investments.
- Second, the project targets highly vulnerable, food insecure rural populations, more than half of whom are women, living in remote areas with poor access to financial services. The majority of these people live below the poverty line. This segment of the population is significantly cash constrained and therefore not yet interesting for more commercial initiatives (such as commercial insurance).
- Finally, the public good nature of the solution to address the current deficiencies in integrated risk management approaches means that there will be zero cost recovery from the proposed measures to save lives and livelihoods of vulnerable populations in the country.

The project includes the set up and maintenance of a weather index insurance scheme as well as the provision of climate services and climate disaster risk reduction (DRR) assets. As such it is very demanding in terms of human resources, technical expertise and financial resources. The implementation of this project will require significant institutional capacity building to overcome several barriers that constrain Senegal's ability to scale up the use of climate risk management.

These barriers include limited availability of financial, technical and human capacity for insurance index design, implementation and monitoring; lack of awareness among farmers about the benefits of insurance products; inability to reach the desired volume of participants; lack of access to financial markets and awareness of use of micro insurance, especially in rural areas where there is a high level of illiteracy. The project will address these barriers by increasing accessibility to early warnings and low capacity at community level to prepare for and respond to climate-related disasters.

Current financing gaps in domestic financing are hampering Senegal's ability to implement adaptation measures and exacerbating the vulnerability of its population to the adverse effects of climate change, including extreme weather events and climate-related disasters. The grant resources will help remove barriers to support investments that due to the public good nature of the project, do not entail revenue generation and therefore prevent private investments. In addition to this, the project will provide a platform for the development of an insurance index design and implementation framework which will allow implementation and institutional capacity building to be cost-effective. Strengthening of local public and private communication and information systems along with WFP's contribution to creating rural financial markets, may incentivize future investments.

In terms of financial viability, the objective of the project is primarily to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of the Government's climate risk management strategy by providing a more effective and efficient insurance index design. At the same time we are gradually reducing the costs of the program and hence the need of grant financing in the future:

a. Developing insurance products that are more cost-effective
b. Using more cost-effective insurance distribution mechanisms
c. Testing conditional transfer mechanisms for insurance (CTA) to create a market for low income farmers
d. Bundling insurance with a range of complementary services (including climate services)
e. Increasing the number of clients to reduce unit costs

It should be noted that the use of insurance products does not generate income WFP pays premiums on behalf of participants and partners, and is therefore not an alternative source of revenue for WFP. The project will also provide a platform for DMIAS and partners to explore other forms of financing such as micro-insurance and catastrophe risk transfer (CRT) mechanisms. CRT is currently the only entity (private or public) allowed to provide crop insurance in the country – hence the access to the crop insurance market and available alternatives is rather limited

# C.1 Strategic Context

Please describe relevant national, sub-national, regional, global, political, and/or economic factors that help to contextualize the proposal, including existing national and sector policies and strategies.

## Senegal's Climate Vulnerability

### Senegal is highly vulnerable to shocks associated with climate change.
Recent years have been marked by erratic rainfall patterns, increased frequency of extreme weather events such as floods, droughts, desertification, land degradation, salinization in agricultural soils, and destruction of critical infrastructure. About 34% of Senegal's territory is affected by desertification, and the country has experienced a significant increase in the number of people displaced due to climate change.

### Key Vulnerable Regions
The most vulnerable regions include the eastern part of the national territory. Moreover, due to their forest cover, the regions of Tambacounda, Ziguinchor and Kolda - the main provinces of charcoal for other regions of Senegal - are at high risk of deforestation and conservation challenges.

### Economic Dependence
The country's economy is heavily dependent on climate-sensitive sectors including agriculture, fishing, tourism, which represent over 60% of employment. The majority of Senegalese people’s livelihoods are highly climate-sensitive: subsistence agriculture, cash crops (e.g., cotton), livestock farming (especially cattle breeding and growing), and dry agricultural land are the main sources of income.

### Proportion of Income from Climate-Sensitive Sources
The proportion of income that comes from these climate-sensitive sources is highest in the central and eastern parts of the country. These regions are also most vulnerable to climate change impacts.

## The Negative Impacts of Climate Change

### Increased Climate Variability
A climate risk analysis carried out by WFP and partners in 2014 illustrated how increased climate variability and more erratic rainfall patterns across the country have led to reduced agricultural productivity, lower crop yields, and a reduction in livestock numbers. This has resulted in reduced availability of agricultural land.

### Crop Droughts
A 2009 report by the World Bank also associates crop dries to droughts, suggesting the correlation between adverse climatic events and yield losses.

### Food Security Challenges
Recent climate-related events such as the 2011/2012 drought highlighted the impacts of these shocks on food production, access to markets, and livelihoods leading to increased vulnerability and food insecurity. By compromising agricultural productivity and diversification, these events pose a major threat to achieving food security and other crucial Sustainable Development Goals.

### Climate Shocks in Recent Decades
Climate shocks in recent decades, such as droughts in 2002, 2006, 2007, and 2011 and flooding in 2009, have severely increased the vulnerability of the households and negatively impacted economic growth. The food crisis of 2012 caused by a combination of climate shocks (droughts) and poor agricultural management practices in regions such as Ziguinchor, Kolda, Matam, Tambacounda and Kidugou. Households have had to resort to negative coping strategies such as distress sales of livestock or crops, migration from rural areas to urban centers, and food aid.

### Frequency of Poor Growing Seasons
The frequency of poor growing seasons (measured in terms of number of years out of the last 10 years), variation in the start of the rainy season (measured in terms of regularity of the start of the season), and dry spell length (measured in terms of conditions during the rainy season) have all increased over time.

### Climate Variability
Climate variability, particularly variability in precipitation, has a strong relationship with crop yields. About 50% of variations in the total production of groundnut, millet, sorghum, rice, and maize is explained by interannual rainfall variability. The remaining 50% can be attributed to other factors such as soil fertility, land management practices, and differences in farm inputs, irrigation techniques, and economics changes influencing agricultural management techniques.

### Yield Variability

## C2: Project / Programme Objective against Baseline

Describe the baseline scenario (i.e., emissions baseline, climate vulnerability baseline, key barriers, challenges and/or policies) and the outcomes that this project/programme will aim to achieve in improving the baseline scenario.

### 1. Project overarching objectives

The proposed project aims at improving the food and income security of 405,000 people from vulnerable smallholder households, including female-headed household/girls in the regions of Kaffrine, Kindia, Tambacounda, and Ziguinchor, through increasing their adaptive capacity to climate change impacts, build resilience and adapt to the adverse impacts of climate change. The outcomes to reach this overarching result will be the following:

- **Outcome 1:** Increase adaptive capacity of food insecure smallholder farmers and their families by increasing the resilience of their environment and reducing the risk and impacts deriving from climate change.
- **Outcome 2:** Protect food insecure smallholder farmers from the impact of covariate climate shocks**[1]** and provide them with the confidence to invest in agricultural inputs and diversified Income Generating Activities (IGAs).
- **Outcome 3:** Increase the adaptive capacity of food insecure smallholders by gradually building savings, while improving their ability to produce and sell surplus, and diversifying their income sources through investments in IGAs that are not climate sensitive.
- **Outcome 4:** Enable the Government of Senegal (GoS) to mainstream climate change adaptation and climate risk management into its safety net and social protection programs.

The baseline scenario for the proposed project builds on the national level analysis carried out by WFP in 2012 to support the design of the pilot phase of the RG Rural Resilience Initiative (Annex 12 and 13). The effects of the pilot on the baseline scenario are described below. Please see Annex C.

## Biophysical and environmental baseline

Climate change is characterized by high rainfall variability at seasonal, inter-annual and multi-decadal scales, and is one of key drivers of food insecurity in Senegal. The most recent period (1962–2005) was the driest since 1870, which was linked to persistent drought in the 1970s and the 1980s. Recent observations suggest a reversal of these conditions with higher rainfall variability at seasonal scales and more frequent heavy rain events during the wet season. These trends highlight that rainfall patterns are highly erratic in Senegal as mentioned above, and in the future, more generally, and can result in a more uncertain risk environment in the future.

Gridded station precipitation data show high intra-seasonal and inter-decadal variability in precipitation. There has been a noticeable increase in the frequency of heavy rain events since 1962 but an increase since the mid-1990s. The event period is part and parcel of the late-20th century persistence of drought that affected the entire Sahel belt, and more recent periods have experienced increased rainfall variability at both intra-seasonal and inter-decadal timescales that characterizes the Sahelian rainfall. The high variability makes forecasts and agricultural planning difficult in Senegal. A linear regression test suggests a significant increase in wet season rainfall between 1962 and 2006, with the 500 mm daily threshold regressing across about 100 kilometers (km) to the south.

### Social baseline

Sénégal's population of 15 million (World Bank, 2015) is unevenly distributed, with the majority living in western areas where most major cities are located. The eastern parts of the country, particularly Kédougou, Saraya and Sédhiou, are less densely populated.

### Economic baseline

Sénégal's economy has been growing at an average rate of 5% per year since 2010 (World Bank, 2016). In contrast to its landlocked Sahelian neighbors, Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger, however, rapid population growth in Sénégal is delayed by low fertility rates driven mainly by migration from rural to urban areas. Demographic pressure is a major issue of concern for the country as its population grows faster than agricultural production; the challenges of addressing poverty and food insecurity have increased.

Poverty remains high in Sénégal, affecting the majority of the population. GDP growth is well below the rates necessary for significant reduction in poverty (World Bank, 2016). The lack of access to education and employment in rural sectors limits the creation of new jobs. Repeated shocks in recent years have further hampered progress, with poverty incidence increasing from 35% in 2009 to an estimated 48% in 2017 (World Bank, 2016). The number of people living below $1.90 per day increased by over 6.3 million in 2011.

Despite some development progress in recent years, Sénégal remains a food insecure country: estimates suggest that one out of every three Senegalese (i.e., 16% of the population) is food insecure, while almost half (47%) of the population just manages to meet food needs. Geographically, food insecurity is very high in the regions of Sédhiou (58%), Kédia (42%), and Kayes (39%). The departments of Kédougou, Ziguinchor, and Kaolack are also highly vulnerable; those in these regions are still suffering from the consequences of the 2012 food crisis and are very vulnerable with eroded resilience.

Additionally, the situation of acute malnutrition is generally precarious and close to an alert threshold with a worrying situation in some departments where the rate exceeds the critical threshold: the departments of Matam (20.5%), Rassene (16.3%) and Kaniâté (17.9%) and the department of Podor (16.7%).

Assessments by WFP have identified the causes of food insecurity in the country to be: (i) low agricultural productivity due to poor access to inputs, high input prices, and high grain prices; and (ii) high rainfall variability at intra-annual and inter-decadal scales, particularly in key production areas. (For detailed info on the relation between climate change and food insecurity see WFP, 2016).

Against the biophysical and social baseline, the project will improve the natural resource base of targeted populations through the provision of climate-resilient agricultural inputs to food insecure smallholder farmers and their families by increasing the resilience of their environment and reducing the risk and impacts deriving from climate change. The project will also address the increase in climate risk through objective 2 (Protect food insecure smallholder farmers from the impact of covariate climate shocks) and provide them with the confidence to invest in one or more other assets, e.g. foods, droughts, hurricanes.

It provides an average temperature for every month and gives parameters for the coming 3-6 months. All farmers receiving the forecast then receive a monthly SMS. This SMS breaks the coming month into 4 weeks and again states how much rain is expected in each week.
- Short-term forecast: Farmers receive a 48 hour weather forecast daily. The forecasts focus on a rain-on approach and is probabilistic, which means that every forecast of rain is followed by a risk assessment (e.g., "Today, high chance of heavy rainfall, with a 70% probability"). This information can be accessed through the Igntisa mobile application or via mobile phone number and GPS location.
- The daily forecast SMS is delivered 7 days per week, during the full rainy season.

### SIC functioning:
Women meet weekly and keep records mostly orally. Groups receive no seed capital. Loans are approved by a majority vote of members present at meetings. Interest rates are determined by group expenditures. Loan terms, conditions and interest rates are defined by each group. A saving cycle lasts between eight months and a year, after which each member collects her savings and her share of the generated interest. Groups schedule their own meetings and decide on loan amounts and interest rates. The SIC groups are formed as women join. As women meet, they learn to manage their group, and develop bonds of mutual assistance and solidarity. These bonds also function to support income generation, agricultural production.


The SIC methodology follows a four step process which includes community studies, training and coaching of field teams in the identification of potential communities, and the formation and training of savings groups. An exploratory study of potential areas is conducted to identify intervention zones and local partners based on the socio-economic and environmental conditions of each zone. The SIC methodology has been piloted in 10 countries. Once the intervention zones and local NGO partners have been determined, field teams are established in each zone. Field teams are typically made up of two or three people who work with a group of communities for an extended period. They conduct both theoretical and practical sessions, and to select and coach replicating agents. Once the trainings are completed, the animators will introduce SIC in the village after having presented the program to the village leaders and secured their permission.


Animators self-select groups of 15-25 members and, in the first six months, visit them weekly and guide them through essential training sessions on savings group management. The animators also collect data from each group upon joining, and the interest rate and conditions of loans are determined. Animators also select and train replicating agents, and collect data from each group at least once a year to monitor group functioning and ensure that they are still core training. In the next six months, animators visit the groups twice a month to ensure they are functioning well and provide assistance to group members during their first share-out. Animators also continue to coach replicating agents to train groups in their communities on SIC principles and to monitor considered成熟。Animators will mature groups quarterly to monitor group functioning and answer any questions.


Based on the need and request from members, groups are also trained in the SIC-programs on citizenship, small business management, and financial literacy.

## Output 3.1 and 3.2 – Saving for Change (SFC) and warrantage

SFC groups are trained to graduate in their first year, typically once they have gone through an entire cycle, including the production phase. The group then continues to operate on its own with limited external support, which is a factor of sustainability. Dropout rates are usually around 2% and mostly due to economic migration.

Animators visit mature groups quarterly to monitor group functioning and answer any questions.

SFC also has a strong organic replication model: When expanding SFC to new villages, animators focus on creating groups in the village where the animator is based. Once these groups have graduated, they become trainers and go on to become a volunteer replicator. These trained replicators then create new groups within their village. Some replicators also move to other villages to train groups there as well.

The SFC model has been designed with the objective of broadening its reach in the communities they work with; groups are brought together in Associations which allow them to become an integral part of the community landscape, and initiate dialogue with authorities and give women a voice within their communities. The Association also provides a platform for women to share experiences.

Based on the need and request from members and on funding availability, groups are further trained in the "SFC" programs on childcare, income generating business training, and agricultural reproduction.

Warrantage is initiated by the group itself with the objective of enabling smallholder farmers which typically do not have access to credit from formal financial institutions, in part because of their lack of collateral. It offers a simple mechanism for groups to harvests in a safe place and use it to collateral to access loans instead of selling soon after harvest at a low price.

For component 2 - Risk Transfer, WFP also uses the best available technology and practices. Conventional crop or livestock insurance relies on direct measurement of the loss or damage suffered by the farmer. However, field loss assessment is normally costly or not feasible, particularly where there are a large number of small-scale farmers or where insurance blocks are underwritten. This is why WFP uses weather-index insurance (WII) for its climate risk management approach.

The essential feature of WII is that the insurance contract responds to an objective parameter (e.g. measurement of rain amount), which can be measured with a high degree of accuracy and at low cost, as opposed to the subjective data used in conventional crop or livestock insurance contracts. The weather-index insurance contract also allows for accurate assessment of the loss suffered by the policyholder based on the same contract and measurement at the same station, eliminating the need for field assessment.

Compared to traditional crop insurance, WII has the following advantages:

- **Transparency:** Index insurance contracts usually allow the policyholder direct access to the information on which the claim is based. This increases transparency in the claims process.
- **No-on-farm loss adjustment:** This is a primary advantage of index insurance as, on-farm loss adjustment is quite costly and time-consuming for both farmers and insurers.
- **Lack of adverse selection:** Adverse selection occurs when potential insured parties have hidden information about their risk profile. In the case of traditional crop or livestock insurance, this can be mitigated by the insurer assessing the risk of the insured. Traditional insurance encourages high-risk producers to insure, while low risk and premium are concentrated on the average producer. Index insurance ensures that all insured farmers in the defined area have the same level of protection regardless of their individual risk profile. Hence, more farmers benefit from reduced adverse selection.
- **Lack of moral hazard:** Moral hazard occurs when individuals engage in hidden activities that increase their exposure to risk as a result of purchasing insurance, or attempt to influence the claims outcome. These hidden activities can be mitigated by index insurance because it is based on objective parameters and predefined rules were established. With WII, there is no benefit in individual producers trying to influence claims. All producers in the defined area are treated equally.
- **Addresses correlated risks:** Index products work best where there are correlated risks. With traditional products, each producer has a unique risk profile which makes it difficult for insurers to pool these risks together. Low operational and transaction costs: Index insurance requires limited individual underwriting (client selection) compared with traditional crop or livestock insurance, resulting in lower cost. Education on the product remains important, both prior to product launch and as an ongoing process.
- **Rapid payout:** Measurement of weather station data, with no field loss adjustment, allows rapid payouts.

### For component 2 - Risk Transfer:

WFP also applies the best available technology and practices. Conventional crop or livestock insurance relies on direct measurement of the loss or damage suffered by the farmer. However, field loss assessment is often difficult to perform in a timely manner after a disaster event has occurred, and weather insurance markets are undeveloped. This is why WFP uses weather-index insurance (WiI) for its climate risk management approach.

The essential feature of WiI is that the insurance contract responds to an objective parameter (e.g., measurement of rainfall or temperature through satellite data) during an agreed time period. The parameters of the contract are set so as to correspond with the farmer’s specific risk exposure, and the insurance premium is calculated based on this exposure within a defined area. Recipients pay premiums based on the same contract and measurement at the same station, eliminating the need for field loss assessment.

Compared to traditional crop insurance, WiI has the following advantages:

- **Transparency:** Index insurance contracts usually allow the policyholder direct access to the information on which the payouts will be calculated. Trust is strengthened by transparency.
- **No-on-farm loss adjustment:** As there are no farm losses to assess, so as far loss adjustment is quite costly and complex, it may not be credible in many low-income countries.
- **Lack of adverse selection:** Adverse selection occurs when potential insured parties have hidden information about their risk exposure. With index insurance, the objective parameter (e.g., rainfall) is used to determine the level of risk of the insured. Traditional insurance encourages high-risk products to insure, while risk and premium are calculated based on the specific risk profile of each farmer. Index insurance eliminates adverse selection by using the same insurance payout conditions, regardless of their specific risk exposure. Hence, insurers and clients benefit from a more equitable product.
- **Lack of moral hazard:** Moral hazard occurs when individuals engage in risky activities that increase their expected loss. This is because they know that the insurer will pay out if something goes wrong. Such risky activities can leave the insured exposed to higher levels of risk than had been anticipated when premium rates were set, leading to a situation where the insured parties trying to profit from insurance. All products in the defined area are treated equally.
- **Addressing climate change:** Traditional insurance products work best where there is predictability. With index products, perils such as droughts are challenging to insure.

### Low operational costs:

WiI can be distributed, and claims can be settled at relatively lower cost. Education on the product and training of local staff (both prior to product launch and as an ongoing process) are also required.
- **Rapid payout:** Mechanisms for rapid payment and automatic adjustment, allow rapid payouts.

## Post Distribution Monitoring and Satisfaction surveys

There are at least two important assumptions underlying the causal path that leads from the delivery of outputs to expected outcomes:

- The first is, that the targeted population (such as individuals, households or institutions) is the one that receives the outputs. Access is determined by the design of the intervention, i.e. what are the mechanisms to deliver the outputs and often by the process to design these mechanisms.
- The second is whether the targeted population actually uses the products and services delivered in the expected way.

A food or cash transfer is often made with the implicit assumption that people will eat the food or will use the money to buy food of the right quality and in sufficient quantity. However, in the real world people have a range of different objectives and constraints. If there are other pressing needs for the household, they may decide to sell the food and/or use the money to purchase something other than food.

The same may occur when assets are built, or when financial services are delivered. For instance, upgrading an irrigation system might not be in line with a farmer's objectives if he has no land to irrigate. Similarly, providing a tractor to a farmer without resources may not be in a position to intensity production and may actually decide to rent out his plot for other uses.

Financial institutions assume that clients will invest the money they have on loan rather than using it for consumption. However as farmers may have more pressing needs, they may divert the loan to purchasing food or paying for medical expenditures, thus reducing the likelihood of repaying that loan and risking to be trapped in a cycle of indebtedness.

Accordingly, it is important to understand whether the targeted population actually uses the products and services delivered in the expected way. This information can be crucial to understand whether the intervention is going in the right direction to achieve the long-term outcomes. This information can directly feed into the design of services and processes for delivering these services.
