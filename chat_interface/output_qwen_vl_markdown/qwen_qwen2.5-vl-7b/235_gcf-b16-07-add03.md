### A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

Tajikistan is the most vulnerable country to climate change in Europe & Central Asia (ECA) region. Its energy system is dominated by hydropower and is therefore highly exposed to climate change risks. Hydropower is of fundamental importance for economic development, social welfare, and food security. However, it also faces significant challenges due to its inherently precarious and challenging context. Strengthened governance and institutions are necessary to improve the climate resilience of hydropower systems. Additionally, the climate vulnerability of Tajikistan's energy system also has important social and gender dimensions.

In response to these severe challenges, the proposed Project aims to scale up the adoption of climate resilience practices and technologies in the Tajik hydropower sector. Enhanced institutional capacities, modern climate resilience technologies and relevant infrastructure will be developed and implemented through a phased approach, targeting the most critical and highly climate-vulnerable hydropower system. The proposed Project will support the transfer of knowledge and technologies for enhanced climate resilience to Tajikistan's hydropower sector. This will be achieved by implementing a multi-phase Project structure consisting of three closely inter-related components linked to three targeted Project Outputs. Project implementation will be carried out in close cooperation with the Government of Tajikistan (GoT) through the Programme for Climate Resilience (PCRe), as set out in section B.2. This proposal is for further funding under Phase II. This arrangement will allow for a smoother, gradual implementation of the overall Project. The phases are intended to be staggered and not sequential. The first phase (Phase I) has now reached its final stage with all project arrangements completed. The second phase (Phase II) has now reached a critical stage, with all procurement arrangements and engineering services (covering both phases) now in place. The third phase (Phase III) is currently under preparation.

The proposed Project will be delivered through a structured approach comprising of technical assistance, policy dialogue and facility upgrades in close partnership with the Tajik authorities.

The specific objectives of the Project are as follows:

- To increase the adoption of best international practices in the assessment and management of climate change risks to hydropower operations by providing training for hydropower operators and other associated agencies such as providers of hydromet services (Phase I+II);
- To develop enhanced capacities and structures needed to ensure effective transboundary management of hydropower cascades in order to promote the climate resilience of hydropower operations (Phase II); and
- To scale up the integration of climate resilience approaches, technologies and design standards into a strategic hydropower development framework (Phase III).

Specific benefits will include:

- The modernisation of a major hydropower facility taking into account projected future climate conditions;
- The population of Sugan region (approx. 2,400,000 people), in particular women, to benefit from a more secure and climate-resilient electricity supply.

### C.1. Strategic Context

#### Climate Change and Poverty Reduction Strategy (2001)

Hydropower provides approximately 98% of Tajikistan's electricity, making it fundamentally important for the country's economy and development, and for the livelihoods and well-being of its population. Yet this critically important sector faces a number of challenges that are seriously exacerbated by the threat of climate change.

#### Climate Change

The climate in Tajikistan is changing rapidly. The average temperature has increased by 0.5°C since 1976, with an increase of more than 2°C over the past two decades. This warming trend is expected to continue and accelerate. The number of hot days per year has doubled from about 30 in the mid-1980s to around 60 today.

#### Hydroelectric Power

Tajikistan's hydroelectric power plants are among the largest in Central Asia, with a total installed capacity of approximately 25 GW. However, the majority of these plants were constructed during the Soviet era and have seen few upgrades since then. Consequently, most of Tajikistan's hydropower sector operates at below optimal levels.

#### Climate Change Impacts

The climate change impacts on Tajikistan are significant:

- **Water Availability**: The average annual precipitation has decreased by 10% over the past two decades.
- **Glacier Retreat**: Glaciers in Tajikistan have retreated significantly, with a loss of about 25% since the mid-1960s. This is expected to continue and accelerate.

#### Energy Sector

The energy sector faces several challenges:

- **Energy Demand**: The energy demand has increased by more than 40% over the past two decades.
- **Renewable Energy**: Tajikistan's renewable energy potential, particularly solar and wind power, remains largely untapped. There is a need to develop new technologies and infrastructure to harness these resources.

#### Economic Development

The economic development of Tajikistan is heavily dependent on hydropower. However, the sector faces several challenges that are seriously exacerbated by climate change:

- **Water Scarcity**: The water scarcity in Tajikistan has increased significantly over the past two decades.
- **Glacier Retreat**: The glacier retreat in Tajikistan has led to a decrease in water availability for hydropower plants.

#### Conclusion

The situation of chronic electricity shortages has manifested itself further since 2009 when electricity imports through the Central Asian Power System (CAPS) and other regional interconnections have been insufficient. This has resulted in significant power outages, particularly during peak demand periods, which are not able to meet the needs of the region and reduce the overall cost of supply without taking national borders into account.

At the same time, the differences in the resource base for each country mean that the systems became unbalanced which caused dispute among member states.

These observed and projected climatic trends have significant implications for hydrological conditions in Tajikistan, and are projected to result in increased hydrological variability. Some scenarios forecast increased surface runoff and snowmelt in the near future, while others predict a decrease in precipitation and an increase in evaporation rates, leading to reduced water availability and accumulated snow levels. These climate change trends and the consequent increase in hydrological variability have serious implications for Tajikistan's hydropower systems, which are already operating at their maximum capacity due to constraints on the hydrological system. This is why, in the case of Qarokhan reservoir, the detailed studies carried out by EBRD indicate that annual inflows into Qarokhan reservoir will be subject to increasing variability and may change significantly in the future. As part of the resulting increased uncertainty, it is important for Tajikistan's hydropower sector to have a clear understanding of how climate change will impact its operations.

### Management regimes and practices

Hydro-meteorological information services in Tajikistan are typically weak and poorly resourced, having suffered significant under-investment and neglect since the collapse of the Soviet Union. In addition, hydropower operators in Tajikistan still use dam operation methods that were developed decades ago based on historical hydrological data. This is a major reason why hydro-meteorological observations dating from 1970s or even earlier now, raise demand, aging equipment and projected climate change impacts and the related increased uncertainty in hydrology mean that the use of such static approaches is increasingly inappropriate. Models of rainfall-runoff relationships are also often based on historical data and may not be representative of future conditions.

### Understanding climatic conditions

Understanding climatic conditions and their impact on river flows is crucially needed. This means taking into account the new climate reality, which includes increased variability in precipitation patterns and runoff volumes. The lack of understanding of these mechanisms for hydropower dam cascade management and coordination have deteriorated or disappeared since the collapse of the Soviet Union, with river systems such as the Fary Darya and Amu Darya being fragmented across the now independent countries of Tajikistan, Afghanistan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, and Iran. The lack of a coordinated approach to hydropower management and hydro-meteorological information across borders is lacking, with informal, ad hoc practices taking their place in some cases.

### Building new mechanisms

### Downstream Irrigation for Energy

Hydropower dams in Central Asia are almost always built for the dual purposes of energy generation and irrigation, and this is also true of the dams on the Fergana River including Qaraqum. For these reasons, these dams operate under two seasonal regimes: i) a winter regime that optimizes power generation while storing enough water for irrigation during the following spring/summer; and ii) a summer regime that prioritizes the release of water for downstream irrigation. The severe electricity supply and climate resilience challenges in Central Asia are exacerbated by the fact that these dams operate under two seasonal regimes, which creates difficulties concerning the energy security and climate resilience of the population. During the summer season, the electricity demand is high due to increased air conditioning use as well as the need to pump water from the reservoirs into the canals to meet demand for electricity for lighting. In summer the water is spilled instead of being used to generate power due to limited market opportunities to export electricity and as the reservoir capacity in the system is inadequate to allow storage of the spilled water for later use. During this period, the population faces a lack of water for irrigation and drinking purposes, which can be resolved only by importing water from other countries.

Furthermore, there are no reported deficits of water during the spring/summer irrigation season and therefore no competing risks to agricultural irrigation. However, in the winter season, when agricultural irrigation has always been the main water consumer in the region, in the coordination between competing water uses, agriculture has thus priority. It is expected that the Project will deliver further improvements in the optimization and balancing of competing water uses in the face of a changing and more variable climate.

Fiscal corrections: This measure aims at adjusting inflows and outflows (prices and costs) for tax effects, as these represent an important element in the calculation of the financial indicators of the financial economy. The time series of FCS's used for the calculation of the financial(IRR) are thus corrected for taxes.

Externalities: Benefits from personnel employed by the implementation: This measure is to compensate for outflows which do not occur in a socio-economic sense, i.e. which are actually the benefit of some part of the national society. Such an external benefit is the income earned by the employees who work on the project and who are thus remunerated with salaries which are earned by households in the country and thus remain with the society. These amounts are hence considered in the socio-economic perspective.

Willingness To Pay (WTP): As electricity tariffs are kept low in Tajikistan, while load shedding is common feature suppressing demand especially during winter, an approach was chosen to compare the above regulated tariff to a market value, which people are willing to pay for electricity. The Willingness to pay (WTP) in this context is defined as the maximum amount companies and households would be willing to pay for electricity if they had access to it at any time of year. According to the World Bank, the study provides an estimate of the value of unused demand as well as various electricity supply expansion programs which are currently being considered by the government. The WTP is calculated on a per kWh basis and is used in the estimation of project variants. The World Bank study arrives at a weighted average value of WTP of 7.50 c/kWh, which is proposed to be gradually reached by 2025 from the present less than 3c/kWh. WTP levels surpassing the assumed tariffs have been as such not considered in this analysis.
