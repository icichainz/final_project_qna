### A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

The proposed project supports Comoros, one of only two LDCs [Least Developed Countries], to adapt to increasingly extreme weather events and climate change impacts on water quality impacts from landslides/erosion that impact the country's drinking and irrigation water supply.

Comoros has a very small national land area of only 2,613 km² consisting of steep volcanic terrain; with no land further than 700 m above sea level. The population is concentrated in coastal areas, which are already under pressure from limited capacity, and consequently are highly vulnerable to climate change magnified rainfall variability—as is the rural population reliant on rainwater harvesting—resulting in predicted increases in water scarcity due to drought, flood and salinization of groundwater.

In conjunction with national and state governments, water service providers, water user associations and communities, and their development partners (China, Arab Fund for Economic and Social Development and UNDP) who provide the co-funding required to implement this project, Comoros will address these climate change induced financial barriers impeding the improvement of climate resilience of the country's water resources and water supplies.

The project will achieve a national paradigm shift in strengthening the climate resilience of water supply by mainstreaming integrated water resource management (IWRM) into all relevant sectors. This will be achieved through: water sector infrastructure and water user management, including in planning, investment, design, operation & maintenance.

Specifically, the project will invest in:

- Reinforcing the marginalization of climate resilient water supply by strengthening the water sector enabling environment for medium to long-term climate adaptation planning.
This will be achieved by integration of climate information into the recently revised national water legislation reforms, training on risk-based water management practices and capacity building for climate change adaptation.
- Protecting water quality and moderating extreme high and low water resource flows using integrated watershed management approaches. This will be achieved through: improving water supply infrastructure; managing water resources monitoring to provide early warnings and forecasts of climate risks to improve water supply reliability;
- Increasing the climate resilience of water supply infrastructure by diversifying the water supply sources for 450,000 people (rainwater, surface water and groundwater); and designing and constructing climate change risk information systems.

The project is consistent with the priorities identified in the NAPA and has a no objection letter from the NDA.

### B.1. Description of Financial Elements of the Project / Programme

Grant financing is requested from the GCF to build the resilience of the Comoros water supply to the impacts of climate change, more specifically, to manage its limited resources and watersheds, protect its water supply infrastructure and strengthen its capacity for operation and maintenance (O&M) in the face of increasing frequency and intensity of climate extremes. While there are other types of financial instruments available, the proposed traditional grant is being requested due to the potential for leveraging co-financing from the GCF to support the project activities. The Comoros faces a lack of resources; and the urgent need for climate adaptation finance in Comoros as a LDC, SIDS and African first. First, the grant will be used to finance the O&M costs of the water supply system. Second, it will be used to strengthen capacity and project impacts of climate change, which in the absence of climate change, would not be required. Second, as one of the poorest countries in Africa, Comoros faces a number of development challenges including poverty, high levels of poverty and unemployment (with 80% of the rural population poverty-stricken), 45% of the population in absolute poverty, and a lack of access to basic services such as education and health. The country is also highly exposed and vulnerable to current and projected impacts of climate change which has been identified by the World Bank as one of the most vulnerable countries in Africa. This is due to the creation of distressing subsidies given the general lack of private sector financing of water supply and management in Comoros.

The current water supply tariff system is undergoing national reform as part of an on-going sector wide governance strengthening initiative. # Detailed Project / Programme Description

## Strategic Context

### Climate Change Impacts and Vulnerabilities

The IPCC AR5 states that in the southern Indian Ocean (including the Comoros Islands) there will be:

- a decline in rainfall, which will cause a reduction in surface water flows and groundwater recharge and an increase in pollution due to reduced dilution potential;
- increases in mean temperatures - that will reduce water supplies via evapotranspiration and increase water demand;
- continued sea level rise that will contribute to further saltwater intrusion into coastal aquifers; and
- an increase in the frequency and intensity of natural hazards such as cyclones, tsunamides and droughts.

### Comoros

Comoros is one of the poorest countries in the world, with an estimated 80% of the rural population considered poverty-stricken and 46% of the population living in absolute poverty (≤1.25/person/day). This severely constrains its national development capacity.

One of the most urgent needs in Comoros, as stated by the NAPA, is to build the resilience of their water supply to the impacts of climate change. More specifically Comoros needs to increase the resilience of its limited water resources and wastewater management systems, improve access to safe drinking water for all households, enhance the capacity of institutions and communities to plan and operate in increasing climatic extremes.

National capacity to adapt to climate change risks in Comoros is extremely limited as it is both a SIDS but particularly those that are small island developing states (SIDS) with low adaptive capacity. The unemployment rate among young people aged 15-24 is very high at 50.7%. Between 70-80% of the Comorian population are small-scale farmers that are dependent on rainfall for their livelihoods and are therefore particularly vulnerable to climate change impacts, including droughts and to climate change impacts and their successful adaptation. More widely, poverty issues and limited employment opportunities are severely hindering the country from self-sustaining economic growth.

Comoros has a very low capacity to adapt to climate change risks due to its limited current capacity to strengthen its adaptive capacity to be resilient against these impacts. This lack of resilience extends as far as the capacity to submit grant applications to the global climate change adaptation funds.

The UNDP Comoros Climate Profile for Comoros developed by the University of Oxford used refined Global Climate Models to evaluate climate change predictors for this country. This work identified up to a 47% reduction in rainfall during the dry season by 2090, with up to a 45% increase in rainfall in the wet season and a further 14% increase of rainfall in extreme events. This does not include the potential increase in cyclone activity – Comoros is in the Indian Ocean region – which will be captured by GCM projections. This work also identified a rise in sea levels of up to 56m by 2090.

A special review of Global Climate Model predictions for Multimodels in Agroparif shows a 5% reduction in annual rainfall in the dry season and an increase of between 13-47% in wet season rainfall. This model also predicts up to 100% increases in monthly rainfall for some GCM predictions in some wet season months. Data from the existing rain gauges has indicated that there is a trend towards increased rainfall over recent years (2005–2013). With this information, it can be reasonably expected that there will be increases in the duration, intensity and frequency of both droughts and floods in Comoros.

## Impact of Climate Change on Rainfall in Comoros

A detailed analysis of local monthly and daily rainfall analysis (see Feasibility Study Section 1.2 and its Appendix 3) has been undertaken to investigate evidence of climate change influences on rainfall. Monthly data has been used to look at potential increases in climate change aggravated drought, and daily rainfall used to consider potential increased flooding risk.

A ranking analysis using monthly data has revealed a clear downward trend of annual rainfall over the last 50 years, with the most extreme rainfall event recorded being in April 2012 (7.16 mm) where more than 9.48mm fell over days. When considering the period from 1963 to 2013, a period of 100% increases in wet season monthly rainfall, this demonstrates an increasing likelihood of extreme flood events.

Daily rainfall analysis revealed the longest consecutive sequence of dry days (≤1 mm/d) currently averages 34 days per year across the country, with an increased frequency of the occurrence of 10 consecutive dry days over the last decade.

The above analysis was repeated for Effective Dry Days i.e. when rainfall minus evapotranspiration is less than 0 mm/d, which is a more useful index for agriculture. In this analysis the longest consecutive effective dry period in the country averaged 48 days per year across the country with an increased frequency of occurrence.

Daily rainfall analysis also revealed maximum daily rainfall to have exceeded more than 100mm in 8 of the last 9 years, with the most extreme rainfall event on record being in April 2012 (7.16 mm) where more than 9.48mm fell over days. When considering the period from 1963 to 2013, a period of 100% increases in wet season monthly rainfall, this demonstrates an increasing likelihood of extreme flood events.

An analysis of the GCM results on the World Bank Climate Portal has been undertaken as part of the feasibility study for the project. The analysis shows that there is a high probability (95%) of more than 30 consecutive dry days (i.e. ≤1mm/d) falling over the duration of the project investment lifecycle (248 days at the start phase and 768 days at the end phase). This period of time has an average number of 30 consecutive dry days (≤1mm/d) observed across the country in the last decade varied between 54 and 132 days at 95% depending on location.

More than 50% of the population in Grand Comore currently relies on rainwater harvesting. Rainwater harvesting analysis* of average household rainwater tanks** shows they can currently go dry for as long as 30 days (using a government rural demand estimate*** of 35 lpd for a household of 6 persons), although the analysis shows this can be reduced to 15 days with improved water use efficiency and increased water storage capacity.

The use of community shared rainwater harvesting*** does provide some added water security, but the analysis* shows even using drought rationing these are unlikely to provide an additional 21 days of supply.

The feasibility study rainwater harvesting analysis therefore identifies current dry season rainwater harvesting practices in the local context as unsustainable. The only alternative is to rely on groundwater resources which have been shown just getting rural communities through the most recent decade of climate extended droughts, by 5-7 days. However, these are also under pressure and will not be able to provide water security for more than a few days even in towns. Under the current climatic conditions therefore, the existing water schemes and hot backup supplies are just sufficient (by 5-7 days) to provide water supply through the currently experienced dry periods.

However, climate change is expected to reduce the number of dry periods by up to 10 days due to climate change will therefore have catastrophic impacts on rural communities in Grand Comore. Furthermore, there is the island wide failure of the rainwater harvesting system.

### Impacts of Reduced Rainfall on Surface Water Flows and Water Supplies (primarily Anjouan and Mohéli)

The small size of the catchments in Comoros and lack of other natural reservoirs means the stream flows are very variable and many watersheds dry out entirely during dry periods, limiting those which can be used for year-round water supply. Limited hydrological information indicates dry season flows in perennial streams are only sustained by groundwater recharge.

Reduced annual and seasonal rainfall will result in lower average river flows as well as less groundwater recharge and therefore reduced dry season groundwater fed base flows. This is consistent with reports that although permanent rivers exist on the island of Mohéli, they have been reported to be dry for up to 10 months per year. The number of permanent rivers on Anjouan is reported to have declined from 45 in 1953 to 10 by today.

Climate change can therefore be expected to reduce the number of perennial rivers that will be able to supply water for the two islands. In addition, climate change will also reduce the volume of groundwater fed base flows and have both water supply quantity (increasing the frequency and duration of low flow periods) and no flow periods) and water quality impacts (increased temperature, increased sediment load, increased biological activity and micro-biological presence e.g. parasites).

These surface water supply systems are highly vulnerable to such impacts as they have little on-line water storage and few have any water treatment, only delivering raw water.

### Impacts of Reduced Rainfall on Groundwater Resources and Supplies

Ordinarily groundwater aquifers contain multiple years of recharge and therefore require multiple dry years in order to be expected to fail by reducing rainfall. Groundwater resources are therefore generally considered to be much more resilient to dry periods.

In the most populated island of Grand Comore, groundwater is the main water source for 30% of the population – primarily in urban areas (e.g. Moroni). At present this represents about 15 MLD (million liters per day) or one third of total round**. ### Impacts of Reduced Rainfall on Water for Agriculture

Agricultural dry seasons already limit rain-fed cropping – with effective dry periods (i.e., rainfall – evaporation) of 3 months' duration. Rural farmers are therefore already reliant on streams and non-potable water rainfall structures to sustain crops through the dry season, and in some cases rely entirely on groundwater for irrigation purposes.

### Impacts of Increased Storm Flows on Water Resources and Water Supply

Increased rain intensity is projected to increase with climate change, both in terms of wet season rainfall (+45%) and rainfall falling as storms (+14%). The amount* (at the 90th%ile) of very wet days per annum is predicted to increase by 30 days per year. The largest storm event will have a 5-day cumulative rainfall increasing by 120mm (+34%) and the largest monthly rainfall increasing by 250mm.

These increases in storm event rainfall intensity represent an increased risk to both surface water and groundwater resources, with the potential for increased flooding of rivers and streams. Increased flood flows will cause greater erosion, leading to physical damage and also increased catchment erosion resulting in higher turbidity (water entering the supply water systems becomes more concentrated) into water courses, and because the catchments in Comoros are so small and so steep, the percentage increase in peak flow does – i.e. those flows most likely to be creating damage, is greater than the percentage increase in rainfall. So peak flood flows will increase at least as 34%–45%, but it really considering more.

Furthermore because of the feedback process of erosion, once these climate change enhanced peak flows increase erosion and sedimentation rates in rivers and streams, this leads to a further increase in storm event rainfall intensity in later smaller flow events than it was before. Thus turbidity increases all year round due to the climate change enhanced flood flows, which then impacts on water quality.

These observations are supported by research* on watershed erosion rates that show a doubling of the rain-intensity rate increases the erosion rate by an order of magnitude. Furthermore, the same research shows the erosion rate is an order of magnitude greater than the sedimentation rate in streams and rivers. This means that the increased damage to steep watersheds which removes mature canopy during the storm events not only can increase erosion rates rapidly (by an order of magnitude but then leaves the watershed more erodible by an order of magnitude, to subsequent non-storm events.

The April 2012 floods (~1500mm over 6 days; ~400mm on one day) caused much damage in both islands. Stream water surges and increased pressure from groundwater due to the deliberate lowering of groundwater volumes by local inhabitants at the time of the flooding into the system. The groundwaters perched on Moroni however did escape the flood damage. Several of the main water supply boreholes are located in close proximity to storm run-off routes off the volcano.

### Climate Vulnerability Variance and Distribution

The Union of Comoros consists of three islands – Grand Comore, Anjouan and Mohelé. The most populated is Grand Comore, then Anjouan, then Mohelé, with the capital Moroni in south-west Grand Comore.

Grand Comore has a humid tropical climate with rainfall concentrated from November to April. This supports water availability for locations within 5km of the coast, where saline intrusion is problematic. The population living away from the coast (50% of the island) is solely reliant on rainwater harvesting.

Anjouan and Mohelé rely on river flows from steep streams, high, deeply incised volcanic watersheds. The catchment flows respond to heavy rainfall events, but drying out in extended dry periods and generating rapid and intense storm floods after heavy rainfall.

The islands therefore have different water regimes: some are vulnerable to the effects of climate change enhanced by droughts, others are prone to flooding due primarily to Grand Comore’s steep slopes and turbid waters from Anjouan and Mohelé. 

### National Climate Change Policies and Strategies

The Comoros national sustainable development strategy; the Accelerated Growth and Sustainable Development Strategy, SAG20 (2015-2019), articulates GCC’s vision to achieve recovery and resilience in the face of climate change and expansion of access to basic services. The SAG20 also aims at reducing vulnerability for the most vulnerable people. The SAG20 includes a national strategy on climate change reduction through initiatives that include the establishment of appropriate monitoring networks to detect climate-related changes, as well as the development of adaptation strategies.

Both the Second National Communication on Climate Change (2012) and the NAPA (2006) identify specific climate change impacts (most notably drought), but also heavy rains and cyclones) on the water sector (see Section E.5). These include:

- Strengthening water resources management and environmental monitoring;
- Improving groundwater management and preservation;
- Enhancing the resilience of existing infrastructure, including roads and bridges;
- Protecting ecosystems and regulating stream flow;
- Integrating local populations in water resource management.

The National Water Code recently received a public consultation notification, includes detailed plans on how the more decentralized, current institutional reforms will improve water management. The National Water Code does not however make explicit reference to climate change risks and the need for water sector’s adaptation to these risks.

#### Water Resources

The types of water resources available and exploited in Comoros are island specific. Watersheds on Anjouan and Mohéli islands are steep and highly vulnerable to erosion. These catchments respond very quickly to intense rainfall events (typically within 1-2 days), generating rapid peak flows, yet also have limited retention times and can drain out within 1-2 hours of the rain event. The rivers in these watersheds are small and ephemeral with low baseflows. Most river flows are, compounded by highly turbid flood flows and some have dry season flow shortfalls. - The water supply schemes on Anjouan and Mohéli are therefore dependent upon groundwater resources and rainwater harvesting.

## Water Supply Coverage

The population sources their water via rainwater harvesting (25%) or from household (40%) and community (25%) fountains led directly by gravity fed stream inlets and some well supplies in coastal towns. The type of water supply system varies from island to island, with groundwater supplies only being used in Grand Comoré and stream inlets only in Anjouan and Mohéli.

Groundwater distribution systems are restricted to the coastal plain due to limitations on pumping and electricity, as well as the locations of boreholes and wells being restricted to low ground elevations near the sea. There is minimal water storage within the groundwater system with a maximum capacity of 24 hours at an average flow rate of 24. The groundwater system is not currently sized to support the rural population currently using rainwater harvesting.

Surface water distribution systems are gravity fed only and are therefore generally restricted to supplying communities and towns on the coastal plain. There is minimal water storage within the surface water supply distribution systems, with water supply effectively being from instantaneous flow scheme.

Water consumption per capita is estimated to be only 35 liters per day, but is reported to be as low as 11 litres per capita per day (l/c/d) in Anjouan. The majority of this water use is for domestic purposes and the remaining water is used mainly for daily needs (drinking, cooking and hygiene washing). Excessive water use and water user wastage are not issues in Comoros.

For those connected to a reliable year round local water supply system there is a thriving private sector road tanker distribution system. A pervious of 20 liters of non-potable water (not sterilized) costs 250 KMF USD (25-30m³) in Grand Comoré and 20 KMF USD (2.5m³) in Anjouan – the difference in cost reflecting the better water quality in Grand Comoré compared to groundwater (compared unpressed stream water) and the greater poverty in Anjouan.

## Water Governance

Comoros recently updated its National Water Code (2016) to deliver institutional reforms necessary to improve sustainable water planning and management. Unusually, the country has not only national ministries but also issued specific water management authorities, which fragment national efforts to manage sectoral planning, and disparate regional water resources. The new code was approved by Parliament in 2015 and officially published in 2016 but has not yet been ratified by Parliament. Once ratification is obtained, the Code will need to be translated into specific regulations. The Code lays out reforms necessary to improve water resource management, in particular the harmonization of water resources across all islands, and the establishment of MINW in the urban areas within Aruquipa and Moheli.

With respect to QAM laws and responsibilities for the various institutions within the water sector, they are outlined in Annex XVIII: Operations and Responsibilities and aligned with the new Water Code.

There is no mention of climate change issues in the new Water Code, which is more focused on service provider institutional strengthening and improved cost recovery. Drought and flood planning and management are not currently practiced at national level.

In the Water Code, GCo has committed to put in place the framework for implementation and full enforcement of a new tariff system in 7 years. Plans for a comprehensive tariff reform require significant regulatory work prior to implementation. The new tariff will be based on water use levels and will apply to all users regardless of income levels. Principles include universal access, preferential treatment for lowest-income households and cost recovery for service providers. The new tariff will be implemented in stages with the first stage being introduced by 2019. Users must cover at least 80% of costs. QAMs will include a fixed and a volume-based component and will be revised periodically by a water user committee (WUC) on a five-year rolling basis.

The process to set tariff levels results in the establishment of an independent water regulator (with its own staff and budget), public consultations, affordability studies (undertaken during the project) and pilots on-site to fine-tune tariff levels. The latter is an important step since the sustainability of a tariff system relies on evidence of improved service quality.

The Code also enshrines the creation of a National Fund for the Development of Water and Sewage Infrastructure (FNDAW). This fund will be established by law in 2017, with the objective to finance water infrastructure as management and supervisory structure. Like tariff reform, FNDAW appears to be little more than a concept at this stage.

The present groundwater abstraction rate has been increasing at an average of about 6% each year since 1970 and is now in the upper communities, which are currently supplied by a network of 1,744 boreholes (82%). i.e. 40% of the normal water demand will be able to be supplied by 5 new boreholes – 1 in each of 5 target zones. But to meet the remaining demand, it is necessary to construct additional boreholes and increase pumping capacity at existing distribution systems and pumping stations expanded into the rural upland areas.

In Anjouan and Mohéli, the climate change risks do not relate to water quantity but to water quality degradation, both during the storm periods and after the storms. The main risk in Anjouan is that of saltwater intrusion, which occurs mainly after the storm has passed. Great amounts of fresh groundwater resources are also thought to exist in Anjouan and Mohéli on perched aquifers for instance. Due to a lack of groundwater studies, this source has never been exploited.

# D.1 Value Added for GCF Involvement

The water resources and water supplies of Comoros, a Small Island Developing State and Least Developed Country, are inherently vulnerable to climate change and specifically vulnerable to increased climate variability, given its location in the Indian Ocean. The country has limited financial capacity and institutional and human resource capacity to adapt to and manage climate change risks. Existing public and private water resources and water supplies are capable of meeting national water demand during normal operating hydrological conditions, but are progressively becoming increasingly stressed due to increasing population pressure, increased water use intensities (food demand and erosion). Recent baseline water supply projects have failed to target or address water security issues in the face of climate change. The country’s existing water sector is also vulnerable as the new national Water Code does not refer to climate change adaptation – resulting in the current reform of the water sector being hampered by this lack of reference.

The GCF involvement is therefore critical to Comoros because it provides the financial resources to address the additionally caused by climate change impacts by taking a functional existing national water supply regime and increasing its resilience to climatic extremes. The support provided by the project for each Output is described below:

### Vulnerability of the country and population (Adaptation only)

#### Country Vulnerability

The Union of the Comoros is classified as a Least Developed Country and a Small Island Developing State (SIDS) by the UN System. It is one of not only the poorest countries in the world but also an extremely vulnerable SIDS, with less than 25% of its total population considered to be urbanized, a small land area of approximately 1800 km², and one of the most vulnerable to climate change impacts.

The global position on SIDS vulnerabilities is articulated in the SIDS Accelerated Modalities of Action (SAMOA) Pathway[1] (2014). This position statement (previously defined in the Barbados Programme of Action and later the Mauritius Strategy for Sustainable Development) identifies SIDS as having high vulnerability to climate change, natural hazards such as cyclones, droughts, volcanic and seismic hazards, which are not only hinderable sustainable development but also climate resilience.

The IPCC Fifth Assessment Report (AR5, 2014), Chapter 29 specifically identifies SIDS as having high vulnerability and low adaptive capacity. The report states that the combination of economic, social, political, institutional, and environmental issues will increase human and environmental resilience to climate change effects while also recognizing substantial changes to human settlement patterns within SIDS may cause climate change effects. Land and water management are critical for adaptation in SIDS.

In addition to limited technical, financial and human resource barriers to climate adaptation, constraints from weak political and legal frameworks and difficulties in translating national mainstreaming into over-coming rural and especially small-scale farmers' access to resources and services are also significant challenges.

National capacity to adapt to climate change risks in Comoros is extremely limited as it is for many SIDS. At least 14.3% of the population is unemployed. The unemployment rate among those aged 15-24 is very high at 50.5%. Between 70-80% of the Comorian population are small-scale farmers that are dependent on rain-fed water resources for subsistence and food security; they are therefore closely linked to water security and to climate change impacts and their successful adaptation.

More widely, poverty issues and limited employment opportunities are severely hindering the country from self-sustaining economic growth.[2]

A national consultation process on climate variability within Comoros was undertaken as part of this project design process. Facilitated by UNDP, the participatory consultation was undertaken between November 2015 and November 2016 using an expert stakeholder group involving senior national experts from the key line ministries and island agencies. The results were used to identify climate vulnerability hotspots in Comoros. These were then ranked upon review of existing water supply feasibility studies as well as national climate vulnerability and disaster risk management assessments, followed by expert discussions. Consultation documentation is enclosed in Annex X (f & g).

The country was divided into 26 zones. The vulnerability of these zones were then assessed using 34 metrics covering 8 priority areas: population density, water resources, food security, water resource management, disaster risk management, socio-economic vulnerability, on-going governmental and non-governmental programmes, and likely ease of project implementation (e.g. accessibility). These zones were then ranked based upon a summarization of climate and socio-economic vulnerability, as well as the presence of ongoing or co-occurring project initiatives and ease of project implementation.

Using these criteria, the most vulnerable zones to climate risks are those with existing ongoing or planned climate adaptation projects. Of the 19 most vulnerable zones identified, 4 were already being targeted by development partners working in them, resulting in 15 being selected as targets for the GCF proposal.

These 15 zones deliberately covered all three islands (6 on Grande Comore, 7 on Anjouan and 2 on Mohéli), which enabled a comparison of vulnerability across different island types but also the differing hydrological regimes (i.e. rainwater, groundwater and surface water) and water supply types.

### Tariff and financial IRR simulations

In the absence of nationwide affordability data, the project design team has run simulations based on two scenarios: (i) the current water tariff of US$5.3/m³ charged by MA-MINE in Moron is applied to the entire user base of the project from year 1 onwards; and (ii) a progressive increase in tariffs over time, starting at 6% above the current tariff for year 2 and subsequently increase every five years by 20% (higher likelihood scenario). If GCF were to fund the entire project – initial capex, operating costs, and debt service – the IRR would be 1.9% in the first scenario (low tariffs scenario) or 8-8.5% (full tariffs scenario); to 8.7% (progressive tariffs scenario). In other words, the government would have to step in and support the project for it to be financially viable. If GCF were only to fund part of the project – even at GCF’s very concessional terms – would only widen the funding gap by adding interest expenditure.

Household and community rainwater harvesting was evaluated and found to be drought vulnerable due to the limiting role of rainfall in replenishing these systems during construction programmes. Further investment in household rainwater harvesting would not improve drought resilience.

In Grand Comore, the fresh groundwater resource is not uniform nor well understood, but years of successful operating in some areas has demonstrated that it can be a reliable source of water for communities to access during periods of drought at least in some locations. Existing boreholes with acceptable salinity will be evaluated to assess their role in providing control through controlled pumping trials and their pumping regime altered so as to provide maximum resilience.

New production boreholes will be drilled once exploration drilling confirms fresh groundwater to exist, and used to replace existing shallow wells that are not sustainable and/or used to reach high elevation valleys with currently no access to groundwater and sole reliance on rainwater.

Groundwater monitoring equipment (i.e., multi-level piezometers and conductivity equipment) will be installed to measure the water quality of the aquifer in the Grand Comore, Anjouan and Mohéli. The objective is to assess sustainability and that aquifers are effectively recharging after each season. Preliminary groundwater investigation will also be undertaken on Anjouan and Mohéli to confirm for the first time the presence of viable groundwater resources on these islands.

The groundwater abstraction water supply networks will be fitted with water tanks. These tanks will hold 1-2 days of water demand. These are used to ensure the borehole can pump at the lowest uniform flow rate – which minimizes the risk of over-pumping and ensures that pumping is at peak water use rates (peak flows last for about 3-4 times average daily demand rates).

Storing the water for 1-2 days does however require the borehole to be inactive, hence the water treatment will only rely on gravity flow. Gravity flow delivery is also responsive to drought periods. The tanks are also necessary due to the risk of storm water run-off entering the existing pumpings wells – as happened in April 2017 for the capital Moroni and Anjouan.

On Anjouan and Mohéli the groundwater resources are not exploited although they are believed to support the dry season flows in the watersheds. Consequently, nearly all water supplies currently use stream inflows. These inflows are highly variable due to storm event erosion associated turbidity and its associated pollution, as well as dry season low and no flow periods.

# F1. Economic and Financial Analysis

## Approach and Methodology

The economic analysis of the proposed project was carried out in accordance with the Guidelines for the Economic Analysis of Projects of United Nations Development Program (UNDP). The economic efficiency of the investment was determined by calculating its internal rate of return (IRR) using a shadow wage rate. UNDP recommends an internal rate of return (IRR). For consistency purposes, all projects developed with the support of UNDP have thus far used this methodology.

### Economic Values

Economic values (costs and benefits) are all measured in real terms of 2017. Economic costs of the project are net of taxes, duties, and price contingencies. Furthermore, the analysis assumes a shadow wage rate of $1 for unskilled and semi-skilled labor. This is because the project will be implemented by local contractors who will hire workers at their market wage rate (financial cost), we expect this assumption leads to significantly over-estimating the economic costs of the project. The shadow wage rate is used in order to reflect the true opportunity cost of unskilled and semi-skilled labor. The benefit-cost analysis of an energy efficiency project in Cambodia, the World Bank used a shadow wage rate factor of 0.75. This implies that the economic benefits are understated by this factor.

The above assumptions are made for the purpose of calculating the true net present value (NPV) of the investment noting that in doing so, the economic cost of the project is over-estimated, and the net present value of the investment is then under-estimated.

# H.2, Arrangements for Monitoring: Reporting and Evaluation

GCF funding will be used to ensure that monitoring and evaluation systems are put in place to track progress over the 5 years of project implementation towards the planned project outcomes and fund level impacts. This will be achieved through the means of verification outlined in Table H.1.2 above, where proposed indicators and methods for data collection have been identified.

The project will use the Randomized Control Trial approaches to monitor community adaptive capacity and vulnerability to climate change impacts on water access, livelihoods, food security, watershed management, water access, improvement of water supply delivery services and health condition improvements. The evaluation will be conducted through a combination of quantitative and qualitative methods gathering baseline data on sources of water, capacity on integrating climate information on water related decision making, adaptive capacity to climate change impacts, vulnerability to climate change impacts, income and harvesting of natural resources. The questionnaire will also include analysis of additional variables such as gender roles, age groups, education levels, etc., which are likely to influence the impact of climate change impacts on water access, gender roles, etc.

The monitoring will be done routine, continuous and participatory with communities and stakeholders through a combination of quantitative and qualitative methods. The evaluation will be conducted through a combination of quantitative and qualitative methods gathering baseline data on sources of water, capacity on integrating climate information on water related decision making, adaptive capacity to climate change impacts, vulnerability to climate change impacts, income and harvesting of natural resources. The questionnaire will also include analysis of additional variables such as gender roles, age groups, education levels, etc., which are likely to influence the impact of climate change impacts on water access, gender roles, etc.

The project is designed to engage the communities in continuous monitoring of the project activities as indicated in the ecosystems restoration efforts. The project monitoring will benefit beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries through the use of a detailed survey instrument, the Household Survey: Adaptive Capacity and Climate Vulnerability Assessment (HSCVA). This survey instrument will be used to assess the impacts of the proposed interventions, the project includes the use of Randomized Control Trial approaches as part of the evaluation. The HSCVA will be used to measure the following variables: (i) adaptive capacity to climate change impacts; (ii) impact of improving the water supply delivery service in and periods and (iii) impact of restoration on the environment.

The project results as outlined in the project results framework will be monitored and reported annually and evaluated periodically during project implementation to ensure the project effectively achieves these results.
