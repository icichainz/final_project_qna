## The Amazon Ecosystem Business Facility (EEBF) will provide effective climate change mitigation outcomes by investing in eco bio-businesses (EBBs) supporting the sustainable management and conservation of Peruvian forests.

The EEBF will contribute to the implementation of REDD+ infrastructure that will in turn provide support and evidence for its emissions reductions. This will take place in a context in which Peru is developing enabling conditions that promote the development of EBBs, with the aim of achieving results in an uniform manner with a single National Forest Reference Level for deforestation and degradation beginning in 2021.

This proposal responds to the Enhanced Access Request for proposals, establishing an effective mechanism for the implementation of REDD+ projects in Peru. The EEBF will provide technical assistance and seed capital grants in line with those of GCF.

During the project lifetime the EEBF will identify and invest in the expansion, upscaling and supporting technological innovation of EBBs that are focused on forest-based products and services. In parallel, the EEBF will provide technical support through an innovation partner to incubate and accelerate EBBs. The EEBF will prioritize projects in areas with high potential for carbon sequestration in Amazonia biome, namely: Amazonas, San Martin, Cusco, Puno, Loreto and Madre de Dios (See maps in Annex 16).

## Figure 1 Project snapshot

The key objectives of the Amazon EEBF are:

- Provide effective climate change mitigation. The EEBF provides technical assistance and seed capital grants to support the development of EBBs while leveraging private finance to reduce emissions and enhance carbon stocks.
- The EEBF will provide specialized technical assistance to EBBs through an innovation partner to prioritize projects in areas with high potential for carbon sequestration, based on a ranking of repayable grants to 55 EBBs. This ranking is based on the availability of financial resources, financing needs of EBBs and their ability to generate results.
- GCF-funded activities will strengthen Peru’s climate change mitigation measures, empowering Peruvian EBBs to prevent 4,602 hectares of forest from being deforested. During the project lifetime, the EEBF will contribute to the prevention of deforestation and avoiding the emission of 3.8 million tonnes of CO2 with an estimated cost of $2.63 USD / tCO2e.

## B.1. Project Title: Green Climate Fund Proposal V3.0 (Page 7)

Peru has experienced strong economic growth in recent years, reducing poverty from 56 percent to 22 percent between 2005 and 2018[1]. However, the pace of progress significantly slowed down during the past five years as the global commodity price collapse hit Peru’s economy. The country is still one of the most vulnerable countries in terms of people living below the poverty threshold[2]. Vulnerable populations are under increased pressure from climate change and environmental degradation, which have been exacerbated by a series of natural disasters over recent years. Poverty remains high, and poverty is concentrated among rural, indigenous populations, who are also the most affected by climate change.

Peru’s historical emissions have increased by 91 percent from 82.6 MTCO2e in 1990 to 163.8 MTCO2e in 2016[3]. Under historic accounting rules, Peru’s emissions are projected to increase further to 245.7 MTCO2e by 2030 (from the 1990 level). Forest and other land use (FOLU) accounts for 42.3 percent (71.4 MTCO2e) of total emissions in 2016[4]. The FOLU sector is projected to account for 58.5 percent (144.9 MTCO2e) of total emissions by 2030, with the share of FOLU emissions accounting for 53 percent of the total. In its nationally determined contributions, the Government of Peru envisages a 40 percent reduction in greenhouse gas emissions.

Forest conservation and sustainable land use accounts for the largest portion of reduced GHG emissions projected under the NDC[5]. The sector is responsible for approximately 53,800,000 tons of emissions from the FOLU sector will contribute to 68% of Peru’s expected overall emission reduction goal of 30% in 2030. Deforestation in the Amazon region accounts for about 71 percent (49 MTCO2e) of total deforestation, with the remaining 29 percent occurring in approximately 77% of total national deforested areas. These regions are comprised of Amazonian highlands, and high and mid-altitude Andean forests. The majority of deforestation occurs on private lands, but a significant portion also occurs in the high and lowland Amazon are driven by a lack of clearly defined tenure rights—over 40% of deforestation occurs on land with no formal title or tenure security. Deforestation is also driven by infrastructure expansion and land speculation as well as the presence of illicit logging, coca cultivation and mining.

These factors result in climate problems such as degraded land unsuitable for agriculture as well as a lack of quality and quantity of water resources. The combination of these factors creates serious cycle of continuous degradation and expansion of deforested and degraded areas, resulting in increased vulnerability of indigenous populations and low-income rural populations whose livelihoods depend on forest-based activities.

Out of Latin American countries, Peru is the third highest rates of loss and damage due to natural disasters such as droughts, flooding cold waves and hail, as well as more extreme climate events such as El Niño have increased in frequency and intensity. The country has experienced a 10% increase in the number of people affected by land degradation previously described resulting in economic losses (crops, infrastructure and human settlements). In 72% of national agricultural emergencies are related to climate phenomena such as droughts, strong rains and floods.

Increasing climate events adversely affect ecosystems and populations. Flooding and landslide events will decrease water quality, increase soil erosion and damage infrastructure; further affecting water treatment, energy provision, production and transport systems. The combination of these factors results in increased vulnerability of indigenous peoples who cause losses in crops and livestock and changes in plant composition in forests, causing loss of plant species.

In developing national REDD+ results-based implementation and financing mechanism, MINAM has enacted further regulations to identify and categorize REDD+ actions to be considered under the national performance in the context of the UNFCCC. These include a set of criteria for identifying and classifying REDD+ actions as well as environmental performance vis-à-vis Canarias Safeguarding and Peru’s country approach, as well as in relation to carbon units to be considered in the national voluntary markets and overall national greenhouse gases accounting system under the Kyoto Protocol’s NDC Box. Below are the criteria defining, categorize and design, and implement a REDD+ action under the national REDD+ framework.

## Box 1 Criteria to identify, categorize and/or design and implement a REDD+ Action

As defined in Article 1 of the guidelines to identify and classify a REDD+ action, the following criteria shall be met to be considered as an action under the national REDD+ framework:

- **a)** The action must meet with at least one (1) of the five eligible REDD+ activities (measures as defined by the UNFCCC).
- **b)** The action must be consistent with at least one (1) of the eight (8) actions, strategies defined under the EMBEC, as the public policy instrument constituting Peru’s national action plan.
- **c)** The action must be consistent with at least one (1) of the eight (8) mitigation measures in the Climate Change Framework Law.
- **d)** The action must reduce greenhouse gas emissions and/or the enhancement of forest carbon stocks against a national emissions and absorptions baseline.
- **e)** A detailed description of the institutional arrangements related to their activity is in place.
- **f)** Have available and quantitative information on costs and mitigation potential.
- **g)** Are consistent with relevant international, regional, national, sectoral, sub-sectoral, and/or other relevant policies, laws, regulations, programmes and projects, as promoted by sectoral, regional and local authorities, as relevant.
- **h)** Generate benefits beyond climate change mitigation, including social, economic and environmental.

- **i)** Generates greenhouse gas emission reductions and/or the enhancement of forest carbon stocks as a direct result of their activity.
- **j)** The action is consistent with guidelines by the DGCCD, as Peru’s REDD+ focal point, in relation to its national action plan, and is subject to monitoring and reporting in the context of implementation of REDD+ implementation.

#### Output 1.1: Strengthened technical and institutional capacities to monitor and prevent deforestation and forest degradation

In 2021, MINAM submitted to the UNFCCC a new forest reference level (PERL), which is still in the process of being accepted. This update includes improvements in terms of precision and uncertainty calculations. Further, the methodology to estimate deforestation has been modified: this reference level is still limited to the Amazon biome and to forests with a minimum area of 50 hectares. The new forest reference level (PERL) will be fully incorporated; but initial estimates indicate it to be very significant. Developing these degradation estimates is a priority for MINAM.

In this context, MINAM seeks to strengthen the Forest Cover Monitoring Module (MCMB), which is part of the National Forest and Wildland Information System (SNIFS), by developing an analysis of comparability and consistency of forest cover data with other national and international datasets. This will be done through a comparison with the country’s official deforestation information unit (Geobosques). This analysis will ensure a consistent instrument for the calculation of emissions from forest degradation.

The first task will be to incorporate into Geobosques the new methodology developed to estimate deforestation and to continue to improve the level of precision and detail of the instrument until it is able to detect changes in forest cover in sub-national areas. The second task will be to develop a reference level for forest degradation, which will take advantage of its beneficiaries on forests. Not only will it have a positive impact on this project, but it will benefit all REDD+ initiatives, as well as the national and international efforts towards the achievement of the Sustainable Development Goals in maintaining deforestation-free operations.

The second task is to complete the work started on the estimation of emissions from forest degradation, which is currently being developed by MINAM with timber harvesting in standing forests subject to degradation pressure. This will allow for the development of a reference level for forest degradation and the methodology approach to generate information on the data on deforestation activity, which in turn will form part of general reporting requirements under the framework of the country’s various climate commitments.

In addition, MINAM will develop an accuracy analysis for the forest loss maps (years 2001-2019). This incorrect accuracy evaluation of the activity data will allow verification of the reduced emissions for performance payment.

# D. Efficiency and effectiveness (max. 500 words, approximately 1 page)

The cash flow profile of most EBIs represents a barrier to significant investment in the sector. Most EBIs require large upfront capital investments that are not recoverable from project revenues over decades after the first investment depending on several factors. The stretched-out cash flow profile — especially of long rotation projects with a high initial cost and low annual revenue stream — makes it difficult for private investors to finance these projects, even at established costs but also maintenance costs in the early years of operation have to be financed up front until operational revenues can be generated.

The EBIs are not designed as a stand-alone investment vehicle. They are intended to provide equity and bankable for conventional debt financing and thus in dire need for flexible and patient equity financing. In fact, domestic financial institutions (DFIs) and other public sector investors have been the primary source of capital for EBIs up until now.

Despite offering attractive risk-adjusted returns and significant impact potential, especially private investors have had limited interest in investing directly into EBIs due to their high initial costs, long payback periods, and perceived operational or country risks. It is expected that GCF’s investment will catalyze further co-investments into the EBIs by other public sector investors as well as private equity funds. The GCF’s investment will also provide an additional track record to reduce the perceived risk of EBIs and attract future investors. Similarly, the GCF support will help to build a track record for the EBI model in forest-based companies, which can be used for project diversification. Once operational, the EBIs will attract potentially additional capital from REDD+ and other national and international public sector investors, as well as private equity funds.

Capital mobilization and investment leverage: GCF’s investment is expected to have a multiplier effect in two ways. On one side, the EBI will typically not be the sole investor for the EBIs, but provide financing alongside other sources such as local financial institutions (LFIs), impact investors and public sector investors. This will help to reduce the risk profile of the EBIs and increase the demand for the grants.

On the other hand, PROFITABILITY: by its experience in fundraising funds is expected to increase the investment of the EBI facility, leveraging resources from other international sources like donors or impact investors.

Finally, the EBI would be one of the first funds in the region to solely invest in forest-based companies with the potential to focus-on investments by creating more track records for EBIs as an asset class and by reducing potential risks.

CO2 sequestration: It is estimated that EBIs will lead to a total of 3,605.38 tons of CO2 emission reductions due to avoided deforestation during the project lifetime. Hence, every USD invested will contribute to emission reductions of approximately 3.91 tons of CO2. This estimate is deemed conservative because it does not account for (i) emission reductions from additional capital raised through EBIs, (ii) emission reductions from grant repayment by EBIs, (iii) emission reduction due to the project’s impact on other forest-based companies, and (iv) carbon sequestration due to increased forest cover.

The estimated mitigation cost of 2.63 USD/tonCO2 are higher when compared to other — although less comparable — GCF-funded projects. Experiences from other GCF-funded projects in the region, however, confirms that mitigation costs strongly depend on the technology and scale of a project but can be significantly higher than what is estimated for the Amazon EBI.

## Summary of the Secretariat’s assessment

### Project background

1. Peru is home to large carbon stocks in its diverse ecosystems, including the Amazon rainforest and Andes mountains covering 60 per cent of its territory.
2. The economic boom in recent years has succeeded in bringing a substantial part of the population out of poverty, with poverty rates decreasing from 54 per cent in 2001 to 38 per cent in 2010. This has been accompanied by a significant increase in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions: from 67 million tonnes CO<sub>2</sub>-equivalent (MtCO<sub>2</sub>) in 1990 to 168 MtCO<sub>2</sub> in 2016, with 42.3 per cent of the 2016 emissions coming from deforestation and forest degradation.
3. Emissions accounts for approximately 77 per cent of total nationwide deforestation. These regions are comprised of forests that have been converted to agriculture or pastureland, as well as those cleared by migration from highlands and unsustainable land stewardship practices in the high and lowland Amazon and Andes mountain ranges.

4. Peru has been at the forefront in the development of the REDD-plus framework. Its nationally determined contribution (NDC) recognizes the importance of forests in terms of mitigation, with a target to reduce deforestation by 70 per cent between 2013-2020 and half of Peru’s expected overall emission reduction goal (i.e., annual emission reductions of 5.8MtCO<sub>2</sub>-eq.).

5. The project aims to contribute to this mitigation effort by unlocking the potential of small, community-based enterprises, or eco-businesses (EBBs), in the stewardship of forests. EBBs are defined as businesses that use forest resources for their livelihood and natural resources in their activities, which range from sustainable tourism to forestry and agroforestry products. These businesses are often small-scale, family-owned operations with limited access in size and community-based, and they face barriers in accessing markets and regular credits from the financial sector.
6. The proposal would support up to 55 EBBs by providing them with training on business development, market access, and financial management; all of which will help these businesses plan, and a reimbursable grant to enable them to scale up operations. These elements would have a positive impact in terms of increasing the number of small-scale enterprises that can access finance, and thus establishing a channel for impact finance to contribute to the conservation of forests.

7. The EBBs aim to draw down from GCF funds in the first 10 years of operation, supporting 55 EBBs. After that, it will rely on the reflows from the supported EBBS, with the option of scaling up to a maximum of 250 EBBs (i.e., 4.5 per cent of all EBBs in Peru) if when they materialize: It is estimated that an additional 25 EBBs can be supported between years 10-30, and the total number of EBBs could reach a maximum of 305 (i.e., 6.7 per cent of all EBBs in Peru) if GCF funds are available for up to 40 years (i.e., 2018-2058). The annual cost is estimated at USD 250,000 to 300,000 annually but, if successful, there is a possibility to crowd in finance from local financial institutions, impact investors and international donors.

## Component-by-component analysis

### Component 1: Strengthening REDD plus governance, institutional and regulatory systems (total cost USD 886,250; core cost USD 852,750 or 96.8% of project)

In component 1, the project will directly contribute to strengthening Peru's REDD plus institutions and governance by implementing two strategic interventions under the national framework: The EBRF will provide specialized technical assistance and backstopping to (i) strengthen forest governance, including improved monitoring of deforestation and forest degradation; and (ii) strengthen technical and institutional capacities to ensure the social and environmental integrity of REDD plus projects.

Addressing these goals will result in strengthened forest governance, increased knowledge and deforestation monitoring, all of which are key to operationalizing RBPs. Among other things, this component will support the development of a national carbon market by international donors; it will also critically support the ability of EEBS to trade credits on voluntary carbon markets, if necessary for their business model, contributing to their financial sustainability.

In the context of this project, this first component's focus on REDD plus bolsters the financial sustainability of the EBRF. The EBRF is expected to generate revenues from around USD 150,000 annually; they will be earmarked to the Amazon EBB (component 2) in support of EBS costs and project management. These revenues are expected to provide a significant boost in ensuring the long-term capitalization of the EBRF in addition to grant repayments by beneficiaries.

### Component 2: Establishing the EBRF Business Facility (EBBF) (core cost USD 6,649,550; core cost USD 7,237,200 or 89.3% of project)

This component, which represents nearly 90.7% of the total funding, will support the establishment of the EBRF business facility and grant delivery to EEBS through supporting EEBS efforts through two funding windows in line with the GCF enhanced direct access modality.

The first window, funded through an initial USD 742,253 grant from GCF, will be an inclusive process involving a series of activities aimed at identifying potential EBRF partners for activities such as concept evaluation, business planning, market assessment and research, technology transfer, capacity building, and project development. The partners are to be social enterprise organizations with ample experience of investing in high-impact companies and which have established themselves or organizations in 55 countries to assess, identify and develop sustainable business models.

The second window will focus on providing seed capital to a minimum of 55 EEBS with average grant volume of USD 10,000 towards development, implementation, monitoring and reporting activities.

### Women in Forest User Groups

Women rarely formally own land or trees. Women are generally underrepresented in forest user groups, such as village forest committees or community forest associations. Women's participation in stakeholder consultations is often nominal (see country analysis) and women tend to be excluded from decision-making processes.

For communities where women are the primary users of natural resources, they are more likely than men to engage in subsistence activities such as cultivating food crops and collecting fuel wood and non-wood forest products from natural forests. Women tend to disproportionately bear the costs of subsistence tree and forest product collection and processing, which can be a significant burden on women.

The loss of access to forest resources due to deforestation and land use change and loss of community access to forests can have a disproportionately adverse impact on women, with indirect impacts on female-headed households and their extended members. Women represent the majority of the rural workforce in many countries where only 10% or less are in the formal workforce. In forestry, there is much lower share: forestry is often performed as a male sector. ### I.V.A. Violence against women

This section presents some forms of gender-based violence against women, produced by an infringement of their human rights in the public or private spheres, based on behaviors that seek to produce physical, psychological, sexual (including Sexual Exploitation, Sexual Abuse and Sexual Harassment), labor, patrimonial, family abuse or of another nature, being the sex and gender roles of the victim factors that increase their vulnerability. In this sense, variables of femicide and physical violence are analyzed.

In the first case, femicide is considered as the final episode of a series of violent and discriminatory acts against women, which may be intimate, when the alleged perpetrator is a partner, ex-partner or relative of the victim, or non-intimate femicide, when the alleged perpetrator is a stranger, frequently the neighbor, friend, client, or a perpetrator of sexual violence seeking to cover up the crime.[8]

a. Little information on the situation of women in the forestry sector and in the EBBs, which makes it difficult to make decisions and design differentiated actions.

b. Concentration of power and representation functions in decision-making bodies by men, which hinders the participation of women in decision-making and their needs.

c. Difficulties for women to integrate into the labor market under adequate and equal conditions, which will generate an unequal use of the benefits of the project.

### Mitigation Measure 1: Prepare a gender assessment and gender-specific action plans

The companies in the portfolio must prepare a specific evaluation of the situation and main needs of women associated with the local project, understanding the gaps they have in access to resources, the activities they carry out and the limitations they face. This evaluation will make it possible to define the gender action plan for each company and schedule the activities to be carried out so that the project contributes to equal opportunities between men and women.

For this, the EBBF Management Unit must prepare an operational document that indicates the specific methodology that each portfolio company must follow to prepare gender plans, and will provide the necessary technical assistance to the companies that are selected during the diligence phase due*. If the company already has a gender management instrument, it must be adequate to the requirements of this measure.

The gender action plans at the project level are part of the legal documentation that the participating companies must present. These include clear deadlines and responsibilities, as well as solutions in case of non-compliance, and will be incorporated into the action plan and gender evaluation, so progress in this regard will be reported as part of the EBBF impact report that is sent to the UN and other investors.

This measure will be addressed with the following indicators:
- Number of gender evaluations.
- Number of gender action plans.
