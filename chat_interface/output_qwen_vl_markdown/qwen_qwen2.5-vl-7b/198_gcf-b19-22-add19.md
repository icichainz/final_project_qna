### A2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

Unsustainable, the capital city of Mongolia has been experiencing exponential rates of population growth in recent decades since its transition to a market economy in the early 1990s. Most of the rapid growth has occurred in the ger area of US causing unplanned, low density areas in the city which are poorly connected to urban infrastructure (drinking water, waste management systems, etc.). The gers are also characterized by their traditional Mongolian tents (gers) and wooden houses with poor insulation and inefficient heating through coal and biomass burning. This has resulted in about 50% of the population of the capital city, estimated to be about 800,000 people, and 27% of the country's population.

As developed in more detail in Section C of this proposal, the ger areas are, to a significant extent, the result of rural-urban migration. The rapid urbanization has been driven by poverty, unemployment, and lack of access to basic services such as clean water and sanitation. The gers are also characterized by their traditional Mongolian tents (gers) and wooden houses with poor insulation and inefficient heating through coal and biomass burning.

The goal of the project is to reduce greenhouse emissions and air pollution. The Ulaanbaatar Green Affordable Housing® and Residential Urban Renewal Project (AURUP) addresses this by providing the population in selected ger areas a low-carbon and climate resilient alternative—affordable housing in apartments with energy efficient heating systems, water supply, sanitation, and waste management services. It is an integrated demonstration initiative; providing comprehensive, integrated, and affordable solution for vulnerable communities and households.

*Green housing maximizes climate adaptation and climate mitigation (especially on energy efficiency and renewable energy use) while ensuring affordability, sustainability, and resilience. Green housing also considers surrounding areas, community infrastructure, and the environment. The project will consider water supply, sanitation, waste management, air quality, health, water, soil, and air- and sound-development as key parameters. Affordable housing is housing whose cost (in rent or mortgage payments) does not exceed 30% of the gross monthly income of a low-income household.*

### 2. Barriers related to climate resilient housing investments

This set of barriers is discussed in more detail in section C.2. In summary, the main barriers are: (i) limited access to long term and affordable finance for the developer; (ii) constraints on availability of affordable land for buyers (e.g., in the form of long term leases); and (iii) inadequate supply of affordable, climate resilient housing.

The investment gap stems from weaknesses in the financial sector where the cost of capital remains high (typically 19-24% per annum). This is particularly true when considering the affordability constraints for low income households. The circumstances of poor area residents, often low-skilled and low-income migrants from rural areas who will fit into Ulaanbaatar because of economic opportunities, are not conducive to affordable housing solutions. In addition, access to finance will reduce the cost of constructing climate resilient housing units, a central feature of the AHURP Intervention.

### 3. Barriers towards investments in low carbon and climate resilient infrastructure and open space / amenities

The barriers discussed here are similar to those described for housing above. Quantitatively, the public nature of the infrastructure reinforces the gap between the private and social returns on investments in low carbon and climate resilient infrastructures, thereby causing the shortage.

### 4. Other barriers

There are other contributing factors that hinder investments including lack of knowledge on innovative and appropriate technologies and their performance, lack of awareness, etc. These barriers are addressed through capacity building, as described above.

### Concessional loans are critical to overcome key constraints

Concessional loans are used in two different ways to overcome the key affordability constraints:

- To provide a cheap source of funding for developers loans, lowering the cost of new low carbon and climate resilient housing units.
- To provide a cheap source of funding for mortgages, allowing a lower interest rate as an effective way of lowering the monthly mortgage payments.

In addition, the project provides a mechanism to credit land and devolving ownership against the cost of new low carbon and climate resilient housing units. All these mechanisms, taken together, make it possible to overcome the affordability constraint that otherwise might prevent the new low carbon and climate resilient housing units from being realized.

## Banking and Finance in Mongolia

The banking and finance sector in Mongolia is a primary determinant of investments in the real estate market, being (i) a major source of housing and infrastructure finance, (ii) an operational and policy partner in establishing international financial standards, and (iii) a key player in the development of the capital markets. The banking system consists of 14 commercial banks, 195 non-bank financial institutions (NBFs), 186 savings and credit cooperatives, and 17 insurance companies. In addition to these entities, there are 20 foreign banks operating in Mongolia under agreements with the Central Bank Law of 1996; the Bank of Mongolia has focused on price and exchange rate stability while ensuring adequate money supply.

As a result of the economic downturn of 2008-2009, Mongolia's fourth largest domestic commercial bank went into receivership. Following this, new regulations were adopted in 2011 to increase the minimum capital requirements of commercial banks to US$5 million and NBFs to US$312 thousand. The minimum share capital requirement for a Mongolian subsidiary of a foreign bank is US$500 thousand.

## Financial Instruments in Mongolia

Financial instruments in Mongolia have yet to attain sophistication, as significant portion of bank assets remain in cash and Central Bank deposits. The lack of diversification explains the limited liquidity and lack of suitable investment opportunities in Mongolia. The period from 2013-2015 will see a number of new financial products introduced by banks, including structured options and innovative financial products in the Mongolian market. ## Urbanization and the Ger Areas

Ulaanbaatar’s urban population rose from **773,000 in 2000 to 1.380.792 in 2015**, representing an annual average increase of **3.1%**. This growth was due to large migration from rural areas*, due to (i) a series of climate related extreme events, including droughts and floods, which led to the loss of livestock and crops; (ii) the collapse of the state-owned livestock between 2000 and 2010; (iii) the transition to a market economy and economic opportunities developing much more rapidly in urban areas than rural areas; and (iv) the lack of infrastructure development in rural areas. These factors reinforced the geography of the city and generated a vast peri-urban area named ger areas.

## Ger Areas

The ger areas are home to an estimated **774,000 residents in 2019** and are characterized by unplanned plots, unpaid taxes, lack of infrastructure, and poor access to basic services. The ger areas are located on the outskirts of Ulaanbaatar where the ger areas cover about **350 km²**. They account for **60% of Ulaanbaatar’s population and 30% of the country's population**, but they have very little infrastructure, such as water supply, sanitation, electricity, roads, etc., and have developed with very little planning or regulation. Infrastructure is lacking, providing poor access to basic urban services, and are highly exposed to the consequences of climate change. Unlike the residents of many in-migration settlements in urban areas of developing countries, ger area residents have land entitlement and recognized property rights.

Baseline emission calculations for the heating component have been made on the assumption that the heating area is unchanged and a relatively low assumption of heating losses. Below we discuss the change in heating area from the baseline to the project scenario, as well as the assumptions regarding rebound effects.

The new standard for residential buildings in Mongolia compared to the national standard in 2009, gets tents lose 4-5 times more heat, and house loses 3-2 time more heat. With the increased stringency of the new standard, our calculations show that the heating area per person is reduced by about 18%. The average annual energy demand was 461 kWh/m² per year, whereas in the AHURIP case it is 151 kWh/m². The insulation gains assumed in the project are more conservative than those used in the baseline.

In our calculations, we have based ourselves on equal heating in the baseline and project case. That however, is conservative. The average detached house size in our target area is 70m². Through land swap the average housing unit size will be reduced to about 52 m². This means that the heating area per person for a detached house for renting is about 48m².

The average heating area in the baseline case is 30m², which means that the actual heating area is reduced. However, this is compensated by the much higher rate of loss. The amount of heat required in a 50m² house, compared to a 70m² house, will be about 12% less.

The assumptions are conservative—underestimating the extent of the reduction of heating losses and in the case of a shift with wooden houses in the baseline assuming that the heating area is constant while in fact it is reduced considerably. This could lead to an over-estimate of rebound effects, which could lead to a further increase in winter indoor temperatures.

It is important to note that any increase in comfort and rebound effects will be very small in the context of AHURIP. In any case, this will be monitored and if contrary to expectations necessary actions will be taken to reduce such impacts.

### Pricing Structures, Price Controls, Subsidies Available, and Government Intervention

The mortgage market in Mongolia is young and up until recently, the GAF provided free housing to citizens. In mid-1997, GAF and ADB supported the development of legal policy frameworks for the housing sector in Mongolia. In 1999, GAF approved the Housing Law, the National Housing Strategy (NHS), the Housing Privatisation Law and the Community Development Act. The NHS was developed to provide a framework for the development of the mortgage market by shifting responsibilities to the private sector. A few banks had started experimenting with very long-term mortgage lending in 2001/2002, but it was not until 2003 that the first mortgage loans were issued under the Housing Finance Sector Project (HFSP) issued between 2003-2007 of a total of 2.475 million loan amounts to over 16,000 households in the project. The scheme was the first attempt at long-term mortgage lending in Mongolia’s banking system.

The first subsidized housing program was established by the Mongolian Construction and Housing Development (MCHD) in cooperation with the GAF. This provided construction and land for 42,000 houses. In August 2006 MCHD created the Housing Finance Corporation to manage the implementation of the project. Mortgage loans were issued under this scheme from September 2007 until June 2013. The interest rate on these loans was 8.9% which is lower than the market average interest rate. It was also in 2006 that the Mongolian Mortgage Corporation (Mik) was incorporated to provide mortgage lending for up to 50 years. This was a government initiative aimed at providing long-term funds to stimulate a secondary mortgage market. The funding for most of the mortgage programs has been through GAF and ADB support, but in recent years, the private sector has started to play an increasing role by boosting the supply-side of the market by making funds available to the construction sector and the demand-side by subsidizing interest rates on loans.

The AHURP project will be implemented under the Government of Mongolia’s mortgage loan program which mainly caters to upper-middle income households who are able to finance down payments and repay the loans, and had no impact on the lower and middle-income population.

### D.1 Value Added for GCF Involvement

AHURP has significant climate change benefits, both in terms of adaptation and mitigation. Those benefits will, however, only be realized if the project is scaled up to a larger size. While the total financial returns from the project are sufficient to pay the sources of capital used, with the macro-economic situation of Mongolia such that neither the public sector nor the private sector are able to make these investments on their own, GCF support will enable the scaling-up of the project and thus the benefits can be capitalized and a sufficient budget for operation and maintenance can be set aside. In other words, GCF support will make this project financially viable.

The amount of GCF support requested is based on the "minimum concessionary required" argument, as elaborated in Section F.1 Economic and Financial Analysis, and also considers the climate change benefits that justify the GCF funding. The total financial returns from the project are sufficient to pay for the sources of capital used. In addition, concessional loans enable AHURP lifting emission reductions of over 7.9 million CO2eq and total emission reduction of 104,583 tonnes CO2eq per year.

The total amount in grants is $74.4 million and in concessional loans will affect 100,000 total direct beneficiaries, and a total number of 600,000 indirect beneficiaries. The project will also benefit the urban population living in informal settlements and the general population in areas benefit from urban redevelopment investments through community participation activities and the provision of public services.

Apart from the significant direct climate change impacts that the GCF investment would unlock, AHURP is expected to yield further development benefits. This include generating significant learning and knowledge that will support similar projects in Mongolia and other countries with similar socio-economic conditions. The project design criteria for AHURP will provide a reference for future mitigation and adaptation initiatives in the housing sector and urban development in general, at national and regional levels.

Finally, AHURP is expected to create demand for long-term investment in low-carbon urban infrastructure and renewable energy in Ulaanbaatar. GCF is therefore instrumental to ensure that urban development in Ulaanbaatar shifts towards a low-carbon path, and increased climate-resilience of its inhabitants.

Institutional capacity strengthening and technical assistance provided through AHURP will also be essential to ensure the sustainability and continuation of such investments.

### Ulaanbaatar climate

Ulaanbaatar is located at 1350 meters above mean sea level. It has warm, brief summers and long, cold and dry winters. # F.2 Technical Evaluation

Mongolian climate and the long history of energy infrastructure developed under a planned economy present a challenge to the adoption of modern energy efficiency technologies. ### H.1.1 Paradigm Shift Objectives and Impacts at the Fund level

#### H.1.1.1 Paradigm Shift Objectives

The proposed project, by focusing on addressing systemic barriers to low-carbon and climate resilient urban development will address Mongolia’s future GHG emissions and adaptation challenges. By developing a new industry in Ulaanbaatar that will provide affordable housing, eco-district housing capacity and catalyze the development of a new industry in Mongolia, while also providing access to modern urban infrastructure (e.g., water and wastewater services), the project will allow for an increase in economic activity and employment. By increasing access to modern urban infrastructure providing energy, water and wastewater services, solar power generation, and other energy efficient technologies, the project will significantly reduce GHG emissions in these areas.
