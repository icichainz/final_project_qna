## A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 500 words)

Please provide a brief description of the proposed project/programme, including the objectives and primary measurable benefits (see investment criteria in section I). The detailed description can be elaborated on in section C.

### Egypt
Egypt had the world's fourteenth highest Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emissions growth rate in the 1990-2012 period (WRI, CAIT: 2014) and a similar rate of increase is projected for the next decade. The country has a high dependence on fossil fuels for electricity generation; it faces significant challenges in diversifying its electricity sources away from its more than 90% reliance on hydrocarbons (gas and oil). Renewable energy currently only provides less than 1% of Egypt's total primary energy supply, but the government aims to increase this share to around 20% by 2035. The country has a fuel import bill and thus needs to diversify its energy sources.

Despite its potential for renewable energy development, Egypt faces significant barriers in terms of policy implementation and development so far towards the renewable energy target, primarily because there are barriers to the implementation and financing of the projects, namely:
- (i) the uncertainty and high transaction costs associated with the first private renewable energy debt projects,
- (ii) the limited availability of debt from commercial sources, particularly given the required long tenors,
- (iii) the need to establish an adequate infrastructure to integrate renewables into the grid, and
- (iv) the lack of a comprehensive framework for financing.

The GCF-EBRD Egypt Renewable Energy Financing Framework (the Framework) will support Egypt to achieve its goals in two complementary ways: Component 1 – Developing a competitive, efficient renewable energy market that is able to access technical assistance programmes. Component 2 – Scaling up renewable energy investments by blending EBRD and GCF financing and leveraging additional debt financing from international and development financial institutions and in the future from commercial banks as well.

Overall, the Framework will catalyse the development of a competitive, efficient renewable energy market that will allow Egypt to achieve its renewable energy targets while increasing the share of privately owned generation capacity. The projects to be implemented under this framework are expected to generate significant economic benefits for Egypt and contribute to avoided GHG emissions of about 800,000 tCO2e annually once all projects are operational.

GCF Funds will only be used for subprojects with an Environmental and Social Categorisation of B.

**B.1. Description of Financial Elements of the Project / Programme**

The GCF-EBRD Egypt Renewable Energy Financing Framework (the Framework) will support Egypt to unlock its vast renewable energy potential by addressing key barriers hindering its development through two key components:

### Component 1 - Enhancing renewable energy integration, policies and planning

The Framework will include a broad technical assistance program that will help creating the enabling environment for private sector investment in renewable energy. This component aims at addressing three key sub-components:
a) **Enhancing planning and RE integration** – this sub-component aims at ensuring that the new renewable energy capacity commissioned is integrated into Egypt’s national grid, with sufficient transmission capacity to ensure that the network absorption capacity will be sufficient to preserve the Very High Voltage (VHV), High Voltage (HV) and Medium Voltage (MV) voltage levels. This will also ensure that the transmission lines are technically discharged once the new renewable energy generation plants are commissioned. This component will further enhance Egypt’s grid integration capabilities.
b) **Enhancing capacity for administering RE tenders** – this sub-component will seek to provide the relevant institutions (including but not limited to the regulatory and the off taker) with advice on the practical implementation measures needed to ensure that the tendering process is transparent, fair and competitive. This element could be extended to provide support to investors and financiers in the preparation of the subprojects.
c) **Promoting gender equality** – this sub-component will include technical training for relevant projects to strengthen capacity building and awareness raising on gender equality issues with a view to maximize benefits from employment and other economic benefits connected to the development of renewable energy sources.

### Component 2 - Scaling up renewable energy investments

The Framework will support the development and construction of renewable energy projects totaling USD 1 billion. The scale of this investment is expected to have significant multiplier, particularly in renewable energy in Egypt. This investment in the first wave of private renewable projects in Egypt will directly deliver significant renewable capacity and catalyze the development of new renewable energy projects.

The project will leverage EBRD and GCF finance to attract additional investments from international and development financial institutions and commercial banks, as well as equity investments from the project sponsors. The frameworks envisages debt financing up to 75% of total project costs (excluding equity) with a maximum loan amount of USD 120 million which would co-finance up to 35% of total project costs in any single project, with the amount allocated to each project decided based on specific needs. EBRD will provide financing representing up to 35% of the cost of each project and the remaining debt is expected to be provided by other development financial institutions or commercial banks as well as equity investments from a commercial lender. The equity contribution is 25% of the project costs translating into investment from the private sector of USD 250 million.

### B.B Financial Markets Overview (if applicable)

Financial barriers represent a key barrier to the mobilization and scaling up of renewable energy in Egypt, mainly due to (i) the uncertainty and high transaction costs associated with the first private renewable energy projects and (ii) the macroeconomic situation, which results in both an increased cost of capital and limited availability of debt from commercial sources for renewable projects, particularly those that are not yet financially viable or have a low credit rating (e.g., small-scale projects financed by local commercial banks). These barriers manifest themselves either in non-availability of finance or in inflexible grace periods and loan tenors that are not adapted to the characteristics of such investments.

The analysis below refers to the Feed-in Tariff Scheme, which started on October 2014 with Round 1. The tariff was then revised and some of these barriers were addressed by the launch of Round 2 in April 2015 (details are provided in section C.3).

Local commercial banks are prohibited from providing hard currency financing for the renewable energy projects under the feed-in tariff program in Egypt. This restriction is part of wider capital controls set by the CBE that prohibit local commercial banks from providing foreign exchange to private sector entities, including project revenue in EGP (local currency).

The commercial banks also face limitations to finance in EGP. The lack of a sufficient amount of domestic financial finance on offer by local financial institutions hinders the ability of sponsors to invest in renewable energy in Egypt. Finance facilities that address these barriers can contribute simultaneously to increasing market confidence, reducing risk perception and attracting private investors into this sector.

### Section C: DETAILED PROJECT / PROGRAMME DESCRIPTION

#### C1. Context and Background

Egypt’s energy sector faces a number of major challenges. First, there is sustained growth in power demand, driven by economic growth, population growth and changing consumption habits. During the past several years, electricity demand grew on average 6-7% per year. Second, Egypt has an aging generation fleet with a significant portion of its generating capacity nearing or at the end of its life service age and much of the rest has low efficiency and/or availability. Active capacity available at that time was reported to be 22-24 GW against a summer peak of 27 GW in 2014. Second, more than 90% of generating capacity is designed to run on imported fuel oil and natural gas. The country has been facing increasing challenges with the high cost of imported fuels, including paying fuel oil hard currency debt or since 2015, LNG imports. In the short term, the Egyptian government has addressed these challenges by implementing a series of measures aimed at reducing the reliance on imported fuels, such as increasing domestic coal imports, and also securing sufficient generating capacity through (i) the completion of a fast track investment programme which targets stranded assets and (ii) the implementation of a new feed-in tariff scheme.

Nonetheless, the Egyptian government is cognizant of the need for a sustainable change in the country’s energy mix towards renewable energy to both address these challenges and to move it to a more environment sustainable and diverse electricity sector. The Renewable Energy Law was enacted on 21 December 2014 (Law No. 2/2014) with the aim of increasing the importance of renewable power and confirm the target (originally established in 2008) to secure 20% of Egypt’s electricity generation from renewable energy sources by 2020.

In recent years, the Egyptian government has moved both to increase the speed and scale of renewable energy development and to provide a more stable regulatory framework. In this regard, on 17 December 2014 (Law No. 3/2014), Egypt established a robust regulatory framework, built around the Renewable Energy Law (Law 2014/2014) dated 21 December 2014 and two feed-in Tariff Decrees (Prime Ministerial Decree 1974/2014 dated 27 October 2014 (round 1) and Cabinet of Ministers Decree 232/2014 dated 28 January 2015).

This framework sets out several mechanisms to reach Egypt’s renewable targets:

- Publicly owned facilities procured by state agencies or companies and funded from sovereign loans. This is the model used to date.
- Feed-in tariff (FIT) Scheme - Privately owned projects of up to 3 MW relying on private finance, selling power to EETC, relying on a 20/25-year power purchase agreement. These projects would rely on a fixed FIT established by the government for each technology and time to time. The initial target for this programme, launched in October 2014, is 2300 MW of solar capacity and 2300 kWp of solar photovoltaics capacity. No projects under this scheme have entered operation as at mid-February 2017.
- Grid-connected renewable energy projects developed by private sector entities, selling power to EETC on a 20/25-year FIT. These projects would be selected, and the tariff will be determined through a competitive tender process. Some tenders for wind, solar photovoltaics and CSP have been issued.
- Privately owned projects selling through the grid to private off-takers.

The focus of the Framework is on the second of these mechanisms, the feed-in tariff scheme, although projects under the third mechanism are also eligible for a 25% of the FIT subsidy funding under Component 2. The Northem mechanism is currently being supported by a joint EBRD/CTF/EUEG programme called SPREE (http://www.ebrd.org/en/ctf-with-ina/projects/spree-embedded-private-renewable-energy-framework.html).

### C2. Project / Programme Objective against Baseline

#### Emissions baseline
Between 1990 and 2012, Egypt has more than doubled its GHG emissions, i.e., from 134 million tCO2e to 288 million tCO2e (WRI, CAIT). The growth in the energy sector is responsible for most of this increase. This growth rate is significantly higher compared to the growth in global emissions, as well as in OECD and various European countries (Figure 1).

Besides increased industrial output, population growth and other developments, this increase in GHG emissions is due to the fact that power generation from fossil fuels has grown at a much faster pace than for over 90% of the electricity production; hydro and other renewables (solar and wind) represent around 84% and 1%, respectively.

This mix translates into a relatively high grid emission factor at 0.535 tCO2e/MWh, which is likely to increase due to the publicly announced plans to invest in more fossil-fuel based generation capacity in the medium-term.

Additionally, the structure of the power sector such that private sector-driven expansion has been possible. As a result, the majority of new generating capacity has been vertically integrated, and consumption is subsidized. Given the pressures to expand generation, whilst limiting reliance on imported fuels and facing the fiscal burden of subsidies, Egypt has moved to rely more heavily on public sector investment in power generation with private sector involvement, including in expanding generation capacity. This includes the launch of the FIT scheme for private sector renewable developers since 2014.

## Barriers

Renewable energy production in Egypt to date has primarily been hydropower, principally from the Aswan High Dam (2,300 MW – 7% of production). In view of the limited potential for additional hydropower and increasing water scarcity driven by climate change, Egypt must rely on other renewable energy sources to develop its power generation capacity and reduce its greenhouse gas emissions. Egypt has a significant solar resource potential, with an average solar radiation of approximately 2,000 kWh/m²/year, and it has an abundant wind resource in the Suez Gulf area; for details see section C.5). However, there has been very limited development in renewables.

This has all been the case due to the barriers, such as financial barriers, high barriers for first movers, infrastructure constraints and lack of institutional capacity for administering RE tenders. Without addressing these key barriers, the development of a market for renewables and the involvement of the private sector are likely to remain limited and non-sustainable.

## C.3 | Project / Programme Description

The Framework aims at supporting Egypt to unlock its vast renewable energy potential by addressing key barriers hindering its development using EBRD’s proven business model of combining finance with technical assistance and policy dialogue.

The framework will contribute to support Egypt in fulfilling its Nationally Determined Contributions to climate change mitigation as well as the 2036 National Energy Strategy, which aims at increasing renewable energy capacity by 15GW by 2036. The current regulations provide feed-in tariff support for 4,300MW of renewable energy capacity, of which 2.3 GW for solar PV and 2 GW for wind. The Framework will aim to increase this capacity to 10GW by the end of the Framework (which is equivalent to 86% of Egypt’s annual consumption based on the World Bank’s 2013 data).

The programme will include two key components:

### Component 1 – Enhancing renewable energy opportunities, policies and planning

The framework aims at supporting Egypt in developing a technical assistance program that will help creating an enabling environment for private sector renewable investments in Egypt. The technical assistance program will include 3 key sub-components:
- **a) Enhancing planning and RE integration** - this sub-component aims at ensuring that the new renewable energy capacity development is integrated into national planning.
- **b) Enhancing capacity for administering RE tenders** - this sub-component will seek to provide the relevant institutions (including but not limited to the regulator, NREDA and the off-taker) with technical assistance needed for the successful completion of complex procurement processes.
- **c) Promoting gender equality** – this sub-component will include technical training to relevant subjects to strengthen equality of opportunity for women and men to access and benefit from employment and other economic benefits connected to the development of renewable energy.

### Component 2 – Scaling up renewable energy investments

The Framework will support the development and construction of renewable energy projects totaling USD 1 billion with an EBRD-GEF contribution of USD 50 million. The project portfolio reflects the state of the need and opportunity for renewable energy in Egypt. This investment in the first wave of private renewable projects in Egypt will both deliver significant renewable capacity in its own right and catalyse the development of a new industry in the country.

The Framework will leverage on EBRD and GEF finance to attract additional debt financing. The aggregate value of the CCA loans would be up to USD 250 million, with an average loan term of five years and no interest costs in any single project, with the amount allocated to each project decided based on specific needs. EBRD will provide financing representing up to 35 percent per project and the remaining balance will be provided by commercial lenders. The equity contribution is 25% of the projects costs translating into investment from private sector of USD 250 million.

### C5. Background Information on Project / Programme Sponsoring (Executing) Entity

### Responsibilities for the development of Renewable Energy in Egypt

The responsibility for developing renewable energy in Egypt is shared between three entities, under the overall supervision of the Ministry of Electricity and Renewable Energy (MoER).

a) The Egyptian Electricity Transmission Company (EETC). EETC is the owner and operator of the Egyptian high voltage network and acts as single buyer for all wholesale electricity.

b) The New and Renewable Energy Authority (NREA). NREA was established in 1986 as a state agency responsible for developing renewable energy. It is independent from EEHC and the other state-owned electricity companies but reports to the Ministry of Electricity and Renewable Energy.

c) The Egyptian Electric Utility and Consumer Protection Regulatory Agency (EgyptEIRA). EgyptEIRA is the sector regulator, specifying technical standards, issuing generation licenses, approving terms of power purchase agreements, general oversight and regulation of the sector.


### Egyptian Renewable Energy Market

Renewable energy in modern Egypt began in 1970 with the commissioning of the Aswan High Dam. In addition to its effects on irrigation and waterways the Aswan dam also has the capacity to generate 2300 MW of electricity – at the time of construction sufficient for all Egyptian demand. The dam is now generating only about half this amount, due to sedimentation in the reservoirs on the Nile which have a further 580 MW, meets only c.7% of Egyptian electricity consumption. There is limited further potential for hydropower generation and Egypt has no nuclear power plants. In addition, there are no large-scale wind farms or solar farms in operation.

The demand for electricity and the growth of renewable energy worldwide the Egyptian authorities began to promote the development of small scale wind and solar projects. The government has also been promoting the use of biomass and other renewable resources in the world as well as installation levels. In addition there is no shortage of land on which to site wind or solar farms.

The strategy for renewable energy was first set by the Supreme Energy Council’s February 2008 decision to set a target of 20% of electricity production to be met from renewable sources by 2020 (updated to 2022 in the 2015 Energy Strategy). Reflecting market dynamics, the strategy has been divided into two parts: one is the development of small-scale wind and solar projects on two separate tracks; i) a series of state-owned windfarms; and ii) the launch of build-own-operate (BOOT) projects through private sector investment. The first phase of the wind farm project was launched in 2013 with the commissioning at Zafarana, towards the north of the Gulf of Suez, a 200 MW windfarm near the Gabi El Zeit on the Gulf of Suez and a Hybrid gas and solar thermal plant at Kurnetim, with total capacity of 140 MW, of which 20 MW is solar. Together these projects generate less than 2% of annual electricity consumption.

### Current Framework for Renewables in Egypt

The Renewable Energy Law sets out the four separate paths on which the Egyptian authorities are now proceeding to develop renewable energy:

i. State-owned projects: The first path is to develop a number of windfarms initiated before 2011.

ii. Feed-in-tariff (FIT) projects: The Egyptian government issued a decree in October 2014 (Round 1) allowing qualified private operations of wind and solar PV projects of up to 50 MW to secure a long-term power purchase agreement (PPA) with EETC at a fixed tariff. Projects were awarded on a first-come, first-served basis until the end of December 2014. The tariff was revised and some of the terms of the PPA revised by a decree in September 2016 (Round 2). This Framework is expected to be launched soon.

iii. Competitive tenders: A third path to promote renewables is the launch by EETC of competitive tenders for wind and solar projects with capacities up to 50 MW. The first such tender was launched on 30 June 2014, but was withdrawn due to a commercially compliant bid. The first of these projects is the 250 MW Gulf El Zayt BBOO windfarm originally launched in 2009. Subsequently there were a few tenders launched but projects are still to be awarded. The TC element of this framework will aim to assist the authorities in managing the process.

iv. Merchant projects: Consistent with the general trend towards greater liberalisation and private participation in the Egyptian power sector, EETC is also considering the development of merchant wind and solar projects that will deliver their power to the high voltage network but sell it bilaterally to commercial and industrial consumers.

## F.1 Economic and Financial Analysis

A detailed economic and financial analysis will be conducted at project level for investments that meet the Framework's eligibility criteria. The financial analysis of a typical project to be financed under this Framework has been provided to the GCF Secretariat, as noted in [**Funding Proposal**, page 25 of 38].

As mentioned throughout this funding proposal, there are currently financial barriers hampering the mobilization and scaling up of renewable energy in Egypt. These barriers manifest themselves either as non-availability of finance or in inflexible grace periods and loan tenors that are not adapted to the characteristics of such investments.

A comparative analysis will be conducted between the current financing scenarios for the financing of a typical project under the FIT Scheme round 2, which is the focus of this Framework. Such analysis has showed that there is currently no commercial lending available at terms which would ensure the bankability of the projects and attract private investors. The analysis also shows that the GCF will be able to provide concessional funding for the first wave of privately renewable energy projects through the blending of concessional financing with financing from EBRD and other international and development financial institutions.

The derivative analysis on the viability of the first wave of private renewable energy projects in Egypt will show the viability of the sector which will then attract new private investors, local and international. The GCF Secretariat will be able to propose adequate long-term financing with no concessionality needed.

Further economic benefits can also be added at different earlier in the document, such as benefits from the GIZ incentives available under the FIT Scheme round 2 and the FIT Scheme which would set on the basis for further renewable energy support mechanisms, namely through the launch of competitive tenders (that this Framework will also support via Component 1).

Overall, through this Framework, GCF and EBRD financing will demonstrate and scale up commercially viable renewable energy projects in Egypt while catalyzing a paradigm shift not only within the electricity sector in the country but also within the local and international financial markets.

### Gender aspects

The energy market is vital to Egypt's economy and women can in particular benefit from opportunities connected to the development of renewable energy sources. Women in Egypt currently work primarily in services and agriculture, with only very small numbers working in manufacturing or construction. The framework will provide a gender action plan that ensures women can also access employment opportunities in RES engineering, industrial management and core operations. The World Bank (IBRD) has shown that increasing the share of women in the workforce by 10% could increase GDP growth rates, reduce the productivity gap and boost worker's output in Egypt by 3-25 per cent. By strengthening equality of opportunity in this framework, women are expected to be encouraged to apply their talents and seek training opportunities in MEX sectors.

The Gender plan for the Framework will ensure that all consultations with stakeholders will be designed in a gender responsive way and women will be equally consulted and participate in all discussions related to the subprojects. This approach will be incremental and would set a proper benchmark for other projects to follow.

The Framework is accompanied by a Gender Action Plan template for subprojects.

During the relevant period, the implementation status of the Funded Activity and the monitoring of results and impacts of such Funded Activity.

### III. EVALUATION

1. Project evaluation by the EBRD in-house staff and its independent advisors
Throughout the Framework lifecycle, both the EBRD in-house staff and its independent advisors (such as technical and environmental sectoral advisors) will evaluate the success and risks of projects. A final report of each project will contain review and evaluation of the financial and technical performance of projects, capacity building results, climate mitigation and adaptation impact, as well as donor visibility and marketing outreach.

2. Project or Framework level evaluation by the independent EvD Evaluation department
The Independent Evaluation Department evaluates the performance of the EBRD's completed projects and Framework. The Evaluation Department is a department separate from the EBRD’s senior banking divisions (and hence to the developers and managers of the projects they evaluate); therefore, EvD reports solely to the Board of Directors (i.e., to the representations of the shareholder governments). The EvD evaluates the effectiveness, relevance and input efficiency of projects and provides the Board with independent advice on how best to improve project performance. EvD publishes stories and lessons learned. Under the EBRD’s Public Information Policy, EvD publishes summaries of its independent project evaluations.
