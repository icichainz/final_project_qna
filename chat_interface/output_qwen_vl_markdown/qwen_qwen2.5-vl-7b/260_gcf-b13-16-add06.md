Please provide a brief description of the proposed project/programme, including the objectives and primary measurable benefits (see Investment Criteria in section D). The detailed description can be elaborated in section C.

The proposed GCF Trust Fund US$1 billion would scale up the first phase of the World Bank Group (WBG) supported Climate Adaptation and Mitigation Programme for South Asia (CAMPASIA), which focuses on Tajikistan and Uzbekistan (while future phases of the Program, under preparation, will progressively cover the remaining Central Asian countries: Kazakhstan, Kyrgyz Republic and Turkmenistan). The WBG-supported program lays the foundation for an institutional platform for regional cooperation on climate change across a broad range of sectors. This will be the first such platform in Central Asia that will provide access to improved climate change knowledge services for climate change assessment and decision-making at national level; and support for policy dialogue, partnerships, and demand-driven finance for climate action in priority areas common to Central Asian countries.

# C1. Strategic Context

## Regional Context

Central Asian countries experience the highest poverty rates in Europe and Central Asia (ECA). Growth in the region averages 8 percent a year over a decade, with improvements in living conditions and decline in poverty. However, the economic growth of Central Asian countries has been affected by the slowdown in oil prices, the contraction in Russia's economy, and the slowdown of China’s economy. Lower projected growth rates could depress job creation and poverty reduction, as well as undermine social stability, in a region where 40 percent of the population lives below the poverty line (the “Bottom 40 percent”) live in rural areas and rely on agriculture for jobs and livelihoods. For growth and prosperity to continue, Central Asian countries will need to integrate climate change into their development plans and policies and better jobs, which will require improvements in the region's physical, human, and institutional capital.

## Sectoral and Institutional Context

The five Central Asian countries are among the ECA Region’s most vulnerable to climate change and building resilience to climate change impacts is a priority for poverty reduction and shared prosperity in Central Asia. Average temperatures have increased by 0.5°C over the past century (1906-2014), with increases from the south to the north and impacts are already being observed, from melting glaciers in glacial areas (where glaciers have lost one-third of their volume since the 1900s) to droughts and floods in the lowlands (where precipitation has increased by up to 30 percent). For example, Tajikistan’s water supply (the Domestic Product per annum for Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, and Kyrgyz Republic, for instance). Under current greenhouse gas trajectories, climate change is expected to intensify over the coming decades, increasingly affecting the availability of water resources, energy production, agriculture, and ecosystems, with rising costs for key development sectors, such as agriculture, energy, and human health.

In Central Asia, the sectors most at risk from climate change are agriculture, energy, and water resources, with women disproportionately affected. Climate variability and change, in particular, pose significant risks to the agricultural sector, which is a major contributor to GDP (15-20 percent) and employment. Agricultural productivity (including in both rained and irrigated systems) is sensitive to variations in rainfall, hydrologic flows modulated by snow accumulation and melt, system storage, as well as evapotranspiration. Energy systems are also vulnerable to climate change impacts, including the availability of energy resources (in warmer areas in summer), the impact of extreme events on transmission systems, as well as mitigation actions (e.g., in the case of fossil fuels). Across these sectors women in rural areas tend to be more vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. Women are often responsible for water collection and management within households; they change their unequal access to (micro)credit, land, and other economic assets, and lower representation in natural resource management decision-making processes. It is therefore important to identify gender-specific strategies that support sustainable, climate-resilient development.

## C2. Project / Programme Objective against Baseline

Describe the baseline scenario (i.e., emissions baseline, climate vulnerability baseline, key barriers, challenges and/or policies) and the outcomes and the impact that the project/programme will aim to achieve in improving the baseline scenario.

Central Asian countries are ranked as the most vulnerable countries in Europe and Central Asia to the ongoing and projected impacts from climate change, under the combined effect of an extensive natural resource base that is being increasingly stressed by population growth and increasing demands for water management, which climate change will push beyond their limits; and c) low capacity of institutions and individuals to anticipate and manage these risks, due to lack of information on climate impact trends and on solutions to mitigate them. The region's vulnerability is further exacerbated by the fact that most of its water resources in Central Asia are sensitive to climate variability and change, and climate impacts on water provisioning will reveal themselves more quickly than for other regions where water resources are more secure.

The threat of water scarcity will intensify in Central Asia as temperatures climb. Melting glaciers and shifts in the timing of water flows will lead to less water available in summer months, during which most of the region's agriculture is irrigated (90% of the total area under irrigation is irrigated in the region, except for Kazakhstan). Central Asia’s agricultural sector will be significantly impacted by climate change. Hydropower generation will also be at risk as well, hydropower represents more than 90 percent of electricity production in Tajikistan and Uzbekistan, which are both major water catchments. In Tajikistan and Uzbekistan, the countries presently proposed for GCF support, the most pressing climate challenge is water availability.

Reduction in water availability is projected to occur along with a 30 percent increase in irrigation demand (driven by higher temperatures that push up evapotranspiration). Combined with increased heat stress on crops, this will lead to significant reductions in agricultural yields. In Tajikistan, significant agricultural systems can be expected. Agricultural yields could drop by as much as 30 percent in some parts of Tajikistan by the turn of the century while Uzbekistan could face 10-25 percent reduction in yields by mid-century.

### Rural Livelihoods

Rural livelihoods are particularly at risk, with women in the front line. Agriculture, which is critical for the largely rural livelihoods in the region, stands to be severely impacted by climate change, with concerns re: water availability in both irrigated and rainfed systems with reduced runoff during the critical growing season and higher evapotranspiration; heat stress negatively impacting yield of key crops like wheat, further land degradation and desertification; and increased pest pressure on crops (particularly cotton) which often subject to overgrazing and degradation). Around 60 percent of the region’s population (about 50 percent in Uzbekistan and Tajikistan) are directly dependent upon agriculture for their livelihoods. This is based largely on natural, over-depleted assets for their livelihood. Climate change threatens to keep or further push rural populations into poverty, as they lack knowledge and resources to adopt more resilient practices and technologies.

### Food Security

In the context of the GCF’s work in Tajikistan and Uzbekistan, it is important to note that the findings from the CGIAR-CCAFS report on Poverty reports, food security of poorer populations in rural areas can be severely impacted by climate change in several ways: a) availability; b) lower crop yields and more broadly lower productivity of ecosystems (impacting livestock production); c) increased pest pressure; d) reduced water availability; e) increased risk with ecosystem-based livelihoods; f) affordability: there are broad estimates that food price hikes increase poverty rates by 10-25 percent. In Tajikistan, for example, the average household spends about 60% of its income on food (already spend 60 percent of their income on food). Among these, women in rural areas tend to be more vulnerable to the impacts of climate change, given in particular their dependence on natural resources and limited access to productive assets. Women are also less likely than men to have formal education or legal representation in natural resource user associations, which constrain their prospects for adapting resources.

### Within the CPM4ASB Project

GCF funding will provide direct investment support coupled with facilitation and training, to help rural communities design, implement, and sustain investments to expand their productive assets for increased income and improved livelihoods in the face of climate change. For instance, these investments could include: a) improving water management systems; b) increasing crop yields where less predictable in the future; introduce practices to increase/reserve soil moisture (e.g., no-till and other conservation agriculture practices); increase forage production and storage (e.g., pasture restoration, including agroforestry); improve livestock productivity and health; c) diversify livelihoods by testing crops more suitable to new climate conditions (e.g. hot spells, pests and diseases that might become more prevalent in the future); d) improving land management practices (e.g., reforestation, rehabilitation of landslides/mudflows, already on the rise with climate change, among others). GCF funding will provide investment support via sub-grants, thereby reaching the most vulnerable communities in priority areas, including the poorest populations residing on fragile frontier areas and marginalized groups such as women. Particular attention will be paid to gender dimensions, with a target of at least 40% female beneficiaries.

# Detailed Project / Programme Description

## Subcomponent 1.1 Climate Investment Assessment Mechanism (US$0.5 million)

This activity will support the systematic evaluation of climate investments financed under Component 2. The mechanism will be set up by the WBG-UP with CAMPASHP and scaled up in this proposal, for the assessment of GCF-funded climate investments in Tajikistan and Uzbekistan. A regional pool of experts (e.g., national experts with government agencies, consultants, representatives from financial institutions, private sector, universities, academia, and Non-Governmental Organizations) will be established. Approximately six to ten climate investment assessments per year are expected for the two countries. The assessment process will focus on identifying an innovative climate technology or practice, or addressing a climate risk specific to a particular agro-ecological zone, etc. For each of these selected investments, two to three members from the expert pool, including at least one representative from the country concerned, will be invited to provide feedback on the climate investment and draw lessons based on a pre-established assessment methodology. This mechanism will ensure that knowledge spillover from the Program accrue to all five Central Asian countries. In line with the GCF Partnership Strategy, the results of these assessments will be compiled into a report for presentation and discussion at events such as the Annual Climate Knowledge Forum, and will serve as inputs to the GCF’s climate investment strategy. The assessment process is expected to be self-sustaining and catalyze change. In the long term, this material will constitute a regional repository of solutions to provide continued information and foster climate-smart transformation. # RATIONALE FOR GCF INVOLVEMENT

## D1. Value Added for GCF Involvement

Please specify why the GCF involvement is critical for the project/programme.

Pursuing climate-smart development is a critical need for the countries of Central Asia, which are among the Europe and Central Asia region's most vulnerable to climate change. Climate change on-going and projected impacts threaten the achievement of many of Central Asian countries' development priorities (food and energy security, poverty reduction, and environmental sustainability). The agricultural sector in particular will be particularly affected as it depends more directly on vulnerable and depleted land and water resources. In Central Asia, 40 to 75 percent of the population lives in rural areas, with often high poverty rates, and climate change is likely to keep them there.

Climate action in Central Asia is hampered by a lack of resources, where scarce resources are often most concentrated on sectoral issues. Access to climate resources must be facilitated to both avoid and address the region's mounting climate risks. In the framework of the Climate Adaptation and Mitigation Program for the Aral Sea Basin (CAMPASB), the GCF will provide grant financing to support the implementation of climate-smart productivity and improved livelihoods in rural areas, by providing grant resources to the most vulnerable populations.

The CAMPASB program has two main goals: 1) In the short term, enable adaptation measures leading to fringisk-prone areas and marginalized groups such as women; and 2) on a longer-term, enable further climate investments, by enhancing awareness, capacity, evidence base, and institutional frameworks for climate-smart development.

Under Phase I of the program, the GCF support will be critical in accelerating Tajikistan's and Uzbekistan's transition towards climate-smart development and growth. In particular, GCF will help achieve:

- **a) Higher scale of impact:** The majority of the GCF funds will be directed to financing climate-resilient investments that are most likely to have a high impact on poverty reduction in Tajikistan and Uzbekistan. The combination of World Bank and GCF funding will allow the Project to double its expected benefits in these two countries. By supporting the implementation of on-the-ground adaptation measures more quickly, the GCF will help to accelerate the transition towards climate-smart development.
- **b) Greater focus on the most vulnerable populations:** While the CAMPASB Program provides a contribution of funds and credits to rural communities, the GCF grant financing will be directed towards communities that are most vulnerable to climate threats and where the poorest and marginalized groups live. The combination therefore will allow a higher share of vulnerable groups to benefit from climate adaptation measures.
- **c) Transformational impact on regional dialogue and cooperation:** One of the key barriers to developing effective responses to climate change is the lack of coordination between countries, leading to boundary issues, including climate change, with huge inefficiencies in the economy and lack of action at various levels of government and society. By fostering regional dialogue, climate information and knowledge sharing, and joint development of adaptation strategies, GCF will help to develop solutions that are more effective and have greater impact than what individual countries can do on their own.

Average annual temperatures across the region have increased since the mid-20th century by 1°C, with some areas experiencing increases of up to 3°C. The number of days with snow cover has decreased significantly (e.g., from an average of 95 days per year in Ulaanbaatar in the early 1970s to just over 40 days today). Climate variability and change, in particular, pose significant risks to the agriculture sector, which accounts for 20-60% of employment and is characterized by environmental degradation and unsustainable use of natural resources.

The sectors most at risk from climate change are agriculture, energy, and water resources, with women disproportionately affected. In agriculture, which is critical for the largely rural livelihoods in the region, cropping system productivity (including both rainfed and irrigated systems) is sensitive to variations in rainfall patterns and intensity, as well as increased evaporation rates due to higher temperatures and soil evapotranspiration. Among these sectors, women appear most vulnerable to the impacts of climate change, given in particular their dependence on natural resources threatened by climate change as well as their unequal access to resources, particularly in rural areas. A dedicated guide for program monitoring and evaluation is being developed as part of the Program’s Operational Manual. The document will provide guidance on the roles and responsibilities of program beneficiaries and stakeholders, in collecting, analyzing, and disseminating program data and results, including the process for mid-term and final evaluations.

Mid-term review will take place approximately at the end of Year 3 of implementation, with an aim to coincide with mid-term review of the World Bank-financed first phase of the Program, to maximize learning opportunities. The review team will consist of representatives from the GCF Secretariat, World Bank, and other stakeholders. For this review, a team of World Bank Specialists, covering climate instruments/environment management, information technology (including climate-related monitoring systems), sustainable land management, water resources, biodiversity, forest management, social inclusion, gender equality, safeguards, FM and Procurement, will visit national and regional counterparts to review project’s results and plans together with the GCF Secretariat. The review team will also assess the effectiveness of the GCF’s mechanisms.

The Mid-term review will also include a stakeholder workshop (beneficiaries, government agencies, development partners etc.) – which could be held back-to-back with the Forum – to review progress and future orientations for the Program, including issues related to sustainability and resource mobilization. Final evaluation will follow the World Bank’s procedures and methodologies (e.g., preparation of an Completion Report - assessing Results, Lessons learned and challenges).

Please provide methodologies for monitoring and reporting of the key outcomes of the project/program.

National Coordination Units (NCUs) will play a central role in ensuring data and information are appropriately and regularly reported, by beneficiaries themselves, and Facilitating Organizations (who provide technical assistance to communities for the design, implementation and monitoring of climate investments). Dedicated Monitoring Officers will be responsible for collecting and reporting data on project results, including disaggregation as well as number of hectares will be based on direct reporting (e.g., when sub-investment proposals are implemented by beneficiaries themselves). NCUs will also be responsible for ensuring that methodologies and tools already developed and applied by the World Bank and the Climate Investment Funds or similar, such as EX-ADT developed by FAO for carbon balance estimations of forestry/agriculture-related activities, are used in the reporting. The NCUs will also be responsible for providing technical assistance (based, e.g., to references from climate investments in rationale, in economic analysis/feasibility study, in design of strategy/programs), with NCUs responsible for quarterly reporting and the RCU responsible for biannual reporting. The GCF Secretariat will provide guidance on methodologies and tools between World Bank Board Approval (scheduled in early November 2015) and effectiveness of project (envisaged in late April 2016), with support from the Europe and Central Asia Capacity Development Trust Fund, or ECADVPT, which will also finance the preparation of a Project’s Operational Manual.
