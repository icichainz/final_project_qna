### A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

Please provide a brief description of the proposed programme/project, including the objectives and primary measurable benefits to be achieved.

1. The proposed project supports the Government of Zambia to strengthen the resilience to climate change risks of vulnerable smallholder farmers in the country's Agro-Ecological Regions I and II. These regions are facing increasing risks as a result of climate change, which is leading to more frequent and severe droughts with significant direct impacts on the agricultural production in the region. They are also the regions of Zambia which have the highest concentration of poverty and where land tenure agreements are prevalent.

2. This project aims to increase the resilience of smallholder farmers in Agro-Ecological Regions I and II in Zambia, through a multi-faceted approach that includes strengthening capacity for climate risk management addressing risks across key stages of the value chain – planning, inputs, production and post-production. The very high co-financing ensures that this project will shift public financing on agriculture towards climate resilient agriculture through the development of an enabling environment for smallholder farmers to adopt climate-resilient practices while addingitionally like climate information and early warning systems, access to water for smallerholder farmers and tractors, etc.

3. Within the two Agro-Ecological Regions, smallholder farmers in the five provinces (namely, Eastern, Luapula, Muchinga, Southern and Western) will be directly targeted by the project, specifically including the following 16 districts: Mansa, Mufumbwe, Choma, Kabwe, Kapiri Mposhi, Kafue, Mazabuka, Chipata, Nchalo, Sipeme, Siavonga, Namwala, Changamwe, Serenje, Sekele and Mubukwe. The direct beneficiaries will represent approximately 486,153 people.

4. The project aligns with the Government of the Republic of Zambia's (GRZ) key development goals, defined in Zambia Vision 2030, which aims to transform the country into a prosperous middle-income economy by the year 2030. The project is also aligned with the following international frameworks: United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), UNCCD, UNCCD Land Degradation Neutrality (LDN) Strategy, UNCCD Green Carbon Fund, and the Global Climate Change Response Strategy (2016), National Strategy for Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation (REDD+), and the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) Adaptation Communication. ## C.1 Strategic Context

### Economic context

14. Zambia remains a poor country despite recent good economic growth. Poverty rates, particularly in rural areas, are relatively high and the Government has identified poverty reduction as one of the main priorities (NPD, 2017-2022). In fact, the proportion of people living below the national poverty line is estimated at 65 percent while those estimated to be in extreme poverty is about 34.8 percent. The number of people living on less than US$1 a day has been estimated at 7.9 percent compared to urban poverty levels of 27.5 percent. In the 10 largest districts, smallholder farmers live on an average US $1 per day. Though more than 80 percent of the targeted farming households live in these rural areas, these households are among those most vulnerable and least likely to go above the US$1 a day poverty line (World Bank, 2015 Mapping Subnational Poverty in Zambia, 2015). It is evident that the poverty incidence is highly concentrated in Agro-Ecological Region I.

15. The high incidence of poverty is coupled with high food insecurity throughout the country. In 2013, 48.3 percent of the Zambian population was undernourished or food deprived (United Nations Statistics Division, 2014). Between May 2011 and April 2012, 42 percent of rural households experienced food shortages. The average time of food access prolonged to 58 days in 2013 compared to the previous 52 days in 2012 (GRZ, 2013). This is attributed to challenges to challenges of nutrition. About 50 percent of caloric intake was derived from maize and 14 percent from cassava directly or indirectly. The remaining food sources were mainly legumes, tubers, roots, fruits, vegetables, and other micronutrients. Zambian calorie consumption of vegetables, nuts and pulses is around 2 percent (GRZ, 2013).

### Climate risk in Agro-Ecological Regions I and II

16. There are three major Agro-Ecological Regions in Zambia. Region I: In the southern portion of the Southern and Western provinces, one of Zambia's hottest, driest and poorest regions. It is categorized as a low rainfall area, where soil erosion is common due to heavy rains by December/January. The region has poor soils with high acidity and some fishing activities are undertaken. This region is particularly vulnerable to climate change, and is categorized as a drought-prone area.

17. Region II: Has three subregions (Ibii and Ibii), and Ibi) and is a medium-rainfall belt running East-West through the central and northern parts of Zambia. It is an area with relatively good soils and receives more rain than Region I. It has the most favorable agro-ecological conditions in terms of rainfall, soil quality and absence of the tense tree. There is also ample irrigation potential from rivers and dams. The region is characterized by a high population density. In the western part of Region II, it is differentiated from other parts of the region. It can be characterized as a low-rainfall area in the western part of the region. This area has poor soils with high acidity and some fishing activities are undertaken. The lower rainfall and sandy soils, poorer road and market infrastructure, and higher population density, drought and miller are mainly grown as staple crops along with cassava, with some maize also being grown. This drought prone area is also subject to climate change.

18. It is evident that severe weather/climate events have led to significant drops in GDP growth, especially in the relatively dry regions I, Ibii I and II. The strength of the 2015-16 El Niño and severe drought, comparable in strength to the 1983-84 El Niño event, had a devastating impact on agriculture, especially maize production, Zambia's most important agricultural sector, and it reduced its contribution to GDP. As a consequence, a surge in poverty rates, particularly among smallholder farmers who depend almost exclusively on rain-fed agriculture and have little or no coping mechanisms in regions I, Ibii I and II is expected (World Meteorological Organization, El Niño La Niña Update, 12 May 2016).

The second component is the Strengthening Climate Resilience Project for Zambia (SPCR). The second component is support to participatory adaptation. This component will fund all planned participatory investments in the Barotse sub-basin, including facilitation and strengthening of community decision-making; technical support to Western Province and district councils; and increased access to agricultural inputs and extension services. The third component is institutional support for participatory adaptation. This component will fund actual participatory adaptation investments in the Barotse sub-basin. ## Cassava

Cassava is a suitable input for a wide range of products for human consumption as well as animal feed. Zambia's National Cassava Development Strategy (2015-2019) notes that cassava can be used to replace maize in the production of ethanol, bio-diesel and other bio-fuels, which are currently priced high. With the advent of the FISP, cassava has become a more attractive crop for smallholder farmers as it is less susceptible to droughts than maize.

## Copewas

Copewas have a high market value. While the crop is produced in all ten provinces, there are limited supplies and demand. The main reason for this is that there is limited supply of improved seed and knowledge (see Appendix X in the Feasibility Study). In the face of climate change, there is a need to provide resilient varieties to small-scale farmers and knowledge on how to grow copewas.

Soybean has a positive belief among farmers that soybeans are unreliable. Libungu et al. With the growing livestock market, demand for soybean is increasing as it is the major ingredient in stockfeed.

Inadequate weather and water monitoring infrastructure, including district-level gaps, which limits data collection, analysis and provision of timely advisories for agriculture and water management

56. Historically, the Government of Zambia has been providing funding to the meteorological department to procure and install weather stations in various parts of the country. However, this is inadequate due to a rapid climate change and limited availability of weather information systems (including synoptic and agrometeorological stations, water level monitoring, etc.) in Zambia. Currently, there are only 12 weather stations in the country, with 8 of them located in the northern regions and 4 southern regions around Agro Ecological Regions I and II. Recently, MODZ and WARMs have received support through several donor projects to install weather stations in some parts of the country. However, this is still insufficient for agricultural purposes.

This is not sufficient to monitor weather and water availability at the block level, which would provide the specificity needed for agricultural planning and decision-making. The lack of weather information systems also hinders effective water resource management, and to provide the foundation for insurance schemes. Additionally, the scientific and technical knowledge required to interpret this data is lacking in Zambia. This results in a situation where vulnerable communities and the agricultural value chain is weak. This is partly due to a lack of computational equipment and software (model code and weather bulletins), but also because there are no trained meteorologists or programmers who can use these systems to generate early forecasts and translate/transfer the forecast information for use in the agriculture and water sectors. This means that there is a shortage of skilled forecasters and meteorologists who can work on this task, as well as a lack of capacity to disseminate weather advisories as soon as internationally and publicly available forecasts are to be used for agriculture and water management.

Weak institutional coordination leading to limited participation, sharing and disseminating of weather and climate information in the agricultural sector and water management

## D1. Value Added for GCF Involvement

The contribution of the GCF is critical for this project given the country's current economic situation. While some government funds have been used to provide support to farmers through extension services, as well as generate and deliver climate information and support water management, the current public expenditure is limited and only covers a small portion of the total costs required to achieve the project’s objectives. The majority of smallholder farmers lack more resilient and diversified livelihoods. As of 2014, the Government of Zambia had a debt public service ratio (DPSR) that increased steadily every year since 2011. Therefore, the Government is unable to increase investment in this area.

The GCF involvement is critical to triggering a true and sustainable shift in livelihood practices in the two targeted Agro-Ecological Regions. While other projects have addressed different pieces of the value chain in isolation, this project will address all three components of the value chain simultaneously: from seed selection – from inputs production to post-production, in an integrated way. This approach ensures that the market systems are more resilient and sustainable over time.

The GCF funding will provide the catalyst to move smallholder farmers away from and out of their current cycle of vulnerability and poverty. The project will also help them access new markets for their produce and other untapped opportunities. Therefore, the initial contribution, which cannot be supported through other sources, will leverage further investment, incentive government agencies to invest in a different and progressive work attitude, and increase the overall resilience of smallholder farmers.

The additional activities supported by this project need to be urgently implemented with additional finance in order to ensure that vulnerability of the poor populations is addressed before the conditions get worse. These vulnerable populations are characterized as being highly dependent on agriculture for their livelihoods; they suffer from climate change, have insufficient resources to invest in training, information and water services, inputs for resilient agricultural livelihoods and practices, or post-production support.

In the case of activity 2.2, Financial Internal Rates of Return (FIRR) are negative even with GCF funding; however, the project will provide long-term socio-economic and environmental benefits that justify the GCF funding request for this activity is justified.
