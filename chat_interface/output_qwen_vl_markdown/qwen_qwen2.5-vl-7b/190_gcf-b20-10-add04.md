A. Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

1. The proposed project supports the Government of India to enhance the resilience of vulnerable coastal communities to climate change through ecosystem-based adaptation (EBA). The project combines GCF grant finance with significant co-financing from the Indian government and other development partners, as well as national and community-based approaches to adaptation into coastal management and planning by the public sector, the private sector, and local communities. It will support the implementation of EBA in 10 landscape areas across India, reducing hazards, especially storm surges, supporting climate-resilient livelihoods, and enhancing climate-risk information systems.

2. The project objective is to enhance the resilience of the lives and livelihoods of the most vulnerable populations, particularly women, in the coastal area of India to climate change and extreme events, using an ecosystem-centred and community-based approach. This will be achieved by strengthening the adaptive capacity of communities and enhanced livelihoods of the most vulnerable people, communities and regions, and improved resilience of ecosystems and coastal areas.

In addition, the proposed project will contribute towards the achievement of climate priorities outlined in India's National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC) and India's Intended Nationally Determined Contribution (INDC). The project will be aligned with India’s emerging strategic investment priorities for GCF, as well as India’s national development priorities. It is consistent with the GCF Strategic Plan 2014-2018 and the aforementioned priorities; and will also be aligned with the work program of UNDP as an Accredited Entity of the GCF.

3. GCF and other leveraged resources will be used to resource at national, state and community levels to enhance capacities for ecosystem-based adaptation in coastal areas across India. The project will support the development of enabling policies and catalytic climate action in all of India’s coast States and Union Territories. Specific ecosystem-based adaptation interventions will be implemented in 10 landscape areas across India, including Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Maharashtra and Odisha, with pathways to replication and scale across all coastal states, and learning shared across the South Asia region.

The project is expected to benefit approximately 25 million people living in vulnerable landscapes of these states, with more than 1,700,000 direct beneficiaries in the target landscapes whose households’ current livelihoods are affected by climate change. The project will also benefit an additional 34 million people who live within 5 km of the beneficiaries living in these landscapes, who will benefit from the mitigation of economic damages and losses associated with extreme weather events.

The proposed investment demonstrates a high degree of economic efficiency, with an economic rate of return in excess of 50% over its lifetime. The project is also expected to generate significant co-benefits for biodiversity conservation and protection activities. The project was designed through extensive stakeholder consultations, including engagements with civil society organizations (CSOs), local communities, the private sector, and other relevant stakeholders.

The project implementation phase was undertaken by a Technical Working Group, including representatives of the MoEFCC and the three State Governments involved in the Project. The Project is supported by a strong team of technical experts from GCF accredited partners, technical experts and representatives of civil society as well as final submission package, including all annexes, was approved, and arrangements were discussed for project implementation and operations and monitoring and evaluation (M&E) during the final approval meeting; the NDA issued a letter of no objection.

# B.1. Description of Financial Elements of the Project / Programme

The cost of implementing the project to enhance climate resilience of vulnerable tidal coastal communities is estimated at $203 million. Of these costs, $85.64 million (or 42%) are from GCF funding and $79.5 million (or 39%) of costs, with the remaining 67% coming from leveraged co-financing of $86.85 million. A grant financing mechanism is also proposed to support the implementation of ecosystem-based adaptation interventions in coastal areas for the proposed urgent ecosystem-based adaptation interventions that will enhance the public good and benefit vulnerable communities. The project area includes three major coastal states, namely Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, and West Bengal, where climate change adaptation across India's coastal zone, with major gains for resilience in the three target States whose coastal populations are vulnerable to extreme events and slow onset climate impacts. Historically, the focus in India is on hard infrastructure such as sea walls and breakwaters to protect against storm surges and other coastal structures to directly increase protection from waves and flooding. However, ecosystem-based solutions are recognized as a more sustainable approach for enhancing resilience of communities living along coastlines to climate-adaptive livelihoods.

The project will follow the pathway to scale for ecosystem-based adaptation across all of India's 13 coastal states, islands and union territories, where coastal districts house 14.2% of India's total population. According to India’s National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC), 60 million people in India are currently living in areas vulnerable to the impact of accelerated sea level rise, with other climate hazards including more frequent and intense tropical cyclones, heavy rainfall events, droughts, and heat waves. The coastal communities in the targeted states are also impacted by climate change – including slow onset impacts of sea-level rise, ocean warming and acidification, already observed changes in temperature and rainfall patterns, and extreme weather events such as floods and droughts.

The project will focus on three major coastal states, namely Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, and West Bengal. For this project, baseline development investments will have decreasing success in delivering the expected development gains due to climate change impacts, which are already being observed across these states. These include vulnerabilities. Other initiatives in coastal areas, both ongoing and completed, are either rooted in integrated coastal zone management or focus on specific adaptation measures such as mangrove restoration and reforestation focusing on climate risks, or address climate risks through an engineering approach featuring "hard" infrastructure at the coast. The project will build upon existing efforts by the Government of India (GoI) under the Coastal Zone Management Programme. Existing initiatives relying on hard, engineered solutions will fail to harness the potential for ecosystem-based adaptation in coastal areas and, secondly, provide sustainable alternatives to fishing and farming livelihoods threatened by climate change.

The 6 ecosystem-based adaptation (EBA) interventions to be implemented through this project are in support of the public good and benefit vulnerable communities. These include mangrove restoration and reforestation, which will help support new livelihoods for poor fishing and farming communities threatened by slow onset climate impacts such as sea-level rise and ocean warming and acidification. The EBA interventions also address poverty and promote rural development. However, these investments are being undermined by the impacts of climate change on coastal ecosystems. These challenges are addressed in detail within Section 3.2 of the Feasibility Study Annex – Item II). Through co-financing this project, government at national level and in the three target states will be able to implement EBA interventions that address these challenges.

However, the public sector lacks the knowledge and technical capability to pursue an ecosystem-based approach to adaptation. The proposed interventions are designed to break away from the current development paradigm shift, embedding a new ecosystem- and community-based approach to coastal adaptation in the way India's coast is managed. This will be achieved by providing financial resources for the implementation of EBA interventions and maintenance on an ongoing basis post-project. Given that the proposed interventions do not lend themselves to traditional project management approaches, there are no direct costs associated with the generation or recovery of revenue or costs. Instead, the expected small-scale financial returns that will be generated from investments in ecosystem-based diversification will largely be experienced at the local (household and community) level and will underpin the financial viability and sustainability of the climate-adaptive livelihoods that we will promoted through the project.

# C1. Strategic Context

## Climate change risks and impacts on India’s coast

### 18. The Indian coastline is expected to be amongst the regions most affected by climate change globally, negatively affecting the approximately 250 million people (14% of the country's population or 3.5% of the global population) who live along its shores.

#### 19. Three target states—Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra and Odisha—have been selected for undertaking on-the-ground activities focused on protecting and restoring coastal ecosystems and promoting livelihoods activities to enhance the resilience of coastal communities (see Section 2.7 of the Feasibility Study in Annex II). These target states were selected based on their vulnerability to climate change impacts, as well as their geographical diversity across India’s coastline, including both east and west coast areas. In addition, the selection of these three states enables GEF-6 to address a range of key coastal ecosystem issues: (i) high biodiversity; (ii) low adaptive capacity due to biophysical vulnerability to climate impacts; (iii) coastal ecosystems (including mangroves, seagrass, saltmarshes, coral reefs and sea grass beds); (iv) livelihoods of communities living in these areas; and (v) the economic output of states with very low, and medium per capita incomes). Output 3 of the project will create platforms to scale up the model across all coastal zones across India’s nine coast states, two coastal Union Territories and two island territories and their 73 coastal districts.

### 2.2 Rainfall: Similary, India is projected to experience an increase in precipitation.

The CMIP model projections indicate an increase in overall rainfall of up to 20% or more in the western regions, while the rest of the country is predicted to experience a decrease in rainfall. The frequency and intensity of heavy rain events are expected to increase in the variability and unpredictability of monsoon rainfall affecting agriculture.

### 3.1 Sea Level Rise: India's coastline is projected to rise by up to 20 cm during the 21st century under high emissions scenarios.

This will contribute to sea level rise, which is expected to proceed at a rate of up to 10 mm yr−1 during the 21st century under a high emissions scenario. India has 7500 km of coastline – including islands – is home to more than 30% of its population and accounts for about 40% of its GDP.

### 4.2 Extreme Events: India has been adversely affected by natural disasters and extreme weather events over the last century, with a significant increase in the frequency and intensity of such events expected during this century.

A marked increase in high rainfall incidents resulted in localized flooding affecting more than 1.8 million people and caused damages of over $3 billion in 2015. It was estimated to be India's costliest natural disaster and eighth in terms of cost globally, making it the most expensive for 2015[1]. The frequency and intensity of droughts and floods are predicted to increase[2] under future climate change scenarios[3], resulting in considerable losses and damages, as well as increased vulnerability. India is also vulnerable to coastal flooding. Extreme weather events will also increase damage to coastal infrastructure, with storm surges and a one-meter sea-level rise expected to cause $10 billion of damage annually by 2050.

There is a lack of coordination between the various sectors involved in coastal planning and implementation of measures for climate change adaptation within the coastal zone. In particular, there is insufficient coordination or climate change adaptation and disaster risk reduction at both an ecosystem-level perspective through integrated coastal management (ICM) and at a sectoral level by multiple stakeholders including government, local communities and the private sector. This results in: i) coastal development occurring in areas where it is not sustainable; ii) lack of consideration for climate change impacts on coastal ecosystems and consequently iii) greater costs of losses and damages caused by current and future climate change impacts. Available resources are often insufficient to address these challenges, and there is a lack of capacity to factor this into planning and decision-making processes within national, state and district-level government institutions. The Ecosystem Approach (EA) is not widely used in coastal management planning as it has no potential benefit of retroflection through the CAMPA fund, and at state level, EDA is not usually considered as a complementary approach to ICM. This is because there are limited resources for implementation, especially after extreme weather events and severe coastal erosion. Indeed, the potential for EA as an adaptation measure is not widely known or understood by decision-makers in India’s coastal states. The lack of capacity for planning and financing, consequently, development planning along the coast of India does not take climate risks into account in a systematic manner.

It is systemic that there are no national-level policies on ecosystem-based approaches to coastal management and financing. Cooperation management agreements between government, communities and CBOs/NGOs – all of coastal ecosystems – are also lacking. Coastal interventions for climate change adaptation thus largely rely on “top-down” and “hard” infrastructure solutions rather than the implementation of a combination of blended approaches with “soft” approaches such as EA in design considerations, and involve communities in long-term co-management.

# RATIONALE FOR GCF INVOLVEMENT

## D1. Value Added for GCF Involvement

### 151. GCF resources are sought to invest into the incremental costs of climate change adaptation and bring about a paradigm shift in coastal zone management in India.

The coastal areas of India are undergoing rapid economic development owing to industrial expansion and urbanization. At the same time, large proportions of the population of such areas depend on natural resources for their livelihoods, which are increasingly being affected by extreme weather events. The GCF is promoting economic and social development at national and local scales with a focus on improving rural livelihoods through climate-resilient investments in coastal zones.

### 152. Addressing the information, technical, financial, and institutional barriers to investments into EBA:

Key barriers impeding adequate consideration of climate change impacts and EBA potential in baseline investments include: limited awareness among investors about the importance of considering climate change impacts; lack of technical capacity concerning the role of EBA in enhancing adaptive capacity; limited technical and financial resources for developing and implementing adaptation measures; limited institutional capacity to develop and implement climate-resilient value chains; and limited institutional capacity to monitor, evaluate, and report on the effectiveness of adaptation measures.

### 153. Crowding in public and private sector finance and mobilizing complementary land-based investments for EBA:

GCF resources will be used to crowd in public and private sector finance to build the resilience of vulnerable coastal communities through co-financing towards ecosystem restoration, resilient livelihoods and value-chain support. This includes financial support for: (a) developing climate-resilient value chains; (b) providing technical assistance and maintenance, and sustained impact post-project. The overall budget for this project includes committed financing from the GCF, as well as other sources of funding such as the World Bank’s Forest Carbon Partnership Facility Forest and Climate Change. The unlocking of additional funds for large-scale EBA will be catalyzed in a number of ways: (a) through the development of climate-resilient value chains; (b) through the creation of enabling conditions for potential investors; (c) changing policies and regulations to create an enabling environment for EBA investors, and (d) providing technical assistance and maintenance, as well as sustained impact post-project. This will help manage EBA investments. This will be complemented by mainstreaming of climate change adaptation into public and private sector investment decisions in the coastal zone.

#### E4.2 Financial, economic, social and institutional needs

**214.** India is a lower middle-income country (IMF) with deep disparities in wealth and wellbeing and high levels of income inequality. More than 30% of the population lives on less than $1.90 per person day at purchasing power parity. Given this large number of people, GoI is committed to addressing poverty and social inclusion as a key priority. The current focus of government expenditure is focused on socio-economic development activities to address the widespread poverty in India. However, there are significant gaps between urban and rural areas, and between the most vulnerable groups and associated economic infrastructure, rather than on building the climate resilience of the poorest communities along the coastlines.

**215.** Poverty, gender inequality and social exclusion – especially as a result of the caste system – are most prevalent in rural coastal areas. The poor and marginalised communities living in these areas will be disproportionately affected by interventions will undertaken contain significant numbers of members of scheduled castes and tribes who suffer discrimination from poverty because of their caste or tribal background. These communities also have limited access to resources, education, health care, community services, women, children and the elderly are amongst the most vulnerable to disaster impacts such as food and drought. Lack of access to paid employment affects these groups’ ability to adapt to climate change and build resilience. The poor lack access to land and basic infrastructure and services. Given the factors described above, there is an urgent need to undertake interventions that will enable these communities to better manage their natural resources, livelihood assets and their lives and livelihoods from the impacts of climate change.

**216.** There are also technical and institutional capacity needs to enable adaptation strategies for climate change adaptation in coastal areas. This includes building awareness of linkages between sectors related to coastal development currently results in little collaboration between relevant institutions and stakeholders, which limits the ability of communities to adapt effectively to climate change impacts. Building community and other stakeholders for planning, coordinating and implementing adaptation interventions to build resilience is also a key challenge. There are limited institutional arrangements for the integration of ecological infrastructure and EIA as cost-effective and complementary means of adapting to climate change when incorporated into coastal development projects. This limits the ability of communities and government officials on planning for climate-resilient coastal development to address climate-induced impacts.

**217.** The GoI and the private sector are not currently investing significantly in ecosystem-based adaptation measures in coastal areas of India. This is as a result of limited technical capacity and undervaluation of the adaptation benefits provided by ecosystems, which results in low levels of investment in these sectors from both public and private sources. Moreover, adaptation interventions – such as those to be implemented under this project – largely result in the generation of public goods. There is thus little incentive for the private sector to invest in such measures, while companies are more likely to focus on short-term gains rather than long-term benefits. The private sector cannot afford to invest in costly activities such as restoration or establishing new infrastructure for aquaculture or agri-processing industries. In addition, there are no incentives for the private sector to invest in ecosystem-based adaptation and there are few incentives in place for effective co-management of ecosystems. Without grant funding, largely ecosystem-based adaptation measures will not be implemented by the private sector. This is a key challenge required for maintaining and restoring coastal ecosystems to buffer disasters and as the basis for sustainable livelihoods.

### F2. Technical Evaluation

#### Project Description
This project will support implementation of ecosystem- and community-based adaptation interventions to enhance the resilience of coastal communities in Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, and Maharashtra against climate changes in coastal areas of India.

The approaches that will be followed have been tailored to the local environmental and socio-economic contexts of the target states, after careful consideration of the climate vulnerability assessments. The project will also draw on lessons learned from existing adaptation interventions and global best practices on ECA, climate-adaptive livelihoods and capacity building for planning adaptation interventions.

#### EbA Approaches
The EbA approaches to be implemented have been identified based on their potential for addressing current and predicted climate change threats for coastal India, as demonstrated through lessons learned on coastal EbA from around the world. These include: a) restoration of mangroves; b) coral reef restoration and enhancement; c) creation of oyster reefs; d) mangrove-based aquaculture; e) afforestation and reforestation; f) protection of vulnerable ecosystems such as seagrass beds, saltmarshes, and mangroves; g) conservation of water quality and ensure ecosystem health; and h) construction of permeable dams.

#### Mangrove Restoration
A total of 10.57 ha of mangroves will be undertaken in all three target states (4.75 ha in Andhra Pradesh, 3.35 ha in Maharashtra, 2.47 ha in Odisha). This restoration will use a number of complementary techniques including: i) restoration and hydrological functioning from upper catchments; ii) creation of nursery beds for mangrove seedlings; iii) planting of mangroves on degraded sites; iv) enforcement of restrictions on resource extraction; v) protecting vulnerable ecosystems such as seagrass beds, saltmarshes, and mangroves; vi) construction of permeable dams.

#### Coral Reef Restoration
A total of 10.57 ha of coral reefs will be restored in all three target states (4.75 ha in Andhra Pradesh, 3.35 ha in Maharashtra, 2.47 ha in Odisha). This restoration will use two techniques, viz coral gardening and artificial reef creation through structure placement. These are expected to provide a suitable environment for the corals to be established and grow into large colonies of corals within a short period of time.

#### Seagrass Restoration
A total of 10.57 ha of seagrasses will be restored in all three target states (4.75 ha in Andhra Pradesh, 3.35 ha in Maharashtra, 2.47 ha in Odisha). Onshore nurseries will be used for this restoration as it has been demonstrated internationally as being the most effective technique to restore seagrass beds. Seagrasses have also been successfully restored using the Tucano Island (state of Tamil Nadu) and showed survival rate of over 90% within a short period.

#### Saltmarsh Restoration
A total of 700 ha of saltmarshes will be restored across the three target states (200 ha in Andhra Pradesh, 350 ha in Maharashtra, 150 ha in Odisha). This restoration will include: i) land-based restoration; ii) restoration of tidal flushing regimes; iii) removal of sediment from the saltmarsh area; iv) planting of native species and/or transplanting seedlings or mature plants from donor sites. In addition, seagrass (imported by bottom trawling) has been successfully restored along the Tucano coast (state of Tamil Nadu) and showed survival rate of over 90% within a short period.

#### Watershed Restoration
Watershed restoration and conservation will be undertaken in a total of 3.55 ha across all three target states (2850 ha in Andhra Pradesh, 400 ha in Maharashtra, 500 ha in Odisha). A number of complementary techniques will be used to restore the water quality and quantity as well as the ecological health of each site. These techniques will include: i) soil conservation through control of gullies and other badly eroded areas; ii) construction of check dams and small weirs; iii) creation of wetlands and ponds; iv) land management including breaching of silted estuaries and enhancing natural recharge of soil water and aquifers.

# G.1 Risk Assessment Summary

## 298 The project seeks to increase the effectiveness of existing initiatives along India’s coast that promote climate change adaptation and/or ecosystem protection and restoration (particularly the UNDP-Sindhudurg and EGREE projects) and draw on lessons learned from these initiatives, as well as other coastal adaptation projects in India. Project risks associated with project implementation are also mitigated through the executing agency’s well-established risk management framework, which includes a comprehensive risk identification process, robust financial and programme monitoring systems as well as strong technical oversight. The overall risks for the project are considered to be manageable.

## 299 The main risks to project implementation are technical, operational, institutional, social and environmental. Risks associated with the technical and operational aspects of the project may affect the success of ecosystem restoration and livelihood support activities. Technical risks could also result in poor design or application of tools such as the Coastal Vulnerability Index (CVI) and the Coastal Resilience Calculator (CRC), and the Coastal Calculator. Institutional risks such as limited coordination among project stakeholders and weak political will to implement the project may affect the success of project implementation, including the adoption of project interventions. Social risks include poor sensitization – and involvement – of participating communities in project activities, which could result in low community acceptance and participation, affecting the long-term sustainability and viability of project interventions. Environmental risks such as extreme climate events can also result in losses and damage caused to project interventions, reducing their efficacy and success.

## 300 Several mitigation measures have been designed to address these risks. The project will invest in community mobilization activities that will ensure that communities are adequately informed about the project and appropriate repurposing of project interventions during the implementation phase. Project activities will be undertaken in close collaboration with local communities through co-management structures that include clear roles and responsibilities for all stakeholders involved, as well as a strong monitoring system to be introduced for EIA evaluations that take into account local socio-economic and environmental conditions, with due consideration given to the needs of vulnerable groups such as women and children. Community participation will be facilitated through the project management structure (see Section C.7) as well as through the coordination mechanism established under the UNDP-Sindhudurg project.

## 301 Inclusive and participatory planning processes – initiated during the development of this project proposal – will continue throughout the implementation phase to promote ownership and buy-in from communities and government agencies involved in project activities. A risk management framework for the project has been developed to specifically address environmental and social risks that may arise during project implementation.
