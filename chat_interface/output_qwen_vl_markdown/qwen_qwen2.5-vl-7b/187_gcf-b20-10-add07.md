### A2 Project/Programme Executive Summary (max.300 words)

Please provide a brief description of the proposed project/programme, including the objectives and primary measurable benefits (see investment criteria in section E). The detailed description can be elaborated in section C.

The 'Biomass Energy Programme' in the South Pacific (hereinafter the "Programme") originates from the Fijian government's endeavours to control disastrous invasive alien species - Spottedkeeps camarulata (hereinafter African tulip tree) – that have been introduced into Fiji. The proliferation of this invasive species has caused uncontrollable invasive species have had a huge threat to livelihoods of vulnerable farming communities. Nevertheless, the government was reluctant to commence eradication of African tulips without finding the way to make economic use of it as a biomass fuel source. This is because the Fijian government has been committed to climate change mitigation in charge. The Programme finally provides the government a highly effective solution that utilizes African tulips as a biomass fuel resource. Supporting to the country needs, the Programme will supply low-carbon biomass as reliable base load power generation and, hence, enable the Fijian govt to PNDC, which will contribute to energy paradigm shift in South Pacific Small Island Developing States (SIDS) as a whole.

Based on the background above, the Programme is structured by three components in total:
1. Development of four biomass power plants;
2. Development of one wood pellet plant; and
3. Technical assistance

The Programme satisfies two results areas of 'energy access and power generation' and 'forestry and land use', through which it plays a pivotal role in achieving NDC Nationally Determined Contribution goals. For NDC implementation, the Programme will contribute to COP21 Paris Agreement on climate change by biomass deployment including sustainable biomass planting as the largest mitigation corridor: 210,000GCO2/year mitigation target in biomass sector cannot be reached without the Programme. Most importantly, the Roadmap demonstrates how well the Programme upholds country ownership by integrating needs of its target countries through close consultation with NDA. Fiji will harness the Programme as a steppingstone towards its reaching 100% renewable energy target by 2030.

The Programme will also contribute to establishing sustainable land use management systems through eradication of alien invasive species and reforestation of degraded lands. Moreover, it will plantations over lands idled and degraded by dwindling sugarcane industry and disruptive weather patterns affected by climate change. These activities fundamentally aim to convert land that has been long left idle, wasted or abandoned into productive use. The Programme also aims at restoring the livelihoods of communities once again vitalized for ensuring socio-economic benefits to vulnerable local communities. In addition, the Programme ensures to recover local food production chains disturbed by African tulip trees, which finally confirms the Programme as a genuine win-win solution.

# C.1 Strategic Context

Please describe relevant national, sub-national, regional, global, political, and/or economic factors that help to substantiate the need for the proposed project/programme.

The proposed Programme is designed to address various needs of South Pacific SIDS, namely Fiji and PNG, in an integrated manner. That is, the Programme will enable transformation of South Pacific SIDS into countries with not only sustainable energy systems but also improved livelihoods and well-being of their citizens. The specific activities under the Programme would include eradication of invasive plants, utilization of idle and degraded lands, and diversification of the countries' energy portfolios.

## Fiji

### Country Context:
- **Area**: Approximately 18,332 square kilometers.
- **Population**: About 905,000 people living in three largest islands: Viti Levu and Vanua Levu. Although Fiji is relatively developed and a relatively large country among South Pacific SIDS, it confronts various challenges that other smaller countries face such as limited resources, high population density, and vulnerability to natural disasters which often affect health care, water resources, agricultural activities and infrastructure located near the coastal regions of the islands.
- **Cyclone Winston**: A category 5 cyclone, swept through Fiji Island in 2016. During this disaster, 43 people were confirmed dead, 156 people injured, and at least two people missing. Communication systems and power grids have been severely damaged. The impact of the cyclone on a single power station may cause a cascade impact on the nationwide electricity supply.

### Energy Sector:
- **Energy Mix**: Fiji mainly utilizes hydro and diesel power plants for electricity generation; the power generation mix in 2015 was 44.90% hydro, 52% diesel and heavy fuel oil, 0.63% with wind.
- **Energy Challenges**: The energy sector is highly influenced by external conditions such as seasonal fluctuations in precipitation rate and global energy prices. Therefore, the two energy sources are growingly becoming uneconomic; given the increase in public expenditure on electricity generation and transmission, the Department of Energy announced Fiji National Energy Policy 2015-2030 in 2013, which attempts to respond to increasing energy demand, and to decrease imported fossil fuels henceforth. The policy aims at raising the share of renewable energy sources in electricity generation by 8% in 2020 and 99% by 2030.

## Forest land use in Fiji

Fiji has been suffering from rampant diffusion of invasive trees through their lands, as the result of the three main invasive species: African tulip tree, casuarina and eucalyptus. Particularly, African tulip, which was purposely introduced to Fiji in 1930s in Fiji as a street tree and housetop shade, has been spreading rapidly on forest lands and threatening biodiversity as it takes the sunlight and moisture needed for native plants. Recognizing such adverse impacts of invasive species, the government has been implementing various control measures against these species as well as to plantations and agricultural land as early as 1999, when FAO implemented a project to identify the level of impact of invasive species in Fiji. The government has also been working with the University of the South Pacific and African tulip in coordination with FAO, through which a survey of tree distribution in Viti Levu Island was conducted.

In addition, Fiji has been experiencing decline in the country’s major industries such as sugarcane and pine industry. This has led to ongoing exit of farmers and companies from such industries, and consequently, much of the artificially ameliorated lands for traditional businesses have been left idle. Each botanic panel of the proposed Programme is aimed at a particular sector that has been abandoned or severely degraded because of 3 to 4 times pine harvesting. This will serve as a sustainable means for fuel supply of the rural population.

### D1.3 Value Added for GCF Involvement

#### Why is GCF involvement critical for the project program, in consideration of other alternatives?

The participation of GCF is critical for mobilizing and scaling up climate finance and attaining sustainability in stabilizing forestry and land use management systems and distributing clean renewable energy throughout South Pacific islands. The scale of financing required to achieve these goals is estimated at USD 104.6 million, which will be financed, given the GCF financing accounting for USD 104.6 million. The scale of financing would not have been feasible without the GCF.

**Barriers for attracting concessional financing:**

The GCF involvement will create an environment where a large pool of private investors participates in the Programme, and multiply the effect of the initial financing. In fact, South Pacific public sector institutions are already familiar with regional context-based investment hurdles such as low sovereign credit ratings, currency risks, and construction risk.

As an illustration, when the potential project sponsor had reached out to financial institutes for a pilot/Nabou biomass project, the sovereign risk of public SIDS and relatively new renewable technology of biomass in Fiji were found to be high. This was the reason why the private sector did not invest in the pilot Nabou biomass energy project. In the end, the corporate financing scheme based on sponsor credits was applied instead of project financing schemes. The same situation occurred with other projects in the region and elsewhere for similar renewable projects in the Programme. Nevertheless, Nabou project is significantly meaningful in that it showed a proven biomass technology in the Fiji context. Furthermore, this project garnered a strong support from the Fiji government and the Government recognized the role of these pilot projects in Fiji to incorporate them into their action plans to deliver its NDC.

Against this backdrop, KCGF will expand on this biomass case in Fiji and PNG, aiming to scale up the proven biomass technology in both countries. In addition to scaling up the impact of the South Pacific Programme, addressing the specific financing barrier identified in the pilot phase of the project, KCGF is structuring this program with ECA or guarantee agencies to cover the sovereign risk for private sector investors while KGB and GCF financed the uncovered portion of the project. This will be a significant step forward in terms of reducing the financial risks associated with investment in the context by having reputable financial institutions like the GCF and KCGF take a higher risk tranche in the program. Indeed, this is why KCGF is now considering their funding the Programme in case that GCF financing in this project is approved and therefore confirmed.

**GCF concessionality needed:**

The GCF participation does not necessarily lead to bringing the private sector’s attention into the Programme because private investors still have the hurdle rate of financial terms. From ECA’s perspective, the minimum level of concessionality required for a private investor to participate in the project is 12 or more of minimum simple DSCR (see B3. Financial Markets Overview). Therefore, the GCF participation should satisfy a certain level of concessionality to attract commercial lenders. Taking an example of Saba, the Programme requires that the GCF provide at least USD 50 million for the project. This is because Saba is considered as one of the most important factor for investment decision, from 1.06 (non-GCF funding assumed) to 1.2 (generally accepted for an annualized rate of return). If the GCF does not provide this level of concessionality, then the project will be received, which would result in financing failure of the Programme (see E3.5 more detail). The Programme believes that the GCF engagement here is exactly in line with the GCF’s mandate of crowding in private sector investors in climate solutions and scaling up renewable energy projects.

Besides filling in the financing gap, the GCF grant paired with loan investment adds value to the Programme. The GCF grant supports Fiji and PNG to formulate an enabling environment where the uptake of renewable energy combined with sustainable land use management can be well realized and effectively accelerated. # Enhanced Renewable Energy Access

To maintain high combustion efficiency to reduce greenhouse gas emissions from the power plant, a single 12MW biomass power plant can be seen relatively small in size equipped with the combustion system of solid fuels, and the combustion efficiency varies depending on the fuel condition. The combustion efficiency is one of the most sensitive factors for the operation of the power plant. Although the combustion efficiency of biomass from solid fuel-fired power plants, therefore, a high level of the combustion efficiency is particularly important.

To keep the combustion efficiency high, fuels should be uniform in size, moisture level and type. This project however, will use different types of biomass as feedstock for the power plant and the quality of the biomass may not be uniform due to varying conditions during gathering, delivering, and drying processes.

The moving grate system will be installed to resolve the issue of such non-identical types of fuel condition collected from various sources. The moving grate system is designed in a way that the biomass can reach down to the appropriate moisture level for combustion. This is possible because the moving grate system is usually divided into several zones, and each zone has its own temperature control system to heat up each zone separately. This mechanism allows constant efficiency to be maintained regardless of the type of fuels.

# H.2 Arrangements for Monitoring, Reporting and Evaluation

Besides the arrangements (e.g., semi-annual performance reports) laid out in A.MA, please provide project/programme level monitoring, reporting and evaluation arrangements for monitoring and reporting and evaluation. Please indicate how the interim/mid-term and final evaluations will be organized, including the timing.

Please provide methodologies for monitoring and reporting of the key outcomes of the project/programmes.


## Monitoring, Reporting and Evaluation

Monitoring and reporting at the project/programme level must comply with requirements established between the GCF and KfW in the AMA. KfW shall provide to the Fund the following reports prepared in a form and manner compliant with the provisions below:

(a) Annual performance reports on the status of each project throughout the implementation period, including a narrative report on implementation progress based on the logical framework submitted in H.1 (above) and corresponding indicators, as well as an assessment of the financial and economic performance against KfW criteria, and a report on ESS as well as gender. Upon the completion of the implementation period, KfW shall provide a performance report about the Programme meeting its mitigation impacts on a semi-annual basis.

(b) Interim and mid-term reports (if any), which will not be any more detailed than annual reports (in the case of interim reports), an assessment of the performance of this Programme against the Fund’s investment framework criteria, including financial/economic performances as part of the Fund’s Activity efficiency and effectiveness criteria, and a report on ESS as well as gender.

Also, KfW shall provide the financial information on an annual basis, which will be audited and reported on by external auditors in accordance with ISA. The financial information including but not limited to the following content will be included:

- the dates and amounts of disbursements for the period reported and cumulative amounts up to the period, and compliance with financial covenants;
- the amount of income earned as reported and cumulative amounts up to the period;
- a statement of investment income earned on GCF proceeds, as well as the amount of such investment income paid out.

In preparing the reports, KfW may hire an external expert in order to secure expertise and objective. The team which is in charge of matters regarding the GCF is a separate team from the Programme implementing team within KfW, and will review the report and evaluate its appropriateness prior to submission to the GCF.

## Calculation of CO2 Emission Reduction

Upon completion of each project, KfW shall assess the reduced amount of CO2 emission independently through an external organization specialized in the calculation, and estimate and monitor the real amount of reduction on a yearly basis. The team which is in charge of matters regarding the GCF (within KfW) will review the propriety of the overall process of assessment, estimation, and monitoring of CO2 emission.

* *KfW will conduct reporting the audited Fund-level indicator of M 4.0 (H.1) on an annual basis.*
