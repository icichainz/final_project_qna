Agriculture is one of the sectors most vulnerable to climate change and is at the same time a driver and mitigant of the underlying drivers of climate change. The project will be implemented in the LAC region, focusing on developing sustainable and low-carbon agricultural and forest landscapes. The concept and practice of Climate-Smart Agriculture offer a strategic opportunity for addressing these challenges.

According to the United Nations' Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), CSA can be defined as a practice that contributes to the achievement of sustainable development goals. It integrates the three dimensions of sustainable development: economic, social, and environmental. The project will focus on implementing Climate-Smart Agriculture in LAC countries.

The project is structured around a Public-Private Partnership Sharing Facility that will unlock innovative and scalable financial instruments to support CSA investments targeting, during an initial first phase, Mexico and Guatemala. The Facility will leverage the resources of the World Bank Group (WBG) and its International Finance Corporation (IFC), as well as those from the IGF Board, and with substantial leverage of partner funds. The Facility will provide guarantees, funds, anchor equity investments, long-term and low-cost debt funding and technical assistance grants to be channeled by the Executing Entities in LAC countries.

The Facility will target agricultural and agroforestry enterprises that demonstrate innovative and environmentally sustainable practices and support them to engage lenders for longer-term loans needed for climate smart investments. It is estimated that 10 million smallholder farmers can benefit from this facility, which aims to transform smallholder farming from an environmental threat to a conservation strategy. Sub-projects to be financed by the Facility will focus on increasing agricultural productivity and reducing greenhouse gas emissions.

By combining increased access to capital and technical assistance, they will seek to improve farmer productivity and to implement climate-smart practices in their farms. These practices include improved soil management, sustainable land for agricultural use, and adaptation activities such as drip irrigation systems, resistant seed substitution, diversification, and changes to agricultural cycles.

The causal pathways between climate change, agricultural practices and climate impacts is clear for the following reasons. First, in the target countries, poor practices in commodity crop production (e.g., cocoa, coffee, avocados) are important drivers of deforestation and forest degradation. Second, these practices also contribute to increased carbon emissions through increased forest conversion. Application of best management practices improves productivity, while certification and access to markets can increase income for farmers. Third, increased access to low-cost capital and tailored technical assistance are key enabling conditions towards such outcomes.

Second, in most communities in the target countries, both forestry and agroforestry activities are important for community livelihoods and food security. These practices also contribute to improved soil fertility and water retention, which can increase the economic logic of keeping the forest standing, while simultaneously working to reduce threats from poor commodity prices. Agroforestry systems can be more resilient than monocultures in terms of both productivity and competitiveness, and are better able to produce more benefits for a wider group of stakeholders. Finally, by working in both forestry and agroforestry landscapes, the project will achieve its goals at the landscape scale, which is especially important in areas where high forest blocks are fragmented.

The calculation of GHG emission reductions, which was carried out for each sub-project as described in the project profiles annexed to this document, shows that the implementation of Climate-Smart Agriculture practices and technologies is expected to mitigate 9.2 million tons of CO2e and implement climate-smart activities on an area of approximately 300,000 hectares during the lifetime of the Facility. The total number of sub-projects is estimated to exceed 600.

### Mexico

**Financial Sector Overview:**
In Mexico, the banking sector is smaller and deposits and credit are less compared to other countries. Financial intermediation, capital market activities, insurance, and pensions only make up 32-33% of GDP in 2015, according to the Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Geografía (the National Statistics Institute). Accessing formal financial services is difficult for small-scale farmers due to low penetration rates. Penetration of financial services is low, even by comparison with other Latin American markets. One of the main reasons explaining this situation is the lack of access to credit and savings accounts in rural areas. In 2015, only two out of every three microenterprises had access to any form of formal credit. The majority of microenterprises are informal, which hinders their access to the financial sector and consequently results in just 6.6% of them obtaining formal credit; according to data from the CNBV by contrast, 45.3% of small firms have access to some type of credit. In Mexico City, only about one-third of rural areas are devoid of the services that are available in cities; in mid-2015 it was as many as 82% of rural municipalities did not have a bank branch or any other financial institution and only two out of every three had an ATM.

Numerous rural municipalities had no existing credit contracts. Recent financial reforms, however, are helping the private sector make credit more available, which is also helping to boost microenterprises' access to formal financial services. The financial reform is supporting credit growth and increased competition in the banking sector is resulting in increased access to credit for small and medium enterprises (SMEs). In 2015, Mexico's SMEs had access to credit at a rate of 48%, up from just 36% in 2014. Nevertheless, the share of business credit that micro-enterprises and SMEs receive remains very small. Only about one fifth of microenterprises have access to commercial credit, and the majority continues to resort to their suppliers or to the informal market for their financing needs.

**Agro-Forestry Sector Overview:**
Mexico's small and medium agricultural producers face similar challenges as those in other Latin American countries. The sector is highly fragmented with a large number of smallholders concerned about commercial viability (Budar, 2013). Mexico’s ejido system also presents unique challenges for traditional land tenure systems. According to the National Institute of Forestry, Agriculture and Livestock (Instituto Nacional Forestal Agropecuario [INAFOR], 2014), Mexico's small and medium producers are also spread out across vast rural expanses, making it difficult to reach them effectively with financial services. These rural areas have been, and continue to be, underserved by the financial sector.

According to the 2014 MIF Report "Financing Agricultural Value Chains in Latin America: Barriers and Opportunities" a Mexico's smallholder farmers were particularly hard hit during the economic crisis of the mid-1990s. Medium and large agro-processors and exporters were forced to step in to finance their crops.

The Mexican market research by Mexican financial institution FincaMéxico shows that actual demand for credit from agricultural producers may be upwards of US $15 billion annually.

### Challenges and Opportunities

Despite these opportunities, access to finance is a significant constraint. According to the National Economic Development Institute (INEGI), 70% of smallholder farmers in Mexico have no access to formal financial services. The challenge lies in the majority of farms being too small for investment and working capital spread out over a very large territory.

### Financial Sector Overview

At the end of 2012, Mexico's financial sector included 289 financial institutions. According to the 2012 Agricultural Census, only 7.7% of producers said they had access to credit (INEGI, 2012). Most cooperatives are not regulated and operate on a local level with limited capital available for lending.

### Financial Institutions

Caja Popular Mexicana is one institution that offers a productive credit line for financing agriculture, livestock breeding, and aquaculture. It provides a loan-to-fit-all product with an annual interest rate of 20.73 percent and terms of 24 months (Caja Popular Mexicana, 2014).

### Background

To complete the demand analysis for products to be offered by the Facility, in addition to the market players questionnaire/telephone mentioned above, the project team relied on information from studies developed in the context of the Forest Investment Program (FIP) Investment Plans and related projects, as well as on information compiled in the context of the FIP Investment Plan Mexico.

In Mexico, consultations took place both in the context of the design of the FIP Investment Plan<sup>10</sup> and in the design of the two projects executed by IDB/MIF. During the design of the IDB/MIF project a market study<sup>11</sup> was developed that included information on demand for products, as well as on the potential to access credit.

The consultations focused on the potential demand by local smallholders, mainly organized in the form of ejidos<sup>12</sup>, to engage in sustainable agriculture practices and their "appetite" to access such credit lines, micro and technical assistance. The results indicated that there is a significant demand for credit products, particularly those with longer-term credit lines or de-risking instruments such as guarantees, insurance, working capital loans, etc., as well as on information related to the potential of ejidos<sup>12</sup> to access credit.

More recently, additional consultations have been finalized for the design of the Mexican National Strategy on REDD+ (ENAREGID)<sup>13</sup>, where specific types of interventions have been identified<sup>14</sup> to address the need of strengthening agroforestry systems and increasing their resilience. The results indicate that there is a significant demand for credit, technical assistance, and insurance products.

In addition, national strategy specific questions have been addressed by stakeholders confirming that the situation has not changed since the design of the FIP Investment Plan Mexico<sup>15</sup>.

Finally, A market study<sup>16</sup> is about to be finalized for the State of Quintana Roo, also in the context of a potential FIP project, where MSMEs and local financial institutions have been interviewed on current bottlenecks for strengthening their capacity to access credit.

The feedback received highlighted the following four needs:

- Access to credit that reflects the agriculture project cycle (longer and adequate grace periods)
- Access to financial instruments to mitigate residual production risks, such as an insurance that would cover extreme and catastrophic climate events

# Guatemala

Guatemala is a lower middle-income country with a GDP per capita of USD 3,351 in year 2012. ### Guatemala

Guatemala began to regulate the use of land, forest resources management and protection of biodiversity in 1989 when the Protected Areas System was established through the Protected Areas Law. In 1996 the Forestry Law was enacted with the objective to reduce deforestation, promote reforestation protect ecosystems and increase the productivity of forests. The law sets a target for estimated forest cover of 50% by 2030. This is being achieved through the implementation of a period of twenty years. Accordingly, the Forestry Incentive Program (PINFOR) for landowners and the Incentive Program for Forests (PIF) for communities were established in 1997 to provide incentives for reforestation and afforestation. These Programs have allowed reforestation and agroforestry activities in 430,000 ha and have benefited circa 900,000 people. In 2008, the National Forest Policy was approved.

The 2011-2015 Agriculture Policy identifies the development of adaptive technologies to climate change as a strategic action. This policy is in accordance with the Framework Law for Vulnerability Reduction and Mandatory Adaptation to Climate Change (Ley Marco de Reducción de Vulnerabilidad y Adopción Obligatoria al Cambio Climático) which provides regulatory framework for preventing and planning actions for the impacts of climate change. In addition, Guatemala is currently in the process of developing a National Strategy on Forests and Land Use (SAGFOR), which will be aligned with the SDGs. The Strategy addresses land tenure issues, forest governance, safeguards and gender considerations. Other national policies and laws relevant for forest management and CSA include: the Forestry Policy, the National Forestry Agenda, the Policy on Sustainable Management of Forest Resources, the National Action Plan against Deforestation and Illegal Logging, the National Strategy for Sustainable use of Wood, the National Strategy for Landscapes Restoration, among other.

### RATIONALE FOR GCF INVOLVEMENT

## D.1 Value Added for GCF Involvement

Please specify why the GCF involvement is critical for the project/programme, in consideration of other alternatives.

As described in the market analysis section, there is significant private capital that can be deployed into climate-smart agriculture projects. However, the perceived risk of these investments requires public sector long-term resources and guarantees to make them attractive. The GCF has a unique ability to provide such guarantees and ensure that funds are channeled to agriculture mitigation and adaptation activities. The importance of these types of risk sharing arrangements is highlighted in the World Bank’s 2015 report on climate-smart agriculture, which states that “the IFC is developing a sector-wide approach for climate-smart agriculture (CSA) projects, with the objective of increasing private sector confidence in structuring and deploying ad hoc financial instruments for this market segment.”

By sharing the risk of piloting early-stage, innovative financial mechanisms in the CSA space, GCF funding will allow for support to be provided to a wider range of projects. This will help to build up the capacity of the private sector, increasing private sector confidence in structuring and deploying ad hoc financial instruments for this market segment.

In addition, sub-projects entering the portfolio should demonstrate to the clients of the Facility that these investments are viable and profitable. The GCF’s experience with the LAC region shows that investors expect projects such as those to be funded under the Facility are good bets, thus attracting further private capital.

Finally, GCF funding will have a catalytic effect beyond the LAC region in making other MDBs and IFIs increasingly confident that CSA projects are viable and profitable.


## D.2 Exit Strategy

Please explain how the project/programme sustainability will be ensured in the long run, after the project/programme is implemented with support from the GCF and other sources, taking into consideration the long-term financial viability demonstrated by the LAC region.

Sustainability is to be viewed at both the sub-project and Facility scale. At the sub-project scale, sustainability is achieved through a) the design of CSA projects that are financially viable in their own right; b) the provision of equity return and sustainable cash flows to pay any debt service; c) a technical design package to end clients to ensure appropriate project implementation; d) the provision of long-term credit to producers; e) the provision of technical assistance to the Executing Entity to ensure their ability to appropriately analyze and manage the risks of long-term credit to producers, as well as provide ongoing financial management support packages to producers; f) the provision of a strong monitoring system to ensure the technical and financial success of the sub-project over its investment horizon; but the intent at all times is to establish technical, financial, and environmental sustainability of the project/programme over one long-term cycle, i.e. the cycle after even the last loan has been repaid.

At the Facility level, the goal of the initiative will be to attract private sector investments into climate-smart agriculture activities. By reducing the risk of the projects implemented in the Facility it is expected that private sector players will be more willing to invest in CSA projects. The Facility will provide a range of services including: a) knowledgeable of the structure, benefits, and terms of CSA projects and understand the actual risks of these projects instead of relying on perceived risks; b) in many some cases the repayment performance of first-time transactions will greatly improve as a result of the training provided by the Facility; c) the Facility will provide ongoing technical assistance should become comfortable with the longer tenors and terms of these types of investments and make further investments, which will be more attractive to private sector investors. The combination of environmental and financial will be disseminated widely to increase the impact of this demonstration effect.

Exit strategy: divestment from the GCF’s financial products

According to the IPCC, "Mexican agriculture appears to be particularly vulnerable to climate-induced changes in precipitation because most crops are rainfed and irrigation is not widely used. ## Climate-Smart Agriculture (CSA)

Climate-Smart Agriculture (CSA) is an integrated approach to mitigate climate change and build resilience to adverse climate impacts while at the same time improving agricultural productivity.

According to the United Nations' Food and Agriculture Organization, CSA aims to achieve three objectives: (i) increasing agricultural productivity and incomes; (ii) adapting and building resilience to climate change; and (iii) reducing and/or removing greenhouse gas emissions from agriculture.

The application of CSA technologies and practices will differ from project to project in the two focus countries, depending on the specific challenges and opportunities as well as climatic conditions and other factors. CSA can range from agroforestry systems, which are based on the sustainable management of ecosystem services including soil and water; both Micro (drip) irrigation systems, which have been proven successful in the past to help agricultural producers with a higher degree of control over their water use and thus reduce water scarcity as a consequence of climate change. Technical assistance within the sub-projects could look at cost of benefit analysis for micro irrigation systems and the optimal setup depending on factors such as soil, irrigation water, location, type of crop etc.

Agroforestry systems: A combination of different land-use systems can provide a solution to increasing processing and sustainable management of climate change. Agroforestry systems also provide an alternative to traditional slash-and-burn agriculture. Depending on the type of agroforestry system, it is possible that trees (silvopastoral systems) or animals, trees are combined together Possible applications of agroforestry systems include: soil and water conservation; improved nutrient cycling; increased biodiversity; reduced erosion; and improved carbon sequestration.

Sustainable soil management. Climate change effects soil health through erratic rainfall, drought, storms and increased temperatures. These lower the water and nutrient capture in soil, lead to erosion and higher mineralization. There are sustainable soil management practices that can help mitigate these effects such as crop rotation, residue retention, water use efficiency and irrigation, soil restoration to improve carbon sequestration and conservation (e.g. vegetation cover).

Monitoring, reporting and evaluation arrangements will comply with requirements established between GCF and IDB in the GCF-IBD MoU. Every sub-project supported by the Facility will undergo the full IDB Group project cycle, including the development of a project document, preparation for implementation, execution, monitoring, disbursement, supervision, project closing (i.e., once debt has been fully repaid) and evaluation.

As part of the approval process, every investment under the Facility will be subject to an independent assessment by the IDB Group. This assessment will evaluate development impact and the additionality of IDBs and the GCF’s support. Such assessment will normally include an economic analysis and a social impact assessment.

Monitoring and evaluation of the proposed facility will be managed according to the applicable IDB procedures. As such, Executing Entities will be responsible for the monitoring and reporting of sub-project specific indicators, with the financial counterpart being responsible for the overall project performance. This will be done through the existing data collection and monitoring systems or establishing new data collection and monitoring systems at sub-project level as required by the project document. The facility will also use IDB/IMF reporting system to feed data from sub-projects onto the Facility monitoring system.

The mid-term review of the execution of the Facility will be managed by MIF’s Climate-Smart Agriculture Unit (CSA) in collaboration with the selected FIs. The MIFs Corporate Results Framework (CRF) will provide each investment with a results framework for tracking development impact. The MIF CSA Unit will be responsible for tracking the impact of investments under the Facility and the CRF will be updated as necessary to reflect any changes in the project, based on data collected from sub-projects financed under the facility.

The mid-term review will be conducted using the MIFs Project Status Report (PSR), a web-based tool which allows its clients and partners to actively participate in the monitoring of progress and completion of their projects, together with MIF’s specialists in charge of supervision. A mid-term review at year 7 will also use all parameters recommended by the GCF, and will verify information gathered through the PSR tools, as relevant.

The mid-term review will:

a. review effectiveness, efficiency and timeliness of Facility implementation;

b. analyze effectiveness of implementation and Executing Entities arrangements;

c. identify issues requiring decisions and remedial actions;

d. identify lessons learned in project preparation, implementation and management;

e. highlight technical achievements and lessons learned;

f. propose any mid-course corrections and/or adjustments to the implementation strategy as necessary; and

g. verify actual direct and indirect leverage ratios. Direct relates to the Facility level, whereas indirect to the Executing Entities.

Similarly, a final evaluation will take place at the end of the Facility implementation. The final evaluation will review Facility impact, analyze sustainability of results and discuss whether the Facility has achieved its ultimate objectives. The purpose of the evaluation is to provide an independent assessment of the project’s performance in terms of development impact dimensions, based on the information collected from all sub-projects financed under the facility. The final evaluation of the Facility will be conducted by a third-party evaluator appointed by the GCF and approved by the Executive Council (ECG) which provides an expanded supervision report (XSR) of the transaction once it reaches early operating status, and a final evaluation report at project completion date of the investment. As far as the programme outcomes, an assessment of the programme contribution to business performance, economic development and environmental & social aspects will be performed. In particular, and to the extent possible, for each sub-project the calculation of the sub-project's Financial and Economic Rates of Return (FRR and ERR) will be completed in order to assess its financial viability. The FRR and ERR calculations will be based on the assumptions used when performing these calculations; client provided information will be used, such as updated financial model and statements. All ratings will be based on the latest available data.

Finally, IDB/MIF development effectiveness specialists will also explore the feasibility of carrying out an experimental impact evaluation to gather actionable business intelligence by robustly parametrizing the risk of the proposed financial instruments. The objective would be to identify a control group (counterfactual) that could be used as a benchmark against which different types of CSA MISMIS in the intervention area. For example, if excess demand for funding from the Facility is observed and there are no other sources of funding available within the country, it may be possible to conduct an impact evaluation in this setting; the IDB Group and its partners would use objective criteria (e.g., a tailored scoring model) to classify applicants for funding in three categories:

1. The first category would be composed by those applicants for funding deemed to be eligible for funding, according to the established criteria. All applicants classified in this category would receive funding.
2. The second category would be composed by those applicants for funding deemed to be ineligible for funding, according to the established criteria. No further action will be taken with respect to these applicants.
3. The third category would be composed by those applicants for funding for which the established criteria do not yield a conclusive decision regarding their eligibility. A lottery would be conducted to determine final eligibility for funding in this category.

By creating and tracking a comparable control group (counterfactual), this type of experimental impact evaluation would allow to robustly identify the changes in indicators at all levels which is solely attributable to the Facility, in isolation from potential other confounding factors (such as, for example, the evolution of economic indicators during the intervention period).
