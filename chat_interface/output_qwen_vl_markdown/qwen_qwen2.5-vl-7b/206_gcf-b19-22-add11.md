# A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

1. Climate change is causing a rapid increase in sea surface temperatures and sea-level rise (SLR), accelerating saltwater intrusion into the fresh water resources of the coastal belt of Bangladesh. Climate change-induced soil and water salinity, coupled with increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events, are reducing crop productivity or livelihoods as well as the availability and quality of drinking water in the vulnerable coastal communities. Furthermore, given the crucial role that women play in water security and household-level resilience, and their disproportionate vulnerability to climate change impacts, any intervention that improves access to and enhances water security of the affected coastal communities disproportionately affects women and girls.

2. Therefore, the key objective of the proposed project is to support the Government of Bangladesh (GoB) in strengthening its capacity for adaptation to climate change by implementing interventions with an impact on climate-change-induced salinity on their livelihoods and water security. GCF resources will be combined with GoB’s own resources to implement a multi-faceted approach that includes: i) building climate-resilient infrastructure; ii) conserving resilient livelihoods and drinking water solutions for the vulnerable communities in the Southwestern coastal districts of Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh. An estimated 719,229 people (about 24,516 direct and 47,713 indirect) will benefit from the proposed project interventions.

3. The proposed project will empower target communities, especially women, as “change-agents” to plan, implement, and manage resilient livelihoods and drinking water solutions. The project will enable those communities to address climate change-induced salinity in their livelihoods and drinking water security by strengthening the adaptive capacities of these communities in the face of worsening impacts of climate change induced salinity on their respective livelihoods and drinking water supplies. In addition, the project will provide technical assistance that can be invested in promoting a diversification from currently non-adaptive, freshwater-reliant livelihoods of small-scale farmers, fishers, and agro-labourers towards climate-resilient agricultural livelihoods. GoB co-financing is leveraged to support the implementation of the project interventions through the existing institutional arrangements, value chains and market linkages for their long-term viability in the face of increasing salinity and extreme weather.

The project will be implemented by a consortium led by the Bangladesh Water Development Board (BWDB) with other partners including the Bangladesh Climate Change Trust (BCCT), the International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development (ICIMOD), and the University of Dhaka. The project will provide climate-resilient drinking water solutions to secure year-round, safe drinking water supplies for the targeted communities. Access to reliable and safe drinking water is a key component in ensuring food security and nutrition as well as improving the resulting life and cost savings and health co-benefits in enhanced livelihoods and income generating and/or educational opportunities for women and girls. The project will also provide climate-resilient infrastructure, including investments in the drinking water supply solutions in the long-term. Finally, through investments in institutional capacities, knowledge dissemination and evidence-based learning, the project will enable pathways for replication and scaling-up of the interventions to other vulnerable coastal communities across the Southwestern coast of Bangladesh. The project yields significant environmental, social (including gender), and economic co-benefits including: i) improved access to safe drinking water; ii) increased food security and nutrition; iii) enhanced empowerment; and increased income and health benefits, estimated at US$15 million and US$42 million respectively over the project lifetime.

The project contributes towards GoB’s achievement of priorities outlined in the Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs), including climate change strategies. The project objective speaks to the top five key near-term areas of intervention identified by the INDC to address adverse impacts of climate change1: i) Food security, livelihoods and income generation; ii) Water resources management; iii) Coastal zone management; iv) Soil and Waste Management, including Salinity Intrusion control; v) Flood Control and Erosion protection. 2 Building Climate Resistant Infrastructure. # C1. Strategic Context

## Country Background:

### Geographical content and climate change related vulnerabilities

The hydro-geophysical nature of Bangladesh significantly contributes to its high vulnerability to climate change-induced extreme weather events. The country is located in the Ganges-Brahmaputra-Meghna river basin system, which this region must drain 65 per cent of annual runoff generated in the Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau. This area has a predominantly flat and low-lying terrain with hills from the northeast to southeast. It is mostly less than 10 m above sea level, with only 2% of its land area exceeding 30m. Bangladesh is highly vulnerable to cyclonic disasters*, with coastal areas frequently hit by cyclones, storm surges, and droughts. It has experienced more than 50 cyclones each year and a severe flood occurs every 4-5 years, sometimes inundating up to 60 per cent of the country.

### The coastal zone

The coastal zone is especially vulnerable to climate change, extreme events and their impacts on the salinity of soils and water. It has an estimated population of about 38 million people. The coastal zone ranges between 1-2 m ASL and 4-5 m ASL in the southeast and this low-lying area is highly vulnerable to sea-level rise, storm surges, cyclones, and droughts. Climate change will lead to further salinization of soils as well as increasing seawater intrusion into groundwater resources. Climate change will also lead to further reduction in rainfall, increased evaporation/drying, particularly towards the end of the dry season. It is less clear how rainfall changes will affect surface water availability. The coastal zone has a high population density and is highly dependent on freshwaters, whereas river flows are largely dependent on rainfall in the Himalayas/India and Nepal, with releases by upstream countries being critical for Bangladesh’s food security, energy production, and access to water for agriculture, and experience significant changes in coastal aquatic ecosystems. A range of livelihoods depend directly or indirectly on these resources, including fishing communities that are highly vulnerable to cyclonic events. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) reports that by the year 2050 approximately of 17 million people will be displaced from their homes due to sea-level rise and coastal flooding. Assuming SLR of 6-8 cm, up to 17 million, 12 million and 4 million people are expected to be at low, medium and high risk respectively. The coastal zone is also highly vulnerable to cyclones, storm surges, droughts, and extreme poverty compared to the rest of the country; but those communities are also most likely to suffer a wider range of impacts from climate change, including increased exposure to flooding, more intense tropical cyclones and tropical storms, changes in rainfall and temperature, which will further exacerbate the risks they face.

### Climate change impacts

Climate change impacts are not gender-neutral and many of the consequences of climate-induced impacts are more severe for women and adolescent girls, and other socio-economically marginalized groups, given their specific livelihoods and vulnerabilities. Women are also disproportionately affected by information asymmetry and decision in making processes*. Women and girls importantly lack access to productive resources, as well as decision-making power, and that has impacts on their health, food security, and poverty reduction.

#### Climate vulnerability baseline

Given its location, dependence on monsoon systems, transboundary rivers, and the low coping capacity of its population, Bangladesh is vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. In particular, an increase in the intensity of cyclones is expected to cause significant damage to coastal communities and ecosystems. 15. In Bangladesh, cyclones are a major hazard. Between 2008 and 2013, a total of 61 cyclones hit Bangladesh, with the south-west zone affected by 28 per cent of these cyclones (v). #### Key Impacts of CC induced salinity – disruption to agricultural livelihoods and drinking water security.

**Salinity impacts on agricultural livelihoods:**  
Local communities are already experiencing direct damages to crops, decreasing fresh water fish stocks, and income from fishing due to increased salinity. Over 1 million hectares of cultivable land is already affected by saline intrusion caused by slow- and rapid-onset events (e.g., the net cultivated area in Sylhet district has lost 7 per cent since 2005). The total loss of cultivable land in Bangladesh due to saltwater intrusion is estimated at over 200,000 hectares18. With salinity (+4 ppt) of irrigation water expected to further reduce farm productivity by up to 50 per cent19.

**Storm surges and sea-level rise:**  
The projected storm surge will inundate the coastal areas of Bangladesh with an estimated volume of 23,760 million cubic meters of seawater. The total volume of saltwater intrusion is expected to be 48,000 million cubic meters. This will result in a loss of 156,928 tons of Aus rice and 116,060 tons of Boro rice, which will have profound impacts on both the country’s economy and the food security of its citizens20.

**Salinity intrusion and tidal flooding:**  
Salinity intrusion and tidal flooding have already prevented the expansion of agriculture in the coastal region, with land use patterns changing significantly between 1980 and 2010. The Livelihoods Assessment Report (Annex Bb) presents GIS maps on land-use patterns in the targeted project Unionos for the years 1995, 2005, and 2013 (see Section 4.2). It is evident that the coastal areas of Bangladesh have experienced a shift from agricultural land to aqua-cultural land (mainly shrimp farming) due to salinity intrusion. The area under shrimp farming in Cox’s Bazar district has increased by 65 per cent since 2009. Shrimp farming, widely adopted as a way to deal with the increasing salinity of soil and water, has had significant impacts on livelihoods. It has led to the migration of many poor households from their traditional households. Furthermore, weak governance and land-tenure arrangements, as well as value chain structures, have created opportunities for large-scale enterprises at the expense of small farmers and small-scale enterprises, affecting the labour and income opportunities of the poor, especially women.

### Gaps and Key Barriers

#### Lack of awareness and access to tools for climate-resilient agriculture-based livelihoods

A lack of poor and marginalized women's awareness about climate change and how it affects their economic activities and livelihoods, particularly through increasing salinization of soil and freshwater bodies entering farming and fishing productivity. However, while many are aware of the industry-induced discussion on climate change, few understand the science behind it or the evolving evidence base linked to climate change and how to respond to these risks to safeguard their livelihoods.

Traditional farming practices will only provide a reliable source of income for a limited time, depending on shifting climate conditions. Climate-resilient agricultural practices are more complex in terms of technology and management options (innovations). Progressive farming techniques involve using short cycle crops and shifting away from water-dependent agriculture. These practices have higher yields but require more inputs such as fertilizers and have a higher market price. However, there is a limited knowledge-of, and access to these crop varieties, particularly among women in coastal communities.

Reliable information on climate-resilient practices is generally not available, preventing farmers from switching to resilient agricultural practices. This is further compounded by the lack of extension services for new crop varieties or early maturing varieties) or resilient livelihoods away from fresh-water dependent agriculture, given the limited knowledge and access to these options. Women are also less likely than men to have access to such shifting to shrimp farming (which lead to further salinization) or cultivating high freshwater-dependent watermelons, horticulture crops, etc., which are more climate-resilient.

#### Gender-specific barriers

The lack of agricultural extension support services is particularly acute among women, with many extension services and training being geared towards men. This is compounded by the fact that women's participation in activities such as trainings can be greatly limited, both due to norms and attitudes within their communities and because of time constraints. Women are often responsible for household chores and childcare, which limits their availability and time. Training and extension services are often not designed in a manner that will allow women full participation (e.g., training sessions held during the day when women have other responsibilities). In addition, women in coastal districts of south-western Bangladesh have limited literacy. When labour opportunities are created, women are often paid less than men for similar work, and they face discrimination in accessing credit facilities. Their bargaining power is limited in regards to negotiating fair working conditions.

#### Limited technical and financial capacities

Limited technical and financial capacities to adapt climate-resilient livelihoods. Closely linked to the lack of awareness and access to tools for climate-resilient agriculture-based livelihoods, this limits women's options and adopt climate-resilient agricultural practices (e.g., adding saline tolerant crops or intercropping with salt-tolerant species such as crab farming) requires specific knowledge, skills, and technology transfer that is limited, as communities lack the technical assistance and support owing to limited capacities among peers and local extension staff. Extreme poor households are particularly vulnerable because they have limited access to credit facilities, which can cause new shocks and stresses and have limited space and ability to think about and act on choices that will make a positive difference in their livelihoods. The lack of financial resources also limits women's capacity to adapt to climate constraints the capacity of many agricultural-based households to access finance and invest in the necessary technologies and practices for climate-resilient agriculture. This is further compounded by repeated salinity intrusions and cyclone impacts, which lead to significant crop and asset losses.

# F1 Economic and Financial Analysis

## Approach and Methodology

The economic efficiency of the investment was determined by computing the economic net-present value (NPV) with a discount rate of 10%, discount rate, and the economic internal rate of return (EIRR). Economic benefits (benefits and costs) are measured in real 2017 US dollars. Economic costs of the project include taxes, duties, and price subsidies. The cost of labor is based on the average wage for unskilled and semi-skilled labor in Bangladesh. Given that the economic cost of labor in Bangladesh is likely lower than the market wage rate, this may overestimate the true cost of the project, underestimating the true net economic value of the project.

As is common when undertaking the economic analysis of investment projects, numerous assumptions were used to delineate the "with project scenario" from the "without project scenario". These assumptions are presented in Table 1.2 and include assumptions about the future price of goods and services, as well as the net economic value of the proposed investment project. The analysis period is 25 years, including implementation (though Output 2 is limited to run for 10 years post-implementation).

The three main outputs of the project centred on: (for full analysis including references for the assumptions, refer to Economic Analysis)

* **Output 1 - climate resilient livelihoods for women:** Women will be provided support for climate resilient aquaculture and agricultural livelihoods options through a range of training, institutional and material support. 25,000 households are expected to benefit from this output. The economic benefits stream is an increase in household income from the climate resilient livelihoods adopted by women, which is sustained for the life of the project (25 years). This output also incorporates an early warning system which will protect the livelihoods investments made (the potential benefit of delay and/or reduction in risk).

* **Output 2 - saline resistant water supply:** To reduce the reliance on saline water available to coastal communities, the project will invest in rainwater harvesting infrastructure at the household and community level. 136,100 people will be reached by this output. The economic benefits stream is a reduction of the number of people affected by water scarcity, which has a range of benefits including (but not limited) to health benefits and reduced burden for acquiring and processing water for consumption. The benefit stream is measured in terms of deaths-avoided from diarrhoea and other waterborne diseases.

* **Output 3 and project management costs are not amenable to economic analysis on their own. However, the total cost of these two project expenditures cannot be ignored. For purpose of the economic analysis, it is assumed that this cost is distributed across Output 1 and Output 2 proportional to their respective annual direct costs.

The economic analysis included sensitivity analysis, subjecting the project to conservative assumptions to determine if the net present value and economic internal rate of return remained acceptable.
