The ASEAN Green Public Finance Facility: Green Recovery Program (AGCF GRP) is an urgently needed and pioneering initiative which aims to embed three integrated objectives within the countries' economic recovery planning post the COVID-19 pandemic: a) "green infrastructure development"; b) carbon reduction; c) climate resilience. The AGCF GRP will be implemented through a multi-year, multi-sector program that will leverage public and private sector resources in an integrated approach to address the region's pressing needs for economic recovery while also addressing pressure from increased government budgets towards relief measures to counteract the COVID-19 pandemic. While aimed at ASEAN member states, the AGCF GRP is "deep" yet in essence across most of Asia, the program also has immense replication potential in the region.

The AGCF GRP proposes to meet the above three objectives through a district program, associated with the ADF, which will be implemented by an ADB-led team. The proposed AGCF GRP will be structured as follows: a) as part of the ASEAN Infrastructure Fund, is owned by ASEAN member states along with ADB and is managed by a skilled ADB team – one of the few such regionally-owned vehicles in the world.

The proposed AGCF GRP will integrate a) the application of innovative leveraged/blendable finance models for public infrastructure projects; b) the use of blended finance to leverage private sector investment from other sources into projects; b) adherence to global best practices in selecting and setting targets for "Green and Poverty Reduction" (GPR) projects, including the World Bank’s Green Bond Framework (2019), the ADB Green Bond Principles (2018), the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank’s Green Finance Principles (2017) jointly with European and Asian partners); and c) expansion of capital market instruments for greater access to concessional finance. The AGCF GRP will be implemented in a phased manner, starting with pilot projects in 2023/24, followed by the full-scale implementation phase from 2025 onwards.

The AGCF GRP is designed therefore as a highly additional first regional "Green Recovery" program for Asia without which catalytic low-emission projects would not be prioritized within development programs in the region. The AGCF GRP will be implemented over a period of five years (2023-2027) with a total duration of 15 years of COVID recovery (starting in 2021). It will capitalize on a very limited time window of opportunity to influence the development of new infrastructure projects, which can have long-term impacts on environmental degradation, and stimulate growth by creating "green" jobs. With a strong signaling effect to promote green infrastructure investments, the AGCF GRP will also contribute to Asia’s efforts in meeting international standards for climate impact, thereby paving the way for the development of longer-term low-carbon project pipelines and creating grounds for broad paradigm shift in Southeast Asia.

Developing countries in Southeast Asia have historically seen their economic growth paired with increased carbon emissions. The region has experienced a significant increase in energy demand over recent years. Electricity demand in the region has grown at 6% year (one of the fastest in the world), largely supported by a doubling in total fuel use. Carbon intensity growth in Philippines, Indonesia and Malaysia is driven by the rapid expansion of coal-fired power generation capacity. The region’s transport sector accounts for about 20% of energy consumption and 35% of carbon emissions. The region has also seen significant growth in passenger and freight transport. Deforestation and forest degradation continues to destroy critical carbon sinks across Southeast Asia, with Indonesia alone losing an estimated 14 million hectares of forest cover annually because of global warming, including increasing average temperatures estimated at between 0.4°C-0.20°C per decade.

While progress has been made in the last years to scale up green infrastructure, the global COVID-19 crisis has severely affected the momentum on climate action that was gathering pace in 2018-19. ADB estimates that the region’s public and private sector investments in energy and transport projects will fall by about 35% as a result of the emergency response; government funds for NDC implementation have been slashed, and across the region investment in renewable energy has fallen by over 40%. ASEAN countries have committed an estimated USD 2.7 trillion to green infrastructure development between now and 2030. However, many of these projects are in fact, some may even support a rebound in emissions. Across the region, subsidies have been provided to fossil-fuel based power generation and transport, and some countries have witnessed a roll-back in environmental regulations, threatening increased deforestation.

There is a narrow window of opportunity to influence COVID recovery starting in 2021, when multi-year stimulus packages will start being rolled out by governments. Rapid action is needed to signal the importance and benefits of green recovery for both economic and environmental reasons. This is especially important as many jobs and democrats see how a green recovery can be pursued in the next five to ten years. Should ASEAN governments fail to take advantage of this window, they will face significant challenges starting in 2021, risk locking-in emissions-intensive project pipelines for the next two to three decades.

The ACGF Green Recovery Program (ACGF GP) proposes a comprehensive, country-owned, multi-stakeholder approach to support countries pursue a low-emission COVID-19 recovery path. The program will be implemented through four main components:

* **Replicating and scaling up successful green recovery models:** This component will start investments through combined support for innovative project model development and provision of de-risking mechanisms. It will also provide technical assistance to help countries access concessional financial, technical assistance and loans, to leverage a further USD 3.3 billion of co-financing from ADB and other partners.
* **Mobilizing substantial global green capital flows:** both through projects as well as through further replication of these across the region on scale, by catalyzing a further USD 4 in additional private / commercial sector investment, including through green bonds, hybrid public-private partnerships (PPPs) and other innovative financing models.
* **Enabling investments in high climate potential infrastructure projects:** expected to directly reduce 119 million tonnes of CO2 emissions per year. This will be achieved by supporting the development and implementation of a green recovery (with co-benefits in terms of gender and social inclusion), thereby supporting implementation of the ASEAN Green Recovery Framework.
* **Supporting governments to prioritize low-emission projects within infrastructure pipelines:** especially in areas such as transport, energy, water, waste management, shipping and banking and other enabling environment factors that will facilitate further green investments.
* **Transforming ADB’s pipeline for the region (around USD 5 to $10 billion committed per year), building on ADB’s existing climate finance commitments.** This will be achieved by focusing on high-performing, low-emission projects while tackling the financing challenges emerging from the COVID-19 crisis.

The ACGF GP is a ring-fenced program managed by ADB and associated with ACFP, a vehicle under the ASEAN Infrastructure Fund which is owned by ASEAN governments and ADB. Through clear limits on GCF funds used to support the ACGF GP, the program will ensure that at least 90% of all project costs are covered through the revolving use of funds; the program will ensure the creation of at least $4 billion worth of projects over 30 years. So far, the ACGF GP has received USD 15 million in funding from ADB and USD 27 million in co-financing. Of this sub-project financing needs are met by ADB, either through its own resources (at a minimum) or from other partner finance providers.

Through its association with ACFP, the ACGF GP is strongly backed by ministries of finance, who will act as focal points for the program in their national contexts. The program will place a special emphasis on working with National Development Banks and other financial institutions to ensure that green recovery investments are made at scale.


Designated to target the major sources of emissions in the region, the ACGF GP will support multiple sectors targeting: i) investments that reduce CO2 emissions from fossil fuels (i.e. renewable energy, energy efficiency in industry and buildings, electrification of transport); ii) reducing deforestation and land-use change (e.g., CO2 emissions from land-use change (reforestation and sustainable forest management, low-carbon agriculture and agroforestry), and iii) investments that reduce non-CO2 emissions (e.g. methane reduction in the livestock sector, the installation of at least 800 MW of renewable energy capacity; supporting modal shift to railways and public transport models, and supporting the management / reforestation of at least 80,000 hectares of forest land.

Together, ASEAN countries account for 8.5% of global population (a combined population of 649 million people), and 3.7% of global GDP (US$1 trillion). ### A. BASELINE: CARBON-INTENSIVE ECONOMIC GROWTH WORSENED BY COVID-19

#### Increasing energy-related CO2 emissions
Due to increasing dependence on coal and gas-fired power growth of 5% per year from 2000 to 2018, compared with a global average of 3.7%, relative to other regions*. Energy-related CO2 emissions are driven by rising incomes, coupled with urbanization and the expansion of energy-intensive industries. In 2019, ASEAN countries accounted for around 50% of the increase in final energy consumption across region since 2000. Emissions declined across ASEAN countries by about 8% in 2019, among the fastest in the world, largely supported by a doubling in fossil fuel use, and especially coal.

#### Persisting deforestation and loss of carbon sinks
Land-use change and agriculture together contributed over half the region's GHG emissions in 2016 (see annex 28). The region has 15% of the world's tropical forests, and is home to 34% of the world’s tropical forest area. According to a recent study by the World Resources Institute, analysis* estimates that Southeast Asia lost 90 million hectares of forest between 2005 and 2015, with most deforestation occurring in Indonesia (67%), Malaysia (8%) and Thailand (4%). The countries of Myanmar, Laos and Lao PDR, which have relatively low energy-related carbon footprints, land-use change related emissions are the largest contributor to their total GHG emissions. In 2019, forest fires in Indonesia accounted for 53% of all CO2 emissions still from land use change, largely driven by deforestation of tropical forests.

#### Insufficient improvements in immediate pre-COVID period
Recent years have seen an improvement in the carbon intensity of energy supply and demand. However, this has not been sufficient to achieve decarbonization of the economy as required by the temperature goals of the Paris Agreement. Modest progress on improving energy efficiency and reducing emissions from transport and industry has not addressed the steady growth in emissions in most ASEAN countries over the past 50 years*. Key issues include:

- Future emissions gap expected despite NDCs: Even if all targets in the NDCs were met*, emissions in the region are projected to increase by around 1.3% per year from 2020 to 2030, which would not meet the goals of the Paris Agreement, implying that a further 11% reduction in emissions would be needed.

## Lock-in towards intensive investment for the next two decades. Lack of investment in a program such as ACGF GRIP which supports governments to prioritize low-carbon alternatives would result in a quick rebound of coal-fired power generation, lock-in towards carbon-intensive energy generation, lack of attention towards nature-based solutions, or investment in transport systems that encourage private vehicle use and urban sprawl. This would lead to an increase in the global average temperature and the region's energy intensity may increase or at least decrease more slowly than in other non-OECD countries.

### Continuation of carbon-intensive growth trends. The following trends are likely to continue in the face of the COVID-19 crisis, without concerted action through programs like the ACGF GRIP:

* Continued dependence on coal and gas for electricity, and oil and stranded assets.
* Many ASEAN countries are planning or building new coal-fired power plants. According to an analysis of "current and planned fossil fuel generation plants in ASEAN countries shows that over 83% would be built by 2040." In addition, the region is expected to see a significant increase in the number of coal-fired power plants as built are planned.
* Southeast Asia is also expected to see an uptake on about 70,000 emissions-related deaths by 2050.

### Scale up of renewable generation. Non-hydro renewable resources for electricity generation have increased in past years (by around 30% between 2014 and 2018). However, the region has a major potential for solar PV and wind is only starting to be realized, despite falling technology costs.
* Across all countries in the region the structure of electricity markets, financial mechanisms, state-of-the-art technologies, and policy frameworks are still not fully developed. In many cases, such as in Lao PDR, these factors mean that the risks associated with projects are often too high for investors to engage.

### Mobility and transport. The share of private vehicles in urban areas is expected to increase even on demand by oil or more than 50% since 2000.
* Vehicle ownership reflects a pattern of mobility increasingly focused on individual car use, rather than public transit systems.
* Lack of comprehensive public transport systems. Other than capital cities like Bangkok, Jakarta or Manila, most secondary cities are yet to develop comprehensive public transport systems and are heavily reliant on taxi systems, motorcycles, and inefficient vehicles such as jeepsneys (in Philippines) or three-wheelers (across many countries).

B3. Project programme description (max. 2000 words, approximately 4 pages)

OVERVIEW

The ASEAN Catalytic Green Finance Facility Green Recovery Program (ACGF GRPP) is designed as a comprehensive and integrated approach to support ASEAN countries in mainstreaming low-emission actions into COVID-19 recovery pathways as a result of a stronger enabling framework, innovative technologies and financing models, and the creation of public, private and commercial sources, creating significant leverage, scalability, and replicability.

Design principles - programmatic, catalytic, maximizing climate impact

The ACGF GRPP will be managed by ADB, associated with ACF, as a facility of ADB's Green Infrastructure Fund (GIF) and the ASEAN Climate Change Fund (ACC), with ACF as the lead implementing agency. Through GIF, the only existing regional infrastructure financing initiative of its kind, it demonstrates its backing by finance ministers in the region.

Programmatic response, which when compared to project-by-project financing is critical to rapidly finance post-COVID climate projects at the scale needed and capitalize on the window of opportunity created by the pandemic, will be achieved through a programmatic approach that identifies and structures sub-projects with common criteria and themes, as well as efficiency in financing administration, while maintaining flexibility.

Clear focus on infrastructural, high-potential climate and green sub-projects by integrating portfolio climate performance targets, promoting sub-projects that meet global best practice performance thresholds, and ensuring a clear alignment of the project cycle with ADB's climate finance strategy. This ensures a balanced mix of financing for sub-projects with higher impact potential, as measured by climate indicators.

Combining catalytic and programmatic approaches will allow the facility to effectively structure innovative technical and financial models to maximize their CO2 mitigation potential early in the project cycle, while also ensuring that the projects are well aligned with ADB's climate finance strategy. This addresses structural gaps across the infrastructure life cycle to create multiple low-carbon projects.

Emphasis on bankable, blended and leveraged financing, using innovative approaches (with more novel models) for sub-projects with higher impact potential, such as those in the early stages of their lifecycle, and by capitalizing on ACF's pool of existing capital and financing partnerships with other development partners to provide co-financing or co-investment opportunities for high-potential climate and green sub-projects.

Emphasis on partnerships with National Development Banks and GCF DAEs, through proactive engagement with PT SM, Development Bank of Philippines, Land Bank of Philippines, Cambodia Rural Development Fund, and the Philippine Green Climate Fund to provide co-financing or co-investment opportunities for high-potential climate and green sub-projects.

Focus on projects responsive to country context in the face of COVID-19 economic disruptions by linking pricing to country socio-economic context (as measured by GDP per capita at purchasing power parity levels), providing longer grace periods to address expected depressed project revenues in the medium-term future.

### 9.3. Sustainable development (max. 500 words, approximately one page)

The ACGF GRP has been designed to support low-emission projects within the context of sustainable economic development, especially during the COVID-19 recovery period. At a broad level, the program directly supports the achievement of the following Sustainable Development Goals: No Poverty (1); Zero Hunger (2); Good Health and Well-being (3); Quality Education (4); Gender Equality (5); Decent Work and Economic Growth (7); Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure (8); Sustainable Cities and Communities (10); Life on Land (14); Life under water (15).

#### Economic co-benefits, including green jobs

The program is expected to contribute to the recovery by investing in green infrastructure that supports jobs and economic growth. Based on an assessment of projects in the ACGF pipeline, and the assumptions presented under the baseline scenario, it is estimated that the proposed investments in green infrastructure will support a range of broader economic benefits through specific investments such as enhanced trade and transport systems supporting small- and medium-enterprise development.

#### Environmental co-benefits

The program design specifically emphasizes the selection of projects that meet both climate and broader environmental objectives, using project criteria that combine both elements. ACGF investment criteria require projects to contribute to at least one of these two objectives as a minimum condition for an additional environmental objective. Specifically, the program is expected to:

(i) reduce greenhouse gas emissions (through transport systems and public transport);

(ii) improve water quality (through development of energy-efficient urban water supply and sanitation systems);

(iii) promote healthy and climate-resilient ecosystems and biodiversity (through reforestation and restoration projects; sustainable land management; sustainable agriculture practices to preserve soil carbon etc.); and

(iv) promote water resource efficiency (through reducing the use of energy, encouraging integrated waste management approaches etc.).

Contribution to environmental objectives will be monitored by the program based on project level reports, and reported to GCF through annual performance reports.

#### Social co-benefits and gender-sensitive development

Overall, ACGF GRP projects are expected to contribute to social inclusion and access to water and sanitation, with expected co-benefits in terms of enhanced access to energy; improved access to water supply; and improved access to sanitation and hygiene infrastructure.

Beyond this, ACGF GRP has been designed to strongly support sub-projects achieve co-benefits in terms of gender-sensitive development. As at program level, ACGF GRP has adopted a gender and social inclusion target (80% of projects directly advance gender and social inclusion) [GEIS by 2030] to help ensure the portfolio overall supports these issues.

The program GEIS/APP will help guide action at the sub-project level, including preparation of gender-sensitive project design; identification of gender-related risks and mitigation measures; and promotion of equitable participation in trainings and provision of green jobs for women and vulnerable groups.

At a sub-project level, potential environmental and social risks will be mitigated through the application of the program framework which is aligned with the ADB Safeguards Policy Statement (SPS); and through this, will be appraised to identify and mitigate any direct, indirect, cumulative, and induced risks and impacts such as:

(i) adverse impacts on biodiversity; (ii) adverse impacts on water resources; (iii) adverse impacts on indigenous peoples and their communities that may arise from the implementation of subprojects.

## Vulnerability of economies due to COVID-19

The COVID-19 health crisis has severely impacted the ability of governments to implement NDCs. Pre-COVID, the average annual investment gap in developing ASEAN member states was estimated at US$28 billion per year between 2016 and 2030 to support needed investment in climate-compatible infrastructure. The impact of COVID-19 is estimated to generate losses ranging from USD 87 billion to USD 1 trillion globally. In Southeast Asia alone, the region’s economies are expected to lose costs ranging from 11.6 trillion to over 18.4 trillion. Country-level estimates paint a more dire picture, with most countries in the region expected to record negative GDP growth in 2020, and face a fiscal headwind.

Dedicated capacity will also be established within ADB’s Environmental and Social Safeguards Unit (ESU). ## G.1 Environmental and social risk assessment (max. 750 words, approximately 1.5 pages)

The AGCF-GRP Environmental and Social Management Framework (ESMF) has been developed to ensure that the environmental and social risks associated with the project are minimized and mitigated to acceptable levels (see Annex 6). As a program managed by ADB, ADD’s policies, standards, and guidelines will apply. The ESMF outlines the roles, responsibilities, and procedures to be followed for every sub-project that receives funds from AGCF-GRP.

### Purpose of the ESMF

a) To identify, assess, manage, and monitor environmental and financial merit before being subject to safeguards screening, review and assessment.
b) To address impacts and risks of involuntary resettlement; and
c) To protect the environment, people’s health, and their communities that may arise from the implementation of sub-projects.

All sub-projects under the AGCF GRP will be processed by ADB, and will be subject to meeting the objectives set out in the ESMF. The ESMF is based on the IFC Environmental, Health and Safety (EHS) Guidelines to ensure adequate safeguards are in place for all projects.

As per ADB’s SPS, ADD will not finance sub-projects that do not comply with its safeguard policy statement, nor any project activities that contravene international law or national laws, policies, regulations, and standards, including those laws implementing host country obligations under international law. In addition, ADB may not finance activities on the prohibited investment activities list (SPS Appendix 5). All ADB safeguarded categories (A, B, C) of the ESMF will apply to all AGCF GRP sub-projects and ADD safeguards will apply to avoid, minimize, mitigate, and compensate potential risks and impacts.

In addition, as per ADB’s SPS, ADD will work with borrower governments to ensure that all potential AGCF GRP sub-projects:

i. are screened, categorized, and properly appraised in terms of their environmental and social impacts and risks;
ii. are reviewed and evaluated against national policies, laws, regulations and standards on environment, health, safety, involuntary resettlement and land acquisition, indigenous peoples and physical cultural heritage.

G.2. Gender assessment and action plan (max. 500 words, approximately 1 page)

## Summary of the Secretary’s assessment

### Project background

1. South-East Asia is one of the highest energy-intensive regions in the world, and with rapid urbanization and industrialization, coupled with increasing dependency on coal- and gas-fired power generation, the region faces significant challenges.
2. Most of these emissions are related to transport, construction and electricity use in buildings. In addition, the region has a high per capita consumption of cement, which is responsible for 10% of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. It has been estimated that countries in the region require over USD 200 billion annually just to meet their existing commitments under the Paris Agreement and invest in climate-compatible infrastructure and development; this gap is further widening owing to limited public budgets and the high risk associated with large public climate projects.
3. Against this backdrop, the region faces significant challenges in meeting its national climate targets. It is estimated that the regional economy will shrink by 10 per cent in 2020, affecting the countries’ abilities to meet their commitments under the Paris Agreement. At the same time, many developing country public budgets are already constrained and being diverted to support the COVID-19 emergency response measures.
4. The region is also facing a significant infrastructure gap, which has been further slowed and even halted infrastructure investments for supporting low-emission projects.

### Climate-related objective of this programme

5. The climate-related objective of this programme is to support the target countries in implementing their nationally determined contributions (NDCs). At present, there is no evidence of substantial progress towards meeting these commitments or any other national development commitments to be seen.
6. Without integrating climate considerations into key projects, there is a risk of rebound effects and increased emissions from existing infrastructure investments.
7. The programme therefore aims to initially support at least 20 subprojects in the target countries that are expected to result in an annual reduction of over 1 million metric tonnes carbon dioxide equivalent (MtCO₂e). Forty-five per cent of the subprojects would classify as Priority Subprojects, which are defined as projects with high potential for co-benefits such as renewable energy and energy efficiency, low-carbon transport, refurbishment and urbanisation.

### Programme overview

8. The programme has requested USD 300 million in total GCF financing (USD 280 concessional finance from the GCF and USD 20 million in non-concessional finance in technical assistance). At the subproject level, GCF funds will be complemented by parallel finance of up to USD 150 million. # Component by component analysis

## Component 1 - Provision of loans for de-risking low-emission projects (total: USD 4 billion, GCF USD 200 million)

### a. GCF loans will complement financing from the ASEAN Catalytic Green Finance Facility (ACGF) and ADB, and financing from other development partners (as appropriate), to de-risk funds for climate change mitigation and adaptation projects in Indonesia.

#### i. The loan proceeds will be used in a revolving structure with repayments from the first investment phase supporting subsequent investments in the second investment phase. This revolving structure is expected to support a substantial pipeline of 20 subprojects between 2021 and 2035.
#### ii. Progressively, this revolving structure will support up to 40 subprojects over time with increasing climate change mitigation impact, and progressively more sophisticated implementation structures more widely over time.

#### iii. Subprojects will be selected through predefined project-level eligibility criteria, which are consistent with the GCF’s investment policy and ADB’s country strategy.
#### iv. The focus of the subprojects will be on climate additionality and impact, and bankability to catalyze private sector participation in Indonesia’s low-emission development pathway.

#### v. The potential for generating green jobs in Indonesia is a key consideration in the selection of subprojects.

### b. GCF loans will be provided to sovereign borrowers on proposed lending terms (see table 3 in the funding proposal). The pricing proposed is based on extensive discussions with the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) and ADB, taking into account the following issues: (i) the need for a loan that can support the implementation of the ACGF pilot phase in 2019–2020; and takes into account the following issue: (ii) the need to provide a loan with terms that are consistent with Indonesia’s sovereign guarantees to all but the most competitive sources of finance which can demonstrate climate change mitigation impact, and bankability to catalyze private sector participation in projects, especially resulting from the COVID-19 crisis.

#### i. The proposed pricing is intended to be competitive for subprojects, especially if attempting to usher in untested or new technologies which are required to promote a transition towards low-emission development pathways.

51. Large scale renewable energy projects provide opportunities for direct employment for women and for men. ### ADF’s policy framework on gender equality and social inclusion

#### 66. As an ADB-managed program, the ACGF GRP GESAP builds on existing ADB systems to integrate gender mainstreaming and social inclusion into sector strategies.

##### 67. Accelerating progress in gender equality in Asia and the Pacific is one of seven operational priorities for ADB under its Strategy 2030. ADB recognizes that gender equality is critical in its own right, and to help realize socioeconomic development. Strategy 2030 holds that, to achieve a prosperous, inclusive, resilient, and sustainable region, it is essential to address the needs of women and girls. To this end, ADB has identified five areas for action in five areas: economic empowerment, human development, decision making and leadership, time poverty reduction, and resilience to external shocks. ADB is committed to support gender equality through gender-inclusive project designs and implementation.

##### 68. A Gender and Development Framework Policy**, is supported by a four-tier gender mainstreaming categorization framework with associated targets applying to both sovereign and non-sovereign operations. It is supported by a formal monitoring and evaluation framework. Under the ADB gender categorisation framework, a project is assigned one of the following categories:

- **a. Gender Equity Theme (GET)** where the project explicitly includes gender equal outcomes or empowerment outcomes.
  - A project is assigned GET if the project outcome directly addresses gender equality and/or women’s empowerment in any of the following areas: access to social services (e.g., education, health, and water supply/sanitation); and/or economic and financial resources and opportunities (e.g. employment opportunities, financial services, land, and markets), and/or basic rural and urban infrastructure; and/or enhancing voices and rights (e.g., decision making processes and structures, political empowerment, and grievance mechanisms).

- **b. Effective Gender Mainstreaming (EGM)** where the project is assigned EGM if the project outcome is not gender equality or women’s empowerment, but project outputs are designed to directly improve women's access to social services, and/or economic and financial resources and opportunities, and/or basic rural and urban infrastructure; and/or enhancing voices and rights in relation to gender equality or women’s empowerment.

- **c. Some Gender Elements (SGE)** where the project is assigned SGE if by its nature it is likely to directly improve women's access to social services, and/or economic and financial resources and opportunities, and/or basic rural and urban infrastructure; and/or enhance their voices and rights, but that included little, if any gender analysis and few or no specific design features, or is unlikely to directly improve women’s access to social services, and/or economic and financial resources and opportunities. No efforts were made during project preparation to identify potential positive and negative impacts on women.

- **d. No Gender Elements (NGE)** where the project is assigned NGE if it is not expected to provide direct/tangible benefits to women, although there may be indirect benefits for women.
