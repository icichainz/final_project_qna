# A.2 Executive Summary

## According to the Vulnerability and Climate Change Adaptation Index in the Latin America and Caribbean region, Cuba is classified as a "high-risk country". Observations show that the country's climate has been changing, and studies carried out over recent years have confirmed this trend. The main changes are: (i) increased frequency of extreme weather events; (ii) increasingly erratic seasonal rains; (iii) greater frequency of long and severe droughts; (iv) increase in severity of cyclonic phenomena.

## The changes in rainfall patterns and their overall reduction, as well as expected increases in evaporation due to increased temperatures, have a significant impact on the country's food security. This is particularly true with respect to agricultural production, which has been affected by climate change, ultimately impacting the livelihoods of farm households and the availability of agricultural and food products, ultimately putting food security at risk. Mitigated climate scenarios show that 65% of 239 crops studied will be negatively impacted in the next decade (see Table A-1). The most vulnerable crops are: maize, beans, sorghum, rice, cassava, and sweet potatoes (see Annexure A, Table 2). *Severity eight municipalities*, representing an area of 50,867 km² and about 46% of the national territory, are the most affected. These include: Ciego de Ávila, Camagüey, Holguín, Santiago de Cuba, Granma, Artemisa, Las Tunas, Corralito, Guaniguanico de Quinones y Santa Domingo in Las Vegas province; Los Altos en Matanzas province (Central Region); and Arroyo Naranjo, Jatibonico, and San Juan in Sancti Spíritus province. These municipalities have been selected as the target area for this project.

## Cuba is trending towards vulnerability to climate change based on historical records and climate models. The Government of Cuba has concluded that the entire archipelago is at risk. In 2017, the Council of Ministers approved "Frente Verde" (Green Front), a strategy aimed at reducing the adverse effects of climate change through immediate tasks, chosen based on a multidisciplinary analysis of vulnerabilities and other factors, with highest priority given to 15 of these municipalities. The municipalities of Ciego de Ávila, Camagüey, Holguín, Santiago de Cuba, Granma, Artemisa, Las Tunas, Corralito, Guaniguanico de Quinones y Santa Domingo in Las Vegas province; Los Altos en Matanzas province (Central Region); and Arroyo Naranjo, Jatibonico, and San Juan in Sancti Spíritus province have been selected as the target area for this project.

## The "Frente Verde" is divided into two main components: 
- A) adaptation of agricultural activities, particularly those with the greatest impact on the country's food security, to changes in climate conditions; and
- B) diversification of crops, improvement in soil condition, introduction and development of varieties resistant to new temperature regimes.

## This Project is fully aligned with the Government’s strategic actions defined to address the adverse effects of climate change in the agricultural sector and its objective is to increase the climate resilience of agricultural production and ensure food security. The project will be implemented through three phases: (i) a first phase, which includes the development of agroforestry, silvopastoral and forestry modules that are both productive and sustainable has been concluded; the second phase consists in the implementation of an action plan for the dissemination of knowledge on these modules, with emphasis on the knowledge of their modules’ performances in the field and at scale, enhance institutional capacities for extension and adaptation to climate change, and develop a third phase focused on the development of new agroecological systems enhancing techniques and practices to their agro-ecosystems. A third phase will, in future, take the lessons learned from this project into account with a view to further increasing the adaptive capacities to farmers nationwide, who will be supported by the financial mechanism established under this proposed GCF.

### B.1 Climate change and context

#### 9. Cuba is a Small Island Developing State (SIDS), consisting of the island of Cuba, the Isle of Youth and more than 1,600 islands and cays, which altogether cover a surface area of 113,822 km². The country is divided into Western, Central and Eastern Regions.

#### 10. According to the Vulnerability Index for Climate Change (VICC) in the Latin America and Caribbean Region, Cuba is classified as a "high risk" country in terms of its vulnerability to the effects of climate change. Observations indicate that climatic changes are already affecting the Cuban territory: i) an increase in temperature; ii) increasing extreme rainfall events; iii) greater frequency of long and severe droughts; iv) increased frequency and severity of cyclonic activity; and v) more intense and shorter coastal floods.

##### 10.1 Temperature rise: The surface air temperature has increased by 0.9°C since the middle of last century, conditioned by the increase of the average minimum temperature by 1.3°C, thereby producing a decrease in the daytime temperature oscillation (low temperatures). This increase is more evident for the Eastern Region, being even higher than the national average, at an incremental rate of 1.6% per year during the last 35 years.

##### 12. Changes in precipitation patterns: There has been a slight increase in positive precipitation anomalies since the late 1970s with a peak between 1984 and 1985, followed by a decrease until 1996-1997.

##### 13. Although the time series of annual precipitation observations for Cuba during 1963-2007 does not show a statistically significant trend, there has been a slight but steady increase in positive anomalies since the end of the 1970s and especially since the mid-1980s. The rise in annual mean values is fundamentally conditioned by the variations that occurred during the dry season.

#### Table 1 (Appendix 2 of the PDF plus page 4) shows for the dry season (corresponding to November-April), negative average values of rainfall, with a reduction of the magnitude and an increase in extreme positive anomalies. Consequently, there is a reduction of the range of variation of the magnitude means and an increase in extreme positive anomalies.

##### 15. Regarding the rainy season, the distribution of precipitation anomalies reveals a change in average values during recent decades, but contrary to the trend described for the dry season, in this case there is a tendency for negative anomalies at the beginning of the period (1963-2007), with a peak between 1984 and 1985. This is followed by an increase in the average values of rainfall, particularly in the Eastern Region. Most notable in this rainy period is the sharp decrease in the average values of rainfall for the last decade (2003-2007), which has been accompanied by a reduction in the range of variation of the magnitude means and an increase in extreme negative anomalies.

B.2. Theory of Change

35. The project’s Theory of Change starts with recognition of Cuba’s increasing vulnerability to CC due to its geographic location in the Caribbean region and its exposure to extreme weather events such as hurricanes, tropical storms or tornadoes. This is leading to flooding, land degradation from soil erosion and drought and the decline (30 day dry periods within the historic seasonal wet/dry cycle) of water availability for irrigation and drinking purposes. The vulnerability is further exacerbated by damage to coastal infrastructure and vegetation. While vulnerability varies somewhat across the island as a result of heterogeneous topography, climate change will increase the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events and climate variability and reduces the capacity of the country to maintain its food security.

36. Mechanisms: land use is configured, degraded rapidly, losing productivity and sustainability due to maladaptive land preparation, cultivation and irrigation techniques for monocultures leading to exposed soils, rapid runoff, infiltration by invasive species – particularly mangrove - soil erosion and sediment transport to coastal areas, salinization and increased risk of flooding. The vulnerability of the agricultural landscape is further exacerbated by extreme weather events and climate variability and reduces the capacity of the country to maintain its food security.

37. Effective climate change adaptation in this context requires climate-resilient production systems that can withstand or mitigate the effects of extreme weather events, such as hurricanes, tropical storms or tornadoes, prolonged droughts, downpours from hurricanes and tropical storm or too little (periodic) droughts, or lengthy dry periods in the growing season. These systems should be able to maintain or enhance soil fertility, reduce erosion, increase water infiltration and retention/enhancing hydrological regulation as a primary ecosystem service in the production landscape. As such, the priority is to improve the resilience of the agricultural landscape by increasing its capacity to withstand variability in rainfall flow rates, and to enhance infiltration into and through the soil profile, all the while maintaining or enhancing productivity. This can be achieved by improving water management practices (e.g., improved irrigation systems) that increase the amount of water infiltrated into the soil increases groundwater – vital for irrigation during drought and, in coastal areas, a fodder supply.

38. As a consequence, management to enhance the climate resilience of agroecosystems focuses on enhancing and managing the vegetative cover opposite to a strategy of both optimizing ecosystem function and meeting sustainable production functions. This is achieved by increasing the diversity of species within the landscape, which in turn increases the area of the production landscape will combine trees and/or shrubs with crops and livestock. These agroforestry and silvopastoral systems are more resilient than monocultures to extreme weather events.

39. The vulnerability of production landscapes to climate change is reduced with widespread adoption and implementation of climate-adapted, resilience-enhancing agroforestry and disjunctural systems.

40. The Cuban government has adopted a three-phase strategy approach to enhancing the resilience of agricultural landscapes to increasing climate change while generating mitigation benefits: a first phase of research and pilot of resilient agriculture; a second phase of scaling up these practices across the country, with emphasis on areas most vulnerable during phase one; and a third of upscaling countrywide based on lessons, capacities, financial mechanisms and enabling policies resulting from phase two implementation.

B.3 Project description

The project’s objective is to increase the climate resilience of agricultural production and ensure food security through improved ecosystem services from agroforestry, silvopastoral systems, reforestation and assisted natural forest regeneration in order to reduce vulnerability to climate change impacts. This will be achieved by improving water management and regulation of water flows into and through the production landscape, through landscape rehabilitation and management that enhances soil health and biodiversity, and through improved land use planning and practices that enhance carbon sequestration and prevent runoff off and erosion. - Prevalence of non-state agricultural production (e.g. Project Intervention Area (PIA) municipalities e.g. cooperatives, associations, etc.)

## B.5 Justification for GCF funding request

### Cuba is able to cover the incremental costs of adapting to climate change in light of the serious barriers it has historically faced in accessing international financing and the necessary technologies and equipment to build its climate resilience of its agricultural sector. However, the country faces a number of challenges that make adaptation more difficult than expected, particularly as Cuba's climate vulnerability continues to increase in high priority sectors such as agriculture. These include: (i) increasing frequency and intensity of extreme weather events; (ii) increased temperatures leading to increasing rainfall variability – including greater numbers and intensities of hurricanes and tropical storms – and drought attributed to the El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO); (iii) soil degradation due to climate change; and (iv) limited concessional grants: the only option for financing to overcome barriers related to water security undermined by climate change. Fiscal limitations hinder the potential for investments to meet the additional costs of building resilience in agricultural production systems. Costs of effective adaptation to climate change will only rise with delay in action and investment.

### Cuba has cope with these ongoing impediments to investment in equipment and technology by building its capacities for knowledge, skills and innovation. The country has made significant progress in this area over recent years. Nevertheless, additional investment is urgently needed to cover the costs of building the capacities of farmers and producers.

### D.3 Sustainable development

#### Environmental co-benefits will be produced by this project as a result of agricultural diversification and the extension of agroforestry systems in support of climate adaptation and mitigation. The project will also promote the use of green manure crops to improve soil structure, soil compaction and provide shade for soil, thereby reducing soil temperatures, erosion and the speed of physical, chemical and microbiological processes in the soil. ### D. Efficiency and Effectiveness

#### 1. Climate Change Impacts on Agriculture

Climate change is already affecting agricultural systems in Cuba, including forests and pasture, and it is expected to worsen according to climate change projections. Increased temperatures and variations in precipitation patterns are projected to reduce crop yields and increase the frequency of extreme weather events. This will put pressure on farmers' capacity for adaptation and mitigation.

Rehabilitating areas after Marabá infestation has a high cost-endorsement and farmers who own land with this problem have limited access to credit, which leads to low investment in rehabilitation activities. This leads to lands abandonment, limiting the development of production systems that combine soil and water resources, building resilience to CC and generate income to improve livelihoods.

#### 2. Financial Analysis

The revenues accruing from the agroforestry modules in Output 1 of the project were considered, as well as the production costs incurred. Taking into consideration that the agroforestry modules proposed in Output 1 of this project are expected to be more productive than the current land use (agriculture), it is expected that revenues would be incremental. Over the project lifespan of 20 years, the proposed models proved to be financially viable (given the interest rate of return between ranged from -58% to +34%). The Incremental Net Present Value (INPV) for the IES project, specifically, the net present value, the internal rate of return and the equivalent annual NPV for Financial Evaluation were calculated. The results show that the models generate benefits for farmer families that are higher than the investment costs.

#### 3. Economic Analysis

An economic analysis was carried out for the entire project, considering the costs of implementing the full projects (US$ 38.20 million contributed by the Green Climate Fund and US$ 81.70 million of national matching contribution) and projecting the benefits that will be generated in the future.

The project is expected to generate economic benefits in terms of ecosystem services (avoided soil erosion and carbon capture) in the biomes where interventions will be carried out (vegetation cover, agroforestry systems, silvopastoral systems, and natural regeneration). The estimated internal rate of return of 15.4%. In other words, with an investment of US$93.9 million, it is expected to create economic benefits in excess of the opportunity cost of the investment (US$826,841), an economic net present value per household of US$70,457.15 and an economic net present value per beneficiary of $43,743, considering 3.2 family members per household.

#### 4. Cost-Effectiveness

The cost-effectiveness of this project was determined by evaluating previous in-country experiences with rehabilitation of Marabá infested areas as well as agroforestry, silvopastoral systems and natural regeneration. The design of project investments is based on the experience gained from these projects and their ability to provide a high degree of confidence that the project's predicted impacts will be achieved cost-effectively.

## Project Background

### 2.1.3 Background/history of the project to inform assessment of the funding proposal:

- This project constitutes the second phase of a three-phase programme to enhance the climate resilience of Cuba's agricultural sector countryside.
- During the first phase, implemented by the Ministry of Agriculture (MINAG) of Cuba, research, testing and analysis of potential climate-resilient crops were conducted. In this phase, intercropping, silvopastoral and forestry models were identified that are both productive and sustainable.
- The present project will build on these results to scale up the proven modules at the macro level in order to build their performance at scale and enhance institutional capacity for climate-resilient agriculture.

There are unanimous perceptions in these territories that women are in greater conditions of vulnerability than men due to the persistence of machista practices that perpetuate stereotypes and traditional roles of women that put them at a disadvantage.

It is necessary to identify and the articulation of key actors who can promote a substantive movement for gender equality in the territories.
