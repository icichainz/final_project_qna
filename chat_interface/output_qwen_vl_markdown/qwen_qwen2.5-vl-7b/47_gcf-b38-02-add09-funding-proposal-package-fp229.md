## Proposed Interventions

### Acumen Climate Action Pact (ACAP) Fund

The ACAP Fund will be Pakistan’s first adaptation focused fund supporting climate-smart, early growth, and growth stage agriculture-based building climate resilience. The Fund is a USD 90M blended financing facility with USD RM of catalytic investment capital and a USD 10M grant funded Technical Assistance Facility (TAF) to support the development of an enabling environment for adaptation.

The ACAP Fund will provide Pakistan with a comprehensive approach to climate change adaptation solutions across three key themes to support smallholder farmers and build ecosystem level climate resilience. The three key themes are:

* **Theme 1: Climate-smart Agriculture (CSA)**: This theme aims at increasing the productivity of smallholder farmers by providing them with access to climate-resilient seeds, improved irrigation systems, and other inputs.
* **Theme 2: Early Growth and Growth Stage Agriculture**: This theme focuses on supporting early growth stage agriculture-based projects that are likely to be more vulnerable to climate change impacts. These projects will receive funding for the development of new technologies or innovations that can help them adapt to changing climatic conditions.
* **Theme 3: Building Ecosystem Level Climate Resilience**: This theme aims at building ecosystem level resilience by supporting initiatives such as reforestation, afforestation, and other land use changes that can help mitigate climate change impacts.

## Barriers:

### Barrier 1: Conventional, fragmented and highly vulnerable agricultural sector

The agriculture sector in Pakistan, like many other developing countries, remains fragmented, inefficient, and highly climate-vulnerable. The country's agriculture production depends largely on arid farming methods that have left farmers with little or no control over their yields (due to factors such as water scarcity, soil degradation, price uncertainty, etc.). Owing to these structural inefficiencies, Pakistan's agricultural productivity is approximately 30% lower than the global average. Smallholder farms in Pakistan are more vulnerable and less productive; they have a lower level of access to inputs and services compared with larger farmers. The average farm size for smallholders is 1.5 hectares (ha), while large farms have an average area of 27 ha. Smallholder farms also have lower productivity levels, as their yields per hectare are 30% lower than average for small farmers, who account for 90% of the total agricultural workforce in Pakistan.

### Barrier 2: Lack of patent and efficient capital for agribusinesses

Access to finance is a major constraint on the growth of agriculture business growth in Pakistan. The banking sector, which is the largest player in the financial sector primarily lends to the government - and this factor is particularly evident when it comes to lending against agricultural collateral. In 2015, only 3% of all loans were granted for securities in order to earn high returns. Private sector credit as a percentage of GDP is under 15%.

The situation is even worse for the agriculture sector where there is a critical need for growth capital on flexible terms, sensitive to the cyclical nature of the sector. ### Barrier 2: Ag-SMEs are largely informal and lack the capacity to absorb private capital

Due to the high cost of deploying credit to informally structured entities, formal banking institutions are often reluctant to provide much-needed credit from venture to stage businesses. This is a major factor impeding the growth of ag-SMEs in Pakistan. The sector also faces other challenges such as limited access to finance, lack of business registration, and maintain accurate financial records.

This makes it even more difficult for these entities to access formal financing. In addition, the informal nature of many ag-SMEs combined with stringent collateral requirements, dissuades businesses from accessing credit through formal financial institutions. This is a major barrier that hinders the growth – particularly given robust enough resources to navigate climate threats that are a core business risk.

### Barrier 4: Limited farmer understanding of climate change impacts

In Pakistan, farmers have been identified as one of the most vulnerable groups in the world, ranked 18 out of 191 countries by an index developed by INFORM. A multi-stakeholder forum for developing shared, quantitative analyses relevant to Pakistan’s agricultural sector has been established (the Pakistan Climate Change Forum). The country also deals with mass migration due to climate change impacts such as desertification, soil erosion, water scarcity, crop damage, livestock loss, and disruption to transportation infrastructure – all outcomes that negatively impact food security.

The World Bank, in its report “Pakistan: Building Resilience Against Climate Change” (2019), suggests that the majority of the farmers do not consider climate change a potential threat to agriculture. This is due to the fact that most farmers are illiterate and lack access to information on climate change. However, it has been observed that owing to a recent increase in extreme weather events, some Pakistani farmers have begun to perceive future occurrences as more likely.

### Barrier 5: Low gender equity across agriculture sector

In Pakistan’s agricultural sector, women make up of 40% of the workforce. However, according to the UNDP, in spite of women’s contribution to the sector, women remain at a disadvantage as compared to male farmers in terms of access to resources and services. This is reflected by the fact that (i) only 25% of women achieve literacy rates (40.84% of women are literate vs. 71.15% of men); and (iv) land ownership (only 1.2% of women own land). Women also have limited access to credit, inputs, extension services, and training.

In addition, women farmers manage farmed areas that are 20%-30% less productive than farms managed by men. It is estimated that closing this gender gap could increase the productivity of Pakistan’s agricultural sector by up to 15%. This would also improve the lives of rural households. Additionally, this low level of gender equity reduces the capacity of women to adapt to the impact of climate change.

- 40% of the world's food is lost or wasted annually due to poor post-harvest management practices.

#### Factors Contributing to Post-Harvest Losses:
1. Harvesting, handling, storage, and transportation.
2. Inadequate control systems and air circulation in storage facilities.
3. Poor infrastructure for cold chain logistics.
4. Insufficient post-harvest technologies such as ethylene and carbon dioxide absorbers.

- Implementing post-harvest technologies can significantly reduce food losses associated with evening milk (which previously either spoiled or was forcibly discarded).
- Ethylene absorbers can be used to extend the shelf life of fruits and vegetables.
- Carbon dioxide absorbers can help maintain optimal storage conditions for perishable goods.

## 2012

In 2012, Pakistan devised its first National Climate Change Policy (NCCP), focusing on adaptation and mitigation efforts through a multistakeholder approach. The primary goals of the policy were:

- Maintaining appropriate economic goals while addressing the challenges of climate change
- Integrating climate change into national policies
- Engaging in pro-poor, gender sensitive adaptation and advancing cost-effective mitigation efforts

- Restricting near-minimalist decision making and coordination mechanisms on climate change


## 2016

On becoming a party to the Paris Agreement, Pakistan developed its first climate action plan, the Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC). The NDC presents a strategy to cope with the country's GHG emissions in line with Paris Agreement commitments. It also outlines the steps required for implementation and monitoring of the NDC.

The NDC provides a roadmap for implementing nature-based solutions (NbS), developing land-use change and forestry strategies, and enhancing Pakistan’s climate-resilient infrastructure. Some of the NDC’s guiding principles included:

- NbS green livelihood opportunities
- Improved cross-referencing to climate change in national and provincial policies and action plans on climate adaptation and mitigation
- Climate-informed preparatory and approval systems dealing with the life-cycle of projects and schemes
- Explicit inclusion of gender equality, social equity, and environmental justice principles when commissioning capital-intensive projects
- Prioritizing engagement with civil society groups to engage in, and benefit from, Pakistan’s adaptation and mitigation objectives and targets

* **The ACAPF Fund’s private equity fund will complement structured private capital and technical assistance investments with growth potential, improving SHF resilience. It will be Pakistan's first adaptation-focused fund to support the venture, early growth and stage agribusinesses. # Executive Summary

The Acumen Climate Action Pakistan Fund (ACAP) seeks to build climate resilience for the agricultural sector and smallholder farmers in Pakistan by investing in impactful agribusinesses. Smallholder farmers are increasingly vulnerable due to climate change, including frequent droughts and floods that have devastated over 1/3 of Pakistan's farmers. The impacts of climate change have been damaging to crops, livestock, and livelihoods, leading to reduced yields, increased costs, and decreased crop diversity. Farmers have experienced decreased yields, less income, and more vulnerability to extreme weather events. By investing in agribusinesses selling products and services to smallholder farmers, ACAP aims to increase their access to climate-smart agriculture technologies, such as improved seeds, smart farming, and post-harvest technologies.

The ACAP Fund is based on the premise that if investment capital and technical assistance are provided to smallholder farmers through agribusinesses in partnership with the development of strategic public-private partnerships then agribusinesses and Pakistan's agricultural sector at large have demonstrated a viable solution for climate change. The ACAP Fund has developed this gender assessment to understand how it can support vulnerable farmers and catalyze private capital because the investment and partnerships will bolster women’s economic empowerment, increase their income, and make them increasingly vulnerable ag-sector, enhancing its ability to adapt to climate threats impacting food security.

To ensure the effectiveness of the ACAP activities, it is important to understand the differences between men and women in terms of access to resources, decision-making power, and exposure to risks related to climate change, and vulnerabilities and barriers that are important to the stakeholders of this project. The Acumen Climate Action Pakistan (ACAP) Fund has developed this gender assessment to understand the important role of women in agriculture in Pakistan, and how ACAP can play a positive role for gender equity in agriculture by investing in agribusinesses that support women farmers.

The assessment includes an overview of the evidence on women's access to agricultural inputs and services, women’s economic empowerment, and the role of women in agriculture with respect to advancing equality in the sector. This document outlines key entry points for the ACAP initiative, followed by detailed information about the project, including its objectives, target populations, vulnerable populations, sector specific gender outcomes, and project barriers and opportunities.

The ACAP gender assessment has informed the Gender Action Plan and the intended activities of the fund.


Objective of the Assessment

Rationale

ACAP seeks to build climate resilience of farmers and agribusiness with a gender lens. The project's impacts, outputs, and activities are enhanced when women are included. Women are an important part of the social fabric of Pakistan’s rural areas and play a significant role in food production and consumption; women play a significant role in Pakistan’s history and economy. # Legal Status of Women

Legal protections for women were enshrined in the Constitution of Pakistan in 1973. The Constitution has provisions on equality of women in several articles. Article 2 contains a commitment to eliminate the exploitation of women. Article 25 (1) and (2) states that "all citizens are equal under the law and are entitled to equal protection of law" and "there shall be no discrimination on the basis of sex alone," respectively.

Women in Pakistan have earned increasingly strong legal protections and rights in recent history. Last year, the government of Pakistan developed the National Gender Policy Framework, and the Anti-Rape (Investigation, Prevention and Protection) Act 2017 to combat violence against Women (Prevention and Protection) Act in all four provinces of Pakistan in 2020.

Women are allowed to vote, run for elected office, and hold government positions. Women are also allowed to own property.

Rural women in Pakistan have high literacy rates and have very limited civic education on their protections and rights, which has limited their understanding of laws and protections. Beyond lacking awareness about their legal rights, rural women face a number of other barriers that make it difficult for them like shelters. Additionally, the systems in place seem remote, expensive, and inaccessible for many women in rural areas.

Women also lack financial resources that may enable their legal protections. One study found that 78% of women and girls reporting abuse did not have any income.*

Moreover, there is limited female representation in the legal sector including in the police, prosecution, and the judiciary. In 2019, only 35% of police officers were women; 46% of prosecutors are women; judges are women.* The limited representation of women in the Pakistani legal system has impacted the perception of accessibility of the legal system.

Beyond perceived income barriers and other issues with accessibility including a dearth of courts or police stations in rural and remote areas. Both police officials and judges alike encourage female victims of rape and sexual assault to mediate their solutions rather than use formal channels for resolving their cases. In addition, women are often discouraged from reporting crimes against them by community elders and respected individuals who resolve civil disputes. DRCs have been quick and effective in addressing these issues. However, the lack of infrastructure in rural areas in Pakistan have to travel far to access DRCs. Adding to the challenges for women, court rooms and police stations have limited infrastructure for female women’s rest rooms, sitting rooms, and private rooms.

Some researchers have reported that women are often reluctant to report crimes against them or reporting and investigating SEAH cases. They also have limited resources for forensics investigations of women and researchers have reported inefficient and dysfunctional staff making evidence gathering more difficult.

Lawyers and prosecutors have also had limited success prosecuting rapists or defending women. If women are praised as promiscuous or of low moral character, they are far less likely to succeed in court. Moreover, the aforementioned challenge with evidence handling has made prosecuting SEAH crimes far less successful.

# ACAP Gender Assessment

Women in Pakistan have faced significant barriers towards work, income, and wealth-generating opportunities. In 2018, only 35% of the Pakistani labor force was female. The average working woman makes up 9.3% of a man's income. Women make up 81 percent of the 5.36 million Pakistanis people working in the informal sector. Even as women overwhelmingly contribute to the informal sector, accounting for 70-80% of its workforce, they are paid less than men and have fewer opportunities for growth. Low pay is related to low-income security, few social protections, high economic vulnerability, and occupational segregation.

Pakistan has some of the worst female employment rates across Balochistan, KP, and Sindh, which is around 10%. These would mirror the worst countries in the world.

According to the World Bank, women only account for 1% of entrepreneurs in Pakistan. The lack of free movement, limited access to financial services, and restrictions on entrepreneurship and formal labor jobs restricts opportunities for women in Pakistan. Additionally, women lack support networks, business premises, and access to markets. These factors have led to a gender gap in entrepreneurship and deeply held beliefs about gender roles have limited women's opportunities for entrepreneurship.

Women are also unlikely to receive encouragement from male family members further hampering mobility and fundraising. A World Bank Report found that less than 25% of female entrepreneurs are microfinance borrowers, with most of them receiving loans for the purchase of raw materials or equipment for any kind of firm.10

Given the scarcity of female entrepreneurs, researchers have studied the characteristics of female entrepreneurs. Successful female entrepreneurs in Pakistan have demonstrated internal factors including self-confidence, risk taking, and need for achievement, and external factors including economic and socio-cultural support.

## Vulnerable Groups

### Female Headed Households

In 2018, female headed households in Pakistan comprised 12.5% of all households. This increased from 10.4% in 2007. These households primarily led by women face a heightened risk of poverty due to several factors including lack of access to education and training opportunities, limited access to productive resources, income, and essential services. Additionally, the weakening of traditional familial support systems has left these women more vulnerable as they are often responsible for their families' security. Furthermore, these women encounter the added challenge of balancing market-oriented activities with their domestic responsibilities. These households are also at a heightened risk of poverty because they serve as mothers and workers; their employment options primarily lie within the informal sector. Unfortunately, within this sector, opportunities for empowered women are severely limited, reflecting the cultural norms in Pakistan that restrict females to roles where gender segregation can be maintained.

## Agricultural Credit

In Pakistan, the high cost of deploying credit to informal entities has resulted in limited credit opportunities for men and women smallholder farmers. Many agribusinesses operate in the informal sector, which is not regulated by any government agency or institution.

Given their informal nature, including informal structure, and remaining unregulated, commercial credit institutions are reluctant to extend loans to these agribusinesses, especially women and smallholder farmers. Additionally, the high cost of credit has limited formal agribusinesses from entering this market.

As men tend to own agricultural land, products and services, they are more likely to have the collateral and necessary inputs to access credit. Women are much less likely to access credit because they do not own land or other assets that can be used as collateral, especially those in rural areas of Pakistan, who have low levels of access to agricultural credit.

Some smallholder farmers have accessed credit via informal relationships with friends and family but that is not well documented.

The National Commission for Women in Pakistan has encouraged female farmers to establish asset ownership and has worked with almost 2,000 women in rural areas working in the carpentry industry.[2]

## Gender and Climate Resilience

A recent ranking of 87 lower- and middle-income countries on a climate agriculture gender inequality hotspot index (CAGI) by CGIAR researchers shows Pakistan ranking number one in Asia. These hotspots are defined as areas where climate hazards, women’s exposure to climate hazards because of their involvement in agricultural activities, and the lack of access to resources intersect.

What's more, a province-level analysis highlighted Balochistan, Punjab, and Sindh as hotspots within the country. Women farmers in these provinces engage heavily in rice and oilseed production, and are often solely responsible for food security and nutrition, especially during the lean season as for dairy farming. This puts them at risk of suffering under current floods and future climate disasters.

During a disaster, women farmers are not compensated for crop losses as they do not own the land but on it. They also have no access to credit or insurance schemes that could only be availed by farmers who owned up to 25 acres of land.[3] This meant that women stood to gain nothing from such disasters.

Women farmers’ ability to cope with floods and other climate shocks also hinges on their access to credit, knowledge and skills. Only 5.3 percent, 1.6 percent and 8.9 percent of rural women in Punjab, Sindh and Balochistan have access to formal financial services, respectively, and they hardly get loans from formal channels as they barely have any land to offer as collateral.

## ACAP Program Activities, Outcomes, and Engagement

### ACAP and Gender

Given the significance of women in agriculture and the gender inequity within the sector, ACAP will be applying a gender lens across all investments. ## ACAP and Vulnerable Sub-Groups

As agricultural sector is the backbone of Pakistan, the ACAP team will be dedicating a significant component of the fund TAF for gender initiatives. The team has dedicated significant time to understanding climate impacts for women in Pakistan and particularly in the agriculture sector which is extremely climate vulnerable.

Globally, women are more vulnerable to climate change impacts compared to men. This is also evident in Pakistan where women have less access to resources such as land, water, and credit. Women face heightened risks of economic instability, compromised well-being, limited educational achievements, and personal safety. The ACAP team has identified that women’s vulnerability to climate change is due to their vulnerability as women are systematically excluded from decision-making processes concerning matters directly affecting them. This exclusion limits the potential for women to be effective agents in contributing to the formulation and execution of strategies addressing climate change. Consequently, their potential to serve as effective catalysts for change remains untapped.

Diverse groups within Pakistan face different levels of vulnerability. Their ability to manage, react, and adjust varies based on geographical location, livelihoods, and socioeconomic status. Climate change particularly impacts women in rural areas who are more vulnerable due to limited access to resources such as land, lacking the resources (including assets and access to services) to withstand natural calamities and extreme weather events. Women’s vulnerability is further compounded by their role as primary caregivers bearing a disproportionate burden. However, women seldom participate in decision-making processes concerning matters directly affecting them.

Throughout Pakistan, women are part of the millions of landless laborers and small-scale farmers engaged in combating rural poverty. They actively participate in activities such as farming, tending to livestock, and managing household chores. Women often have a dual role as they are also family members in tasks like cultivating crops, harvesting, and processing, and they assume additional duties when men migrate for work or other reasons.

The specific roles and responsibilities of women vary, influenced by factors such as geographical location, farming methods, and local cultural traditions. Therefore, while developing the fund thesis, the ACAP team has:

- Conducted focus group discussions with rural women and female farmers to understand their challenges in accessing resources.
- Conducted further research and interviewing rural women across multiple areas in Pakistan in order to better understand the needs of a diverse group of vulnerable women, children, and the elderly.

The findings from these activities will be shared with stakeholders across provinces in Pakistan which we will augment through primary research/consultations
