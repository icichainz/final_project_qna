# A2. Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

This proposal on Bangladesh is part of a multi-country "Global Clean Cooking Program" planned for support by the World Bank (WB) and the Deutsche Gesellschaft für Internationale Zusammenarbeit (GIZ) with a goal to support development of an alternative fuel supply system in Bangladesh. The project will focus on promoting clean cooking technologies at supporting a sustainable market for adoption of improved cookstoves (ICCs) that will contribute to improve well-being of people, environment and economy.

For a pipeline of activities planned by the WB under the Global Clean Cooking Program please see more details in section 1.2. For further information regarding the joint strategic partnership see Annex I. Bangladesh is one of the world’s poorest countries, with an average per capita income of $300. Cooking food at home is predominantly done in traditional stoves using fuel wood. Burning solid fuels for cooking releases emissions of carbon dioxide, methane, black carbon and particulate matter. The use of biomass as a source of energy has been associated with deforestation and unsustainable wood harvesting. In traditional cookstoves, incomplete combustion of biomass fuels is inefficient and results in the release of toxic fumes into the environment. This can lead to respiratory problems for women and children. In Bangladesh, about 145 million people are exposed to HAP and this contributes to 46,000 casualties per year.

Since the 1980’s Bangladesh has faced steady population growth, placing pressure on the forest resources. A study conducted in Bangladesh between 1988 and 1998 published by the Federal Research Division of the Library of Congress, found that the total forest area had decreased from 24% to 13%. The following table shows the findings of this study: "Wood is the main fuel for cooking and other domestic requirements. It is not surprising that population pressure has had an adverse effect on the indigenous forests." The total forest area has now decreased to 10% of the land only (FAO, 2005). This decline in forest cover has been attributed to increased demand for firewood and charcoal in Bangladesh, dates back prior to December 1989.

Literature shows that the forest cover is seriously decreasing in Bangladesh with the rate for deforestation not repurposing. Due to this, the government of Bangladesh has banned the use of fuel wood for domestic and industrial use, etc. Biomass is the predominant source of fuel for cooking in Bangladesh and fuelwood constitutes about 43% of total cooking biomass used in the country. The rest comes from crop residues (25%), animal dung (18%) and other solid waste (14%). (source collected biomass list). As per the official COM/UNFCCC approved calculations, 65.4% of fuelwood used in cooking is not efficient. This means that only about 34.6% of the total energy content of the fuelwood used in cooking is actually converted into heat for cooking. The installation of improved cookstoves (ICCs) and the use of gas stoves installed under the IDCOL program will have direct contribution to carbon emission reductions. ## B1. Description of Financial Elements of the Project / Programme

An integrated financial model has been provided as Annex 3 which shows the expected financial performance of the POs involved in the project. The model is based on a number of assumptions, including that the rural population will be willing to pay for high-quality ICS and that there are sufficient demand and supply conditions for high-quality ICS.

The primary barriers to adoption of ICS in Bangladesh are (Annexes 2 and 10):

- Lack of consumer ability or willingness to pay premiums on more efficient cookstoves - which have been / are being developed due to absence of wide-spread awareness campaigns.
- The lack of a supply chain that can deliver the stoves subscribed to rural households and cater to the unique cooking needs and preferences of rural population.
- The limited funding to support scale-up activities.

Lack of willingness or ability of consumers to pay premiums on more efficient cookstoves is due to a number of factors, including:

- Lack of awareness of financial and health benefits of ICS.
- Lack of confidence in the genuineness of the claims made by the ICS sellers about the benefits of ICS.
- Many households do not pay for fuel, which makes the financial case for investment difficult to make.

The grant support will address these barriers by (i) creating consumer awareness about the benefits of ICS through above and below-the-line marketing campaigns; (ii) providing funding to POs (Participating Organizations) for distribution of ICS; (iii) selecting the right stove models that have passed both quality assurance test on performance and durability as well as consumer preference and acceptability tests. Households are expected to purchase the stoves at a premium price, which will be paid by the POs through grant funding. The POs will provide households to purchase the ICS as well as provide a "first-mover" incentive for Partner Organizations to develop and market their own stove models.

Among the stoves installed in the first phase (WB RERED II – 1 million units, Annexes 2 and 10) of the program, mostly two models of portable stoves constituted about 95% of the total installation. The remaining 5% of the stoves were single and double-chambered wood-burning stoves.

The program proposed under the GCF grant, IDCOL intends to increase the share of chimney stoves, metallic stoves and compressed fuel-based stoves in the overall stove market by providing funding for the distribution of these stoves. This switch will result in additional GHG emissions reductions, HAP and other benefits. By supporting the demand creation and supply chain activities through the Project, the availability of higher efficiency ICS will increase consumer confidence on the benefits of ICS.

In order to a menu of options that will suit different market segments with varying preferences and affordability, IDCOL ICS program is open to different models of stoves after rigorous testing of efficacy and acceptability. Realizing that the market for high-efficiency ICS in Bangladesh is still nascent, IDCOL has decided to focus on the lower end of the market where there are more opportunities for demand creation. To ensure more efforts at demand creation, a varied amount of grant per stove is proposed under the Project with more support for stoves that have been established in the market through the first phase of the program (as explained in C2.5). This will ensure incentives for the POs for selling different types of stoves as per customers' preferences rather than focusing on a particular model. ## Challenges faced and lessons learned in phase 1 of the IDCOL ICS program

### Supply chain: Production of ICS with consistent quality requires producers to use good models.
IDCOL identified engineering workshops capable of producing moulds in each division of the country and provided them with proper guidance. As a result, capacity to produce moulds developed among several engineering workshops and now POs can make their own moulds.

### Source of skilled technicians: Scarcity of skilled human resources, mostly technicians and masons, was a problem at the start of the program.
IDCOL arranged several training programs to create skilled technicians and masons. In addition, IDCOL provided financial support for the purchase of tools and equipment by the POs.

### Adequate training and engagement of local entrepreneurs helped to ensure availability of skilled technicians and masons under the program.

#### Stove testing facility: Underpinning Inspection:
IDCOL inspectors collect data from the households on ICS usage level. The ICS usage is categorized in four; it always use, it frequently use, it occasionally use; and (e) does not use. In October 2017, IDCOL started to establish stove testing facilities at its regional offices for regular inspection of the customers which was a serious concern for the program. The POs argued that the inspection of ICS took place within their own premises and they were already doing it regularly. However, IDCOL insisted on establishing stove testing facilities.

As a result of IDCOL's emphasis on this issue, the POs started to provide more focus on awareness building and user training under the program to make sure that customers were using their stoves.

#### Quality assurance: Underpinning Inspection:
IDCOL has established a quality assurance system for ICS. The system is based on a particular disbursement request of a cluster if more than 20% inspected ICS from that request are found to be of inadequate quality. This helps in ensuring the quality of stoves.

#### Graduation to higher performance ICS: The IDCOL ICS Program has discontinued support to the lowest performing ICSs and shifted focus towards graduation to higher performance ICSs.

#### Lack of ICS testing facilities: Before the IDCOL ICS Program, the concept of testing of ICS was limited to efficiency parameters only. IDCOL's initiatives have helped establishment of three testing facilities in Bangladesh and enabled POs to test their stoves regularly. This has been a major milestone for the program as it is an important step towards establishing a stove testing facility of international standards in Bangladesh which is important for sustainability of the program.

#### Underpricing of the stoves: One of the challenges the program faced was underpricing of stoves by some POs in order to attract customers. This resulted in poor quality ICSs being sold at low prices, which in turn, on one hand, reduced operating costs and HR overheads per stove. However, selling at lower prices engendered several issues including market distortion, wrong marketing messages and push-sell among others. To deal with this, it was decided to discontinue support for the lowest performing ICSs and shift focus towards graduation.

Also IDCOL decided on grant support on the basis of type of the stoves with a view to discourage underpricing.

## Demand for ICS

In 2015 Bangladesh had a population of 161 million people (World Bank) living in more than 37 million households, giving an average household size of 4.35. By 2050 it is estimated that the population will grow to 285 million, and the number of households could increase by over 90%. This means that Bangladesh presents more than 70% of the total, with around 29 million households and only 1 million have access to ICS solutions.

At present, close to 90% of the population in Bangladesh cook with biomass fuels using traditional stoves with less than 10% thermal efficiency. The main cooking fuel is wood (45%), followed by dung (38%) and crop residue (17%). Traditional forest degradation, with 50% of fuel being sourced unsustainably. In rural households, the main supply for cooking is wood (44%), then crop residue (45%) and dung (9%). Fuelwood has become increasingly scarce and more expensive, which has led to a shift in cooking fuels from firewood to agricultural residues. These residues are used as animal feed waste, cow dung, agricultural waste) are used for cooking annually and are the only feasible cooking energy solution for over 80% of rural households. The average cooking time per meal is around 3 hours. Cooking time during the dry season period for ICS can be as little as 3-4 minutes. Looking at other fuels, natural gas and LPG have presence in urban areas but the infrastructure to supply these fuels is limited and high costs (for later) make these an unfavorable option for many. 60% of urban population still rely on biomass.

All of these factors suggest that biomass will continue to be the dominant fuel in both urban and rural settings for many years to come. This represents an opportunity for more fuel efficient stoves to enter urban markets as well as rural ones. Overall, there is a huge potential market for ICS in Bangladesh with 10 million households (37%) having access to only one stove, while over 50% of households using more than one stove, this could lead to the total number of stoves in use to reach over 90 million.

## Supply of ICS

Despite several various approaches to introduce cleaner and more efficient cooking solutions since the 1980s, in terms of coverage, only about 3-5 percent of households relying on traditional biomass fuels have access to an improved cookstove today, which suggests efforts in the sector have not yet succeeded in creating a sustainable market for stoves. Traditional stove producers are small-scale enterprises that produce and sell their products at local markets or through brick stoves, with households also often building their own stoves at home. The signs of market transformation, however, are unfolding as more and more countries are adopting policies to promote the use of ICSs. This is especially true recently, World Bank/IDCOL programs. These programs have demonstrated that decentralized production of relatively simple stoves can be a viable business model for small-scale enterprises.

However, these programs could achieve far more if a more coordinated approach was taken to stimulating consumer demand, supporting SME & large NGO development whilst also enhancing access to finance for stove producers.

### D1. VALUE ADDED FOR GCF INVOLVEMENT

Please specify why the GCF involvement is critical for the project/programme, in consideration of other alternatives.

GCF support for the clean cooking sector in Bangladesh is essential. Funding in the clean cooking sector is urgently needed to address the problem of indoor air pollution caused by inefficient stoves used by very poor households in rural areas of the country (31% of people in Bangladesh live below the poverty line of $2 per day).

The proposed Project will scale up support for a scheme that is an important contributor to the vision of the Government of Bangladesh to achieve 100% coverage of improved cookstoves by 2030 as per Bangladesh Government’s Country Action Plan for Clean Cookstoves (CAP). The target of this CAP is to disseminate cleaner cooking stoves to over 30 million households in rural areas. This project will build on the success and lessons learned from the pilot phase, indicating the scale of challenge and the critical need for a more coordinated, integrated and innovative approach.

The Government of Bangladesh has given a high priority to addressing the cookstove sector as expressed in the Clean Cookstove Country Action Plan. To this end, in 2012, the Government borrowed $12 million from the IDA for an improved stove project (IDB-34589) and launched the Rural Energy Efficiency Development (REED) program. The REED program was designed to provide a market-based approach to scaling up clean cookstoves in Bangladesh. This program has shown signs of market transformation from this program are underway with promising market-based approaches successfully tested.

For this project, the Government will borrow a further $20 million from IDA and request $20 million as a matching grant from the GCF which will in turn leverage investments of US$17 million by end users of cookstoves. The Performance Based Grant (PBG) approach is being used for this project because it provides a low risk, high value proposition to the Government concomitantly and only support verified use by consumers of higher quality, higher efficiency and lower emissions clean cooking stoves. However, given that the PBG approach is not yet well established in Bangladesh, this Performance Based Grant approach represents a lowest level of concessionality to ensure rapid market take-up.

The project is “low-risk, high-value” opportunity for GCF to invest and achieve impacts in the sector (i) because it builds on existing work by the Government and other stakeholders; (ii) because it uses a strong performance-based financing mechanism (IDCOL), so the implementation risk is low; if the proposed project will use the results-based financing approach, which has been successfully tested in Bangladesh for the REED program, and the results are verified, the performance risk is low; (iii) GCF will leverage $20 million IDA and more than $40 million private sector investment to support the project. The project will also provide a strong market entry point by delivering additional 6 million improved cookstoves which may help to achieve the market tipping point (we know that for new technologies, when the market penetration reaches to 30-40%, the market force will accelerate and become self-sustaining). The project is expected to have significant positive impacts in Bangladesh for the following reasons:

(i) Enhancing the environment for consumer investment in cleaner cooking. The GCF contribution to building capacity in the supply chain, and increase awareness, will overcome systemic barriers to clean cooking markets within Bangladesh, thereby enhancing the environment for more investment by private sector actors.

(ii) Enhancing the environment of investment for entrepreneurs in cleaner cooking supply chains. Through the PBG approach, the project will provide a strong market entry point supported by the GCF grant, the environment for investment by entrepreneurs (participating organizations) in the supply chain is enhanced.

(iii) Fill the financing gap: The financing required to ensure market take-off and transition to a fully commercialized market is significant. The proposed project will fill this gap through a results-based balance sheet funding from the GCF that the market will develop at a significantly slower rate. Describe how the project programme strengthens the national/ local regulatory or legal frameworks to systematically drive emission reductions in the ICS sector, promote development of additional environmental policies, and/or improve climate-responsive planning and development.

Quality assurance and labeling of products is generally key to enabling consumers to understand the benefits offered by higher quality stoves. This has led to a significant increase in demand for improved products. Progress has been made in recent years to develop international standards for stoves. Over the past the years, the Global Alliance for Clean Cookstoves (GACC) has worked with stakeholders including governments, NGOs, and the private sector to help stove sector experts to develop a set of consensus-based standards. Interim guidelines were published through the International Workshop Agreement (IWA) which standardizes measurements and indicators of performance standards for cookstoves. The GACC also developed a framework for measuring and reporting on household air pollution emissions. For each dimension, stoves are ranked from Tier 0 to Tier 4 (Tier 4 being best) based upon laboratory test results.

Grants provided under the Project are based on the performance of cookstove models on these tiers. This will lead to the assimilation of these standards by the Bangladeshis cookstove sector. Thus the country will serve as a model internationally in developing an ICS sector based on these rigorously developed standards.

### Social-co-benefits:
Increased resilience through reduction in deaths and rates of non-communicable diseases, and improved health, especially of women and children

Closely linked with environmental co-benefits, social-co-benefits will be seen primarily in health aspects. The health impact of indoor air pollution is particularly severe for women and children, who are the most exposed groups to HAP. The WHO has estimated that 48,000 women and children die each year in Bangladesh as a direct result of exposure to household air pollution from cooking with solid fuels. These deaths occur due to asthma, cardiovascular disease, eye problems, and lung cancer. 70% of the victims of HAP are children under five.

The best immediate way of addressing this urgent problem is the rapid and widespread introduction of ICS that are designed to draw off the smoke and toxins, thus creating a safe indoor environment, which will primarily benefit women and children. The use of ICS also has other co-benefits, improving the hygienic conditions of the kitchen, and helps maintain proper nutrient values of the cooked food.

### Economic-co-benefits:
Increased resilience through additional time gained for education and income-generating activities, and increased savings from reduced fuel costs

The Project will use social mobilization approaches of the NGOs and marketing techniques of the private sector. By leveraging the capacities of NGOs and the private sector, The Project will contribute to strengthening the on-going development efforts in the country.

In terms of household economy, the use of solid fuels inflicts high economic costs on families who can pay as much as one-third or more of their income for fuel. In addition, the cost of collecting firewood is also very high. Once purchased rather than collected, the initial higher cost of a more efficient cookstove can be often recovered through fuel savings within a few months, and savings after that point allow for expenditures on a range of livelihood-enhancing activities such as education, health care, and income-generating activities.

In cases where fuel is collected, the reduction of time spent collecting fuel may leave more time to work in the fields, start a small business, or engage in other pursuits that can bring much needed money into the household.


### Gender-sensitive development co-benefits:
Income opportunities for women throughout the value chain

Women play a crucial role in the widespread adoption and use of ICS solutions because of their central responsibility for managing household energy. Women are often the primary decision-makers regarding fuel choice, and are victims but a critical component of the sector's ability to scale. Access to clean cooking options especially benefits women and children who spend most time under which meals are prepared, reducing respiratory diseases, reducing time spent on cooking, and saving money.

A woman can spend about four hours per day cooking with traditional methods. By using ICS, the reduction could be around 2-3 hours per day. This means that a woman has more time to engage in other activities such as spending more time with their children, tend to other responsibilities, enhance existing economic opportunities, pursue income-generating activities and education, and participate in community activities.

The time saved from using ICS is also used for an increased involvement of a woman in social activities, for example to attend community meetings. This leads to a greater community coherence and satisfaction level.


In addition, the use of ICS by young girls is associated with improved health outcomes during fuel collection and cooking activities may improve their educational performance and enable them to benefit from the nutritious midday meal that some schools provide. A survey shows that there is a higher school attendance percentage of children in households using ICS compared to those who are still using traditional stoves.

### E.4.1. Vulnerability of country and beneficiary groups (Adaptation only)

Describe the scale and intensity of vulnerability of the country and beneficiary groups, and elaborate how the project will address this issue (e.g., the level of exposure to climate risks for beneficiary country and groups, overall income level, etc.).

#### BANGLADESH

**Population using solid fuels for cooking - 89%**

Number of people affected by Household Air Pollution (HAP) - 138,000,000

96% of all Bangladeshi stoves employ traditional biomass fuels for cooking, and most use inefficient and poorly ventilated clay stoves that produce smoke, carbon monoxide, and carcinogens. The particulate pollution levels may be 30-35 times higher than accepted guidelines. The women who cook over these stoves, and their small children are exposed to the highest levels of air pollution. According to a study by the Bangladesh Institute of Public Health Engineering (BIPHE), 126,000 women die each year in Bangladesh as a direct result of exposure to household air pollution, while millions more suffer from respiratory diseases. The problem is particularly acute for children under five years old; approximately 70% of the victims of household air pollution are children under five. Not only does the inefficient burning of biomass in private households pose such a serious health threat to Bangladeshi women and children; biomass is also becoming increasingly scarce, as more and more people switch from using it for cooking to using it for other purposes.

The best immediate way of addressing this urgent problem is the rapid and widespread introduction of clean cookstoves: stoves that burn biomass much more efficiently—and even more importantly—are designed to draw off the smoke and fumes produced by burning biomass. The use of such stoves would significantly reduce exposure to air pollution, thereby reducing the incidence of respiratory diseases.

Gender related vulnerability: Lack of access to cooking fuel forces women and children to spend many hours gathering fuel—up to 5 hours per day—or spend significant household income purchasing fuel. Women provide 91% of households' total efforts in collecting fuel and water, and globally women have an average working day of 11-14 hours compared with men's 7-8 hours.

The widespread uptake of ICS has the potential to increase resilience of the most vulnerable populations in Bangladesh by increasing their adaptive capacity and reducing vulnerability at the local scale. As has been noted, fuel wood is harvested in unsustainable ways, with its consumption contributing to the degradation and loss of forest and associated ecosystem services. The use of ICS can help reduce this pressure on forests.

* Increased access to clean cooking energy will provide women with more time for education and income-generating activities
* Improved health outcomes through reduced exposure to smoke and rates of non-communicable diseases, and improved health, especially of women and children.
* Increased resilience through the preservation of forests and associated ecosystem services and a potential reduction in degradation and deforestation by using less fuelwood.

The expected total number of direct and indirect beneficiaries is expected to be 17,400,000.

## Technological solutions

### Features of an ICS:

- An ICS is an improved version of the traditional stove having higher fuel efficiency compared with the traditional ones.
- It has a grate in the middle of its combustion chamber and fuel burns on it.
- There is entry of primary air at an ICS below the grate which helps burning of charcoal formed during burning of fuel wood.
- The three raised ends of a chimneyless ICS are much smaller than those of the traditional stoves.
- In case of multi-mouth ICS with chimneys, cooking in the first mouth is done by direct flame produced from fuel, while cooking in the other mouths are done by hot gas coming out from the first mouth and the spent flue gases and air enter into the other mouths.

Since the innovation of ICS in Bangladesh, it is generally made of mud. However, ICS made of mud has two main problems:

After signing of the Participation Agreement, the POs are required to conduct a baseline survey of minimum 3,000 households in each of the clusters allocated to them using a questionnaire set by GOCL. The POs are required to enter the survey findings in a designated software. After submission of the baseline survey by the POs, IDCOL conducts physical verification of the baseline survey data collected from each cluster. IDCOL rejects a baseline survey if more than 15% discrepancy is found during survey verification.

The stoves under the programs are produced by the POs or either their own production centers or through entrepreneurs appointed by them. The POs are required to ensure that the stoves are manufactured in accordance with the production guideline, adequate curing and drying etc. If the production centers are found to be non-conforming to the production standards set by IDCOL, the ICs produced in that production center are marked as discarded.

POCs conduct promotional activities including awareness creation and training of end users. The promotional activities include: courtyard meeting, school session, mini announcement, CBO Ward Committee/ Union Committee meeting, exhibition, folk song session, film show etc. IDCOL has produced several promotional videos on ICs which have been promoted through various media. POs are required to submit the promotional activity data in a web-based software and submit payment requests to IDCOL by the 10th day of each month.

During installation of ICs POs are required to maintain prescribed dimensions of ICs, maintain serial numbers, follow IC installation guidelines and ensure proper usage of ICs. After installing ICs, POs provide an agreement paper and a one year warranty certificate to the end users. The POs also submit the installed IC data in a web-based software and one copy of customer agreement paper to IDCOL regional offices. Then the POs enter the IC installation data using a web-based software and submit payment requests to IDCOL by the 10th day of each month.

All required payment requests from the POs are processed within 30 days. IDCOL rejects about 10% of ICs submitted under the disbursement request for that cluster. In case of 20% or more discrepancy in a particular cluster, IDCOL excludes all ICs submitted under the disbursement request for that cluster.

IDCOL also conducts regular inspections to ensure the grant amount from current disbursement request, if necessary. Currently, IDCOL is conducting its inspection activities through 42 inspectors in 14 IDCOL regional offices which are located in Rangpur, Bogra, Dhaka and Khulna districts.

IDCOL has so far inspected about 19% of the total ICs for which disbursements were done. It has a plan to increase the inspection coverage significantly within a short period.

For the registration of complaints from the customers and to convey these to the POs, IDCOL has a dedicated call center under the program.
