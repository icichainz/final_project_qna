# A2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

## Background

South Africa is a contributor to global climate change, with greenhouse gas ("GHG") emissions resulting mainly from energy production and consumption. In 2012, South Africa's GHG emissions were 548 MtCO₂e, of which coal-fired electricity generation accounted for 62% (72% in 2012) in South Africa (the 2000-2015 GHG inventory report is currently in development). The country is heavily dependent on coal as a primary energy source and the largest contributor to its GHG emissions. This dependence on coal continues to strain the country's efforts to reduce GHG emissions. In 2012, the total GHG emissions from South Africa were 548 MtCO₂e, of which 346 MtCO₂e (63%) came from energy production and consumption. The largest contributor was coal-fired electricity generation (72% in 2012), followed by industrial processes (19%), waste management (10%), and agriculture (9%). South Africa's GHG emissions are also influenced by the country's economic growth, which is driven largely by the energy industry. As a signatory to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change ("UNFCCC"), South Africa has committed itself to reducing its GHG emissions in line with the global climate change agenda and to contributing to a changing climate. In the National Determined Contributions ("NDCs") submitted in 2015, South Africa has committed to a peak, plateau, and decline in GHG emissions trajectory in the range between 389 and 614 MtCO₂e for the period 2025 to 2030. The country's NDC also includes an indicative target of reducing its coal-fired electricity generation by 20% from 2010-2030 IPCC emission limits on the electricity generation plan. South Africa's power sector has to comply with CO₂ emissions targets in line with the NDCs and the national energy policy, which aims for a peak in GHG emissions between 2025 and 2030.

In line with the national commitment to transition to a low carbon economy, the Department of Energy ("DoE") designed the sovereign-backed REPPPP. Since its inception in 2011, the REPPPP has procured renewable energy capacity of up to 4,750 MW (up to 6,300 MW by 2030) from independent power producers ("IPPs"). The REPPPP has procured small scale 10-MW IPPs. Up to 2015, South Africa had the fastest growing green economy in the world and the program was a major contributor to this growth. However, the REPPPP has not been able to procure any projects since the end of 2015 due to liquidity issues at the national unit (Eskom). The PPAs for the 27 large REPPPP projects have been signed but are yet to be implemented because of delays and were on hold in 2015. In April 2016, South Africa launched a new Integrated Resource Plan ("IRP") which is expected to provide clarity.

## Draft Integrated Resource Plan (IRP) 2030

Recognising the initiation of relying only on government-backed renewable energy programmes, the Minister of Energy has published an IRP for South Africa. The IRP sets out a long-term strategy for South Africa's energy mix towards renewables energy, where it is anticipated that PV will represent 10.5% (7,958 MW) of energy to be generated by 2030 and wind power will contribute 4.6% (3,700 MW). The IRP also sets out a target for 2018 for embedded generation capacity (generation for own use allocation) up to 2030 (5,600 MWh allocated up to 2030).

Embedded generation is the production of electricity from small scale power stations, located in close proximity to the place of consumption. The IRP sets out a target for embedded generation capacity at a district level and recognises that its outside of the formal government procurement programmes and usually defined as projects that are planned for own use.

## Rationale for the Programme

The country has a number of private sector backed fully-developed renewable energy projects, targeted at generation for own use. These projects were developed for REPPPP but were not successfully awarded preferred bidders. Out of a total of bankable projects, only 24% were successful in being awarded under the REPPPP. There remains ca 11.2 GW of development potential from these projects and this is an opportunity for South Africa as it is one of few countries that can potentially be supported through the proposed Embedded Generation Investment Programme ("EGIP").

* Public sector entities (acting as off-takers)
* Local government entities (acting as off-takers) and private sector entities (acting as IPPs)

This financing mechanism is intended to crowd-in funding from commercial lenders and to assist South Africa to make further inroads into the international capital markets.

### Justification of GCF concessionality
Purchase of electricity on the open market at a competitive price provides consumers with a certain predictable price trajectory as compared to Eskom and that tariff will be from greener sources. For municipalities, this means reduced costs for energy, which can be used in the poorer communities, thus unlocking capital for further socio-economic spending and infrastructure development.

Sourcing power from independent IPPs will be attractive for municipalities for the following reasons:
- Certainty of cost and supply with long term price predictability.
- Generation from renewable sources compared to Eskom's coal power in times of power cuts by Eskom;
- Threshing of cheaper renewables with Eskom power allows them to serve the poor market at a lower cost thus reducing the level of electricity subsidies allocated for the communities. The municipality will be able to use this money on other basic needs.

Free Basic Electricity (FBE) was launched by the Government in 2023, with the aim to support indigent households in meeting their basic energy needs. According to the Free Basic Electricity policy an allocation of 50kWh per month is provided free of charge for each household registered under the FBE programme. This amount of electricity will be enough to provide basic water heating using a kettle, basic ironing and power for a small television set and other appliances.

Municipalities are required to manage through the Municipal Financial Management Act (MFMA), 2003. The MFMA aims to modernise budget and financial management practices in municipalities in order to increase the capacity of municipalities to deliver services efficiently and effectively. The MFMA requires a municipal council to adopt three-year capital and operating budgets that take into account, and are linked to, the municipality's current and future development priorities and other finance-related matters. The MFMA also provides for the establishment of an Infrastructure Fund (IF) which gives the flexibility for raising financing outlay that plan. Infrastructural projects such as renewable energy that are sourced from nature requires separate National Treasury approvals and are usually handled through PFPO policies.

Sponsor IRR and end-user tariffs: The reasonability of the equity IRR will be determined considering the affordability and acceptability of the tariff. These will be compared to the REIPPPP programmes as well as Eskom bulk buyers' tariffs.

### B.3. Financial Markets Overview (if applicable)

#### Overview of South African Financial Market
South Africa has a well-developed financial market in place, closely life insurance and pension markets. Some institutional investors have bought into projects post completion, as a result of not showing much appetite for construction sites. The South African banking sector is dominated by the Big Four banks (Standard Bank, Absa, Nedbank and FNB) which have so far comfortably surprised to the surprise of some international investors. Nevertheless, the implementation of Basel III will see a reduction in the number of domestic banks, with an increase in capital requirements financing that seeing an increase in capital markets financing for future project financed projects.

Most of the South African banking assets are domestic, although banks have ventured into foreign assets and an expanding presence in the retail market. The largest banks are Standard Bank (SA), Absa (SA), FNB (SA), Nedbank (SA) and FirstRand Bank (SA). A mid-sized investment bank (Investec), and two smaller banks (Africa Bank and Capitec) have ventured into unsecured lending lines recently, with the latter having a strong focus on SMEs. A Technical Working Group (TWG) of IFIs has agreed to use a common set of emission factors for GHG accounting of electricity production from fossil fuel power generation projects that are close to harmonize GHG accounting through the application of common emission factors to RE GHG calculations.

The main principles and assumptions for baseline emissions factor include:

* **For coal-fired power plants:** Baseline emissions would otherwise be generated wholly or partly from more carbon-intensive sources.

The BEF dataset is updated annually by the IFI GHG Accounting TWG in consultation with the TWG. Where an IFI has conducted its own country or project-related baseline study, the results of this study are submitted to the TWG for consideration of inclusion in the common dataset during future annual updates.

The available data sources for the common dataset used by Technical Working Group (TWG) of IFIs comprises, where available, of UNFCCC standardized baselines (SBEs), which are based on the COM Grid Tool or other approaches approved by the COM and/or recognized by the relevant national authorities or international organizations and recognized by the relevant national authorities. The common dataset containing BEFs is constructed using a Combined Margin (CM) for the grid that is completed in the COM Grid Tool. The CM represents the average of all the individual BEFs, weighted according to their probability in the existing dispatch hierarchy that will most likely be displaced by the project. ## Assessment of the independent Technical Advisory Panel

### Impact potential: Scale/Medium/High

#### 1.1 Impact potential

South Africa is the second largest economy in sub-Saharan Africa with a population of approximately 56.3 million people (2017). In 2016, its gross domestic product per capita gross domestic product were estimated to be USD 296 billion and USD 5,299 respectively. The Government of South Africa has committed itself to providing universal access to electricity for the large part of population by public services such as education, health, water, housing and electrification. In 2016, 87% of households in South Africa had access to electricity (South Africa 2016). However, a drop in global commodity prices and domestic constraints, in particular electricity shortages, drought, difficult labour relations and policy uncertainty have led to the decline in the country’s energy sector.

Eskom is an electricity public utility in South Africa and the largest producer of electricity. It has a total installed generating capacity, growing over 90 per cent of the electricity consumed in the country. Eskom’s nominal generation capacity of 45,600 MW includes 21,800 MW coal-fired power (47.8 per cent), 3,413 MW diesel fired (7.6 per cent), 1,660 MW nuclear power (3.4 per cent) and 565 MW gas-fired power (1.2 per cent). The country’s total installed generating capacity in 2016 was over 90 per cent of the country's total energy output at 221,936 GWh. The country has one of the lowest shares of RE among the Group of Twenty countries.

As a part of its commitment to reduce carbon emissions in 2015, the Government of South Africa commits to peak, plateau and decline greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in the range between 483-679 MtCO2e by 2030. The country’s total GHG emissions in 2012 were estimated at 570 MtCO2e. In 2012, South Africa’s energy sector accounted for 62.5 per cent of the country's total GHG emissions.

The energy sector dominated by fossil fuel power generation is the single largest contributor to the country’s total GHG emissions.

Having identified the need to diversify its energy sector, the Government of South Africa launched the Renewable Energy Independent Power Producers Procurement Programme (REIPPPP) in 2011 to accelerate the development of RE generation. REIPPPP was successful in presenting a level playing field for independent power producers (IPPs) and IPPs were able to compete with private sector to date. # Paradigm shift potential: Scale High

The programme aims to establish a market track record of embedded generation RE projects in South Africa by demonstrating their technical and financial bankability. Although private sector RE projects have been developed for one use, no embedded generation RE projects have yet been established on the market. The proposed regulatory framework development mechanism offered by the programme will assist embedded generation RE subjects to establish viable business models. This will be demonstrated through a series of workshops aimed at establishing the viability of these RE projects and a successful model is established on the market for embedded generation RE, which will contribute significantly towards the success of ReIPPY.

The AE will promote the knowledge and learning on embedded generation RE projects amongst the Government (in terms of legislative framework), developers and other stakeholders. Lessons learned through the programme implementation will be shared with the Department of Energy and the South African National Energy Regulator for the purpose of improving the regulatory framework. The implementation of ReIPPY involved a similar mechanism, which was successful in establishing the RE sector in South Africa.

The Association of Development Finance Institutions, South African Power Pool and industry brown bag information-sharing sessions held at the AE last month at least once a month.

1.3 Legal and Administrative Framework Protecting Women and Protecting Gender Equality

South Africa achieved democracy in 1994. Central to this democracy was a commitment to equality, including gender equality and the empowerment of women. The founding principles of the Constitution of the Republic of South Africa include human rights, equality and freedom for everyone without discrimination. Section 9 of the Constitution (Act 108 of 1996) emphasises the need for equality for all mankind within the country. According to this section of the constitution, there shall be no unfair discrimination against anyone on the basis of race, gender, sexual orientation, disability, etc.

After 1994, the principle of gender equality influenced policy and legislation formulation in economic development programmes such as land reform, access to land, housing, water, health care and public works programmes among others. Furthermore, the South African government developed and implemented key domestic development programmes with an overarching focus on poverty alleviation and social inclusion. These include the Skills Development (GED) Shared Growth Initiative -South Africa (AGSISA), the Reconstruction and Development Programme (RDP), Growth, Employment and Redistribution (GEAR) National Development Plan (NDP). Although these programmes were meant to advance the country, they did not specifically focus on women, resulting therefore in the challenge of unemployment, poverty, lack of access to education or training for women.

Additional programmes such as the Presidential Infrastructure Coordination Commission (PICC) and Operation Phakisa and the Green Economy have been put in place with the sole aim of providing the much-needed jobs and grow the South African economy.

At an international scale, the South African Government ratified the Convention on the Elimination of all forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW). The South African Government recognises the Beijing Declaration and Platform of Action, and of the areas of concern under that declaration. South Africa has prioritised the following:

- Women and poverty
  - Women and education
  - Women and economic empowerment
  - Women in leadership and decision-making,
  - Improving the conditions and situation of the girl child
