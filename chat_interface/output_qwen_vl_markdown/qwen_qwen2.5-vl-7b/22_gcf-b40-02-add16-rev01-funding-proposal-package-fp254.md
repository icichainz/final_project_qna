A.21. Executive Summary (max. 750 words, approximately 1.5 pages)

The global climate change crisis is inextricably linked to water with increased variability in the water cycle, reduced water resource availability, and increasing CO2 emissions due to lack of or inadequate water and sanitation (WaS) infrastructure. The proposed Scaling Resilient Water Infrastructure facility aims to address these challenges by overcoming, including improvements in policies and regulations, adequate project preparation, availability of long-term financing, and other enabling factors.

The proposed Scaling Resilient Water Infrastructure facility ("RWI facility" or "facility") is a programmatic intervention to leverage on the strengths of the World Bank Group (including International Finance Corporation, International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, and Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency) in addressing water sector challenges. The RWI Facility has two components: (1) the PPP Structuring Facility that will fund project preparation, structuring, and transaction costs; and (2) the Concessional Co-Investment Facility that will provide co-investments alongside IFC's own account investments.

Support from GCF for the RWI facility will help bridge to close critical viability gaps, strengthen local capacities, bring more projects into the pipeline, and increase the scale of borrowing. The proposed facility will also have strong demonstration effects, proving working business models and PPP structures which can be replicated in other countries.

The facility will address water scarcity and contribute to climate change adaptation and mitigation benefits in the target countries by developing more resilient Water Infrastructure with advanced technologies and sustainable practices. Additionally, it will help increase access to WaS services for poor and vulnerable populations.

The proposed solutions include: (i) conventional water resources (CWR), such as groundwater and surface water, which are commonly used but will not be sufficient to meet the growing demand in water scarce areas; (ii) adequate wastewater treatment and reuse; (iii) unconventional water resources (UWRs), including rainwater harvesting, stormwater management, and greywater recycling; (iv) water reuse and desalination - Unconventional Water Resources (UWRs), which are expected to continuously grow; and (v) non-revenue water reduction (NRWR) to minimize inefficiencies.

### Non-Revenue Water: (Physical losses reduction - leakages reduction, pipe replacement):

The RWF Facility aims at supporting NRW reduction activities and in particular projects that aim at reducing physical losses of water through the rehabilitation of distribution networks. Reducing leakages in a distribution network allows to free up additional volumes available to satisfy the demand without having to increase the volume of water produced. This is particularly important for countries where the majority of the population does not have access to the water supply system (this helps with universalisation). On top of that, it allows reducing the energy required (this is important in countries where electricity generation is based on fossil fuels and/or hydropower).
In addition, by reducing leakages, the RWF will contribute to improving the resilience of the water sector against water scarcity or increasing contaminated sources, being able to count on better-performing networks and/or newly available volumes that can be used for other purposes (e.g. irrigation, industrial sectors).

The global water sector faces unprecedented challenges due to climate change, rapid urbanization, and population growth. These challenges are particularly acute in developing countries, where existing water infrastructure is often insufficient or poorly maintained.
As a result of these factors, the demand for water continues to increase while supply decreases, increasing the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events, and exacerbating water scarcity in many regions. Some countries facing rising temperatures are accelerating glacial melt and sea-level rise, further threatening water security and infrastructure.

Breaking this cycle requires coordinated action at all levels of government and across sectors.

### G.1 Environmental and social risk management (max. 750 words, approximately 1.5 pages)

The Facility is being considered for Category A. IFC has prepared and duly adopted an EAS Management Framework (ESMF) for the Facility that provides an overview of how IFC will manage E&S risks and impacts associated with its investments in the Facility. The ESMF sets out the project-level due diligence, the identification of E&S risks and impacts, assessment for any potential gaps in assessments, and mitigation measures to be implemented under the ongoing supervision of projects implemented by each project level entity that benefits from the proceeds of the Facility.

The ESMF shall be based on IFC’s Sustainability Framework which consists of (a) the Policy on Environmental and Social Sustainability (PES), (b) the Performance Standards (PSs), (c) the Environmental, Social, and Health Safety Notes, Good Practice Notes and other materials prepared by IFC to assist its clients in relation to its E&S policies; the World Bank Group (WBG) Environmental, Health and Safety (EHS) Guidelines, the CAO Policy, and other relevant documents.

The IFC Performance Standards help IFC investments and advisory clients improve and improve their E&S performance through the application of a risk-based approach. The PSs are divided into three levels: the first is the Performance Standard, followed by specific requirements to help clients achieve these outcomes through means that are appropriate for each client’s circumstances.

Central to these requirements is the application of a mitigation Hierarchy to anticipate and avoid adverse impacts on workers and communities; reduce or eliminate significant E&S risks and impacts; and mitigate residual risks and impacts, compensate/offset for the risks and impacts, as appropriate. Within the scope of an agreed advisory activity, all advice and training will be consistent with the Performance Standards.

The IFC Gender Action Plan (GAP) is a gender action plan that will be applied for assessing project specific E&S risks and impacts and related mitigation measures including considering gender differences around risks, impacts, and opportunities. The assessment of gender social risks and impacts will be identified during the due diligence phase and monitored during the life of the investment.

## I. Assessment of the Independent Technical Advisory Panel

### 1.1 Summary of the proposal

The proposal seeks to establish the GCF-IFC Scaling Resilient Water Infrastructure Facility (RWI Facility) across Africa, Asia, Europe, and Latin America. It is designed to address water infrastructure challenges such as inadequate access to clean water and sanitation, water scarcity, poor sanitation, and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions arising from inadequate water and sewerage services. This facility aims to enhance the resilience of water infrastructure, contribute to climate adaptation and mitigation, and support vulnerable communities.

### Key objectives:
- Develop resilient water infrastructure;
- Improve access to clean water and sanitation;
- Reduce GHG emissions by improving water management practices;
- Promote water reuse, desalination, and the reduction of non-renewable water (NRW) or water that is pumped and then lost or unaccounted for; and
- Strengthen public-private partnerships (PPPs) to finance and manage water projects.

### Project details:
- Total target financing: USD 1.26 billion;
- GCF funding requested: USD 258 million (USD 250 million loan), USD 8 million (grant); and
- Project size: Large, with an implementation period of 10 years and a lifespan of 35 years.

### Expected impact:
- Mitigation: Reduction of approximately 3.01 million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO₂ eq) over 25 years;
- Adaptation: Direct benefit to approximately 14.9 million people through improved water access and sanitation.

## Overall remarks from the independent Technical Advisory Panel

### The GCF-IFC Scalable Resilient Water Infrastructure Facility (RWI Facility) is a transformative facility designed to address water infrastructure across many water-scarce regions in Africa, Asia, Europe and Latin America. By targeting water-scarce regions, the facility aims to increase the availability of clean water, enhance sanitation services, and improve water management practices through blended finance support. The project holds significant promise for attracting private sector finance in this underinvested area, but it is important to note that there may be additional water extraction before addressing efficiency issues and may in such cases induce or inadvertently delay interventions to reduce high levels of leakage and NRW.


---

### Increased strain of water extraction globally and in many of the target countries

- Extraction of additional water for systems with high levels of NWR issues can place unnecessary strain on already stressed water sources.
- Without reducing water losses, further extraction risks depleting natural water reserves faster than they are replenished, exacerbating the water crisis.

#### Missed opportunities for efficiency gains

- Addressing NWR is one of the most cost-effective ways to improve water-supply efficiency. By focusing on water-saving measures, providers can reduce demand and the need for further extraction.
- However, if projects under the Water Facility do not prioritize fixing NWR issues before extracting additional water, they risk missing an opportunity to maximize resource use.

#### Sustainability and long-term impact

- From a sustainability perspective, focusing on water extraction without addressing NWR issues first may lead to a short-term solution that does not address underlying inefficiencies. This can result in increased maintenance costs, deterioration of the water infrastructure, leading to higher long-term operational costs and reduced sustainability.
- While the WBI facility does include NWR reduction as a focus area, there is no indication that projects will be required to address NWR issues before extracting additional water. To ensure improved climate resilience outcomes in the long term, projects should be structured to first optimize existing systems through NWR reduction.

### Policy and regulatory gaps

- The funding proposal does not appear to outline strong policy frameworks or regulatory mechanisms for addressing NWR issues.
- If NWR issues are addressed before additional water is extracted. This raises concerns about whether water extraction will be managed in a sustainable manner, as the World Bank Group's current governance arrangements may be weak.

#### IFC and leading institutions

- IFC is a leading institution in carrying out holistic water assessments for the target countries.
- It seems to lack the tools directly affecting government utility behavior. The facility would have been stronger if it had included other executing entities within the World Bank or other international organizations with more direct influence on utilities.
- Still, even within the current design of the WBI Facility, IFC does have a unique institutional opportunity to work with other members of the World Bank Group in holding a matter of interest for all parties involved. During the Q&A with the ITAP, IFC shared that the World Bank Group will target the water sector as one where all the different arms of the World Bank Group (policy, public lending, advisory)

### I. INTRODUCTION

Gender gaps in access to water and sanitation have a high economic cost, are unequal, and they disproportionately affect women and children. In lower-middle-income countries, the economic costs from poor sanitation affecting health, productivity, and the environment are estimated to be $260 billion annually or 1.5 percent of GDP. The burden on women for large developing countries is even greater: 84% of the world's urban population (3.3 billion people) have no access to improved sanitation facilities[1] and 51 percent do not have clean, readily available drinking water.[2] Lack of basic sanitation services has a significant impact on health, education, and economic productivity. For example, in East Asia and the Pacific,[3] Access to drinking water is similarly limited: In Sub-Saharan Africa, 51 percent of people are without access to drinking water as are 25 percent in South Asia and 16 percent in East Asia and the Pacific.

The higher cost of informal water sources in many urban areas not only exacerbates poverty and gender inequality by forcing households to devote a large portion of their income to pay for these alternatives, but also captures revenue that could have been made by a water utility, constraining its customer base and growth potential. In some cases, women are forced to spend up to 15 times more on water from unreliable, private sources,[4] exacerbating inequality for the urban poor, of which women are disproportionately affected.[5] Additionally, in many countries, women often pay the water bill despite having lower incomes than their husbands, perpetuating household inequality. Additionally, in female-headed households in poor urban areas, the women supporting the households are often responsible for collecting and transporting water, making it difficult to clean water and sanitation can jeopardize income generation, financial security, and thus, food security.

Vulnerable populations in fragile and conflict-affected situations, refugees, and forcibly displaced persons may be especially at risk of reduced access to water and sanitation, and women may face particularly severe consequences. Women are often the primary caregivers for children and the elderly affected by population growth, migration to cities, changes in consumption patterns, and climate change. ### B. Opportunity area 2: Increasing representation of women in water and sanitation workforces, management, and supply chains improves service outcomes among client actors

The water and sanitation sector is an overwhelmingly male-dominated industry, yet inclusion of women in workforce and management can improve service outcomes. A World Bank study of 64 water and sanitation service providers in 28 countries around the world found that an average of only 18 percent (Everett et al., 2019) of employees were female. In some cases, the gender gap was even more pronounced: in the utilities were female, 32 percent of the utilities studied had no female engineers and 12 percent had no female managers. Legal barriers to women's employment still exist to varying degrees in most countries, with many countries having laws that prevent women from working in certain jobs, including women's employment in the water sector. Although women make up a small percentage of the water and sanitation workforce, one study found that their inclusion makes it twice as seven times as effective at increasing service quality. In addition to improving service provision, greater gender-diverse workforce can also help service providers better understand and respond to the concerns and needs of female clients—and lead to more efficient and effective services and improved customer satisfaction.

- Data collection using multiple sources such as internal information of the company on workforce, management, suppliers, communities and GBVIN; public sources – country context, sector status in the country, etc.; and other relevant data sources.
- Interviews with the heads of various departments within the company—HR, CSR, Procurement, Communication, Diversity, equity and inclusion (if any).
- Separate interviews with women and men in leadership roles, staff and management if the focus is on workforce; with suppliers both women and men-owned/led business if the focus is on supply chain; with community women and men if the focus is on communities and with customers women and men if the focus is on customers.
- Analysis of data and findings and report back to the top management of the company with recommendations for improvement in key areas.

The gender assessment is led by IFC and conducted with client input and collaboration. As part of the gender assessment, the company will be provided with the gender action plan based on the recommendations prioritized by the company. Trainings and capacity building for the client on how to carry a gender assessment may be included as part of the assessment process. In addition, if requested, IFC will provide support to the implementation of the gender action plan through gender advocacy. IFC has developed gender-smart solutions to reduce gaps within these different stakeholder groups as briefly described below.

Interventions will be tailored to the technical solution pillars, the country context and the capacity of the client to implement them. The IFC’s Gender Smart Solutions (GSS) Toolkit for Water and Sanitation sector companies was developed for water and sanitation sector companies to use to implement such solutions through the IFC’s interactive digital Gender & Infrastructure toolkit Engineering Inclusivity: Infrastructure for Everyone. The toolkit provides a set of practical tools that infrastructure companies can take to make their businesses more gender-inclusive and achieve measurable results.

Corporate leadership gaps: IFC, through its corporate governance team, delivers women on board trainings, undertakes company-specific research on increasing female senior talent in pipelines and provides management with concrete actions on how to develop a company-wide diversity and inclusion compact.

Risks of companies' lack of engagement:

IFC has longstanding experience working with the private sector to embed gender-smart solutions and thereby driving company benefits and closing economic gender gaps. IFC has built a wealth of evidence around gender equality at firm-level engagement (www.commdew.org), which helps to convince companies to address gender gaps.

IFC is confident that based on its deep gender knowledge in this sector, the firm-level gender gap assessments and the framing of gender equality as a core business concern, firms will be engaging with IFC in enhancing women's economic participation. In addition, shareholders and institutional investors are increasingly demanding that companies report on their progress towards gender equality goals for their companies, and they expect increased attention to gender equality from the company's management.

We expect that more companies will be willing to engage with IFC in order to tackle gender gaps in the water and sanitation sector. IFC will also be embedding sex-disaggregated mandatory indicators in its loan agreement with all clients, namely % of women on boards, women in senior management positions, and women in technical roles. These indicators will be embedded into project-specific indicators depending on the type of gender intervention, as for example: indicators related to women's access to water and sanitation services, or indicators related to companies that develop a gender strategy or set up specific programs (mentoring for example) or include more women owned or led businesses in their supply chain. This other type of indicators will be monitored through Annual Monitoring reports or through delivery services where the data may be.
