This makes it difficult for BOM to provide concessional financing, as this would require a large external balance (i.e. balance of payments). BOM is fully committed to flexible exchange rate regime with occasional foreign exchange (FX) intervention if necessary. Intervention is limited by smoothing accessible market volatility and is still conducted by FX auction mechanism twice a week. There is no change in FX policy, auctioning or intervention mechanisms.

The government has been able to access concessional financing from the World Bank and other multilateral agencies for several years now. ## B.3. Financial Markets Overview (if applicable)

In December of 2016, Mongolia’s very first utility scale solar power plant became operational after beginning construction in July 2016. The Darhan solar power plant, which also has a 10MW capacity, was completed with the cooperation of domestic Solar Power International with two Japanese partners.

The reason that current Mongolian renewable energy projects are unable to scale up to more than 50MW is mainly due to financial constraints. In Mongolia, financing for large-scale renewable energy projects typically requires long term financing with high quality materials and expertise, which means that the projects are more expensive in terms of interest rates (typically in the 18.00% to 25% interest rate range for domestic currency financing, and 8.00% to 12.00% in USD financing) with relatively short terms (maximum of 5 to 8 years), and after mitigating these costs, there is not enough debt and equity investments are not adequately available, especially for large-scale projects like these.

All this adds up to the fact that it is difficult to implement large scale solar power plants in Mongolia at the moment. However, with the project being a pilot project for renewable energy transition in Mongolia, this is a brand new field of business in Mongolia. However, through the GCF’s concessional funding and support in addition to the SPP project's international and domestic co-financing possibilities, it is becoming feasible to construct the plant.

# C.1 Strategic Context

## Global
Global political trends have demonstrated that the initiative of governments on climate change and investment in renewable energy infrastructure is unreliable and subject to the changing views of new administrations.

## National
In order to achieve the Mongolian government's stated goal of a 14% reduction in total national GHG emissions, the INDC outlined a number of policies across the energy, industrial, agricultural, and waste sectors. The proposed projects fall under the following INDC goals: (a) increase renewable energy share in electricity generation from 20% to 30% by 2030; (b) State Policy on the Energy Sector, approved by parliament and published in 2015, determined short- and mid-term development of renewable energy projects with a target for the country's total installed power-generation capacity to be 20% in 2023 and 30% in 2030. According to IRENA (International Renewable Energy Agency), Mongolia has set itself an ambitious goal of achieving 100% development and deployment of renewables. The Renewable Energy Law, passed in 2007, provided a legislative framework for the development of renewable energy projects. In order to address unforeseen circumstances, parliament amended the law in June 2015. The improved legislation should strengthen public-private partnerships and create a market-oriented framework for the energy sector.

The Government of Mongolia (GOM) has been working to strengthen renewable energy (RE) regulations to create an enabling environment for RE projects. In addition, the GOM is working with international partners to facilitate policy evolution to mobilize investment in RE projects across Mongolia. For a full summary of key renewable energy regulations and initiatives by date, please see Annex 1.

## Economic
Following the annual economic growth rate of 17% in 2011 and 12.3% in 2012, driven by commodity super-cycle, FDI influx and expansionary policies, Mongolian economy has been hard hit by both terms of trade and FDI shocks since 2013 as well as by overly expansionary fiscal policies for the last two years. Consequently, the country is now facing a challenging economic environment characterized by low but stable inflation, rebalancing current account, and ensure financial sector stability. However, collapsed but unrecovered yet FDI, continuously low external demand, depressed resource prices, electricity and water shortages, and high interest rates have resulted in negative growth of 11% between 2013 to 1.0% in 2016. Current economic difficulty faced by Mongolia is definitely not a solvency issue, affirmed by the investment grade rating.

Apparantly low GDP growth environment in Mongolia is expected to continue in a short run due to economic stabilization. However, this is a completely new environment and a new opportunity, which requires new economic equilibrium after commodity boom bust cycles and resource-driven overexuberances over the past 10 years.

## Electric Power Network
The electric power network of Mongolia comprises five main energy systems shown below in Figure C.5. The largest system is the Central Energy System (CES) which covers most parts of Mongolia (MENDEES) in the South Gobi. The smaller energy systems are the Western Energy System (WES), the neighboring Altai-Ulaanbaatar Energy System (AUES), and the Eastern Energy System (EES). These systems are interconnected with Russia and are dependent on imported energy. There are small cross-border interconnections between the CES and the WES, AUES, and EES.

The CES is supplied by five Combined Heat and Power (CHP) plants covering the main cities of UB, Darkhan, Erdent and 13 provinces. The nominal electric capacity in the CES is 1072.5 MW; however, the power plants are aged and unreliable and the available capacity is only 918 MW.

The national grid imports electricity from Russia and China (19.9% of all electricity supplied in 2016). Electricity is imported from Russia to the western energy system region and areas in the southern, western and eastern region. However, these areas are not connected to the Southern Energy System (SES) which covers most parts of Mongolia. Therefore, the amount of electricity that enters their electricity imports from China are excluded from the calculation of the national grid emission factor.

## List of Economic and Financial Barriers

- **Financing**: high interest rates for loans at financial institutions; lack of low-interest rate RE/EE loan programs; lack of knowledge, experience, and expertise in providing, assessing and evaluating RE/EE projects.
- **Cost**: Cost of upfront capital, transaction (including feasibility study, due diligence) and resource acquisition costs are high compared to other technologies.
- **Financial incentives**: Lack of financial incentives to RE solutions and favorable treatment of conventional energy sources. The total amount of the Renewable Energy Fund in Mongolia is 14.7 billion MNT which is 11 million USD). Negative externalities of CHPs have been ignored and not considered as a cost in the feasibility study.
- **Electricity tariff**: Electricity tariff is set below the marginal cost. According to SREP (2015) report, international comparison clearly indicates that electricity tariffs in Mongolia are substantially lower than in other countries with similar levels of development. The average electricity price per kWh quoted in the Yearly Statistical Book of ERC was 6.6 c/kWh in 2015 as compared to 9.4 c/kWh in Thailand and 8.7 c/kWh in India.
- **Uncertainty**: uncertain electricity tariffs (non-transparent tariff adjustment procedure); uncertain exchange rate; uncertain environment (severe inflation rate and high price fluctuations); uncertain currency exchange rates, etc.

## List of Non-Financial Barriers

- **Market conditions**: Lack of liberalization in the energy sector: market competition is not fully introduced. The power market is dominated with state-owned suppliers; fair competition is not yet in place; electricity tariff is not liberalized; energy sector is controlled and dominated by state-owned suppliers.
- **Curtailment**: Enforcement of curtailment to RE projects can be occurred. RE generation capacity has the lowest marginal cost and should therefore always be displaced behind thermal plant. While there are no legal provisions for curtailment, it is practiced in practice. According to a 2015 Mongolian engineering analysis suggests that more could be done by thermal plant operators so that RE generation can be fully utilized. The existing RE capacity in Mongolia has been installed on the premise that the existing CHP plants are fully depreciated and coal is subsidized; meaning that the existing power system is not designed to accommodate RE.
- **Technical issues**: System constraints and capacity limitation with grid system is a challenge for solar projects.
- **Transparency**: The RE law stipulates, on one hand, that the system is obligated to receive the energy supplied by RE generators, but on the other hand, the system dispatcher has the right to displace RE generation. This creates uncertainty in terms of how much RE generation can be utilized and curtailed energy could be managed by a transparent, universal regulation, but instead it is managed by stipulated rules that are not transparent and have been developed with limited participation and inequitable treatment of RE generators (or at least a perception on the part of investors).

SREP (2015) report has also summarized key barriers and problems of RE development and suggested possible capacity building activities to overcome them.

### Program Implementation Process

Loan assessment and approval process are described in detail below:

### 1. Loan Assessment

As loan program is considered a large loan, it will be assessed by two specialists of XacBank's Corporate Banking Division at corporate headquarters. The Credit analyst will do the due diligence to the project and prepare a report for the credit committee. The credit committee will provide approval to the Credit Department. Loan appraisal will cover all necessary due diligence measures including financial analysis, market analysis, risk assessment, etc., as well as input from investors, shareholders, etc. Credit risk analysis of the Credit department will provide an independent assessment regarding loan risks.

#### 1. Customer behavior CHARACTER: determine business structure, management, employee background, experience, and purpose of loan

#### 2. Business environment CONDITIONS: explore the business, products, equipment, production process, market conditions, competitors, suppliers, competitors, legal environment and other impacting factors and reveal their potential impact on loan repayment.

#### 3. Loan Repayment SOURCES: determine the sources of funds to repay the loan, including primary objectives of the business, plan, and determine the loan efficiency of the business.

#### 4. Capital for risk coverage/capital: based on the financial analyses define the real value of the collateral (real estate, machinery, equipment), secondary income (loan from shareholders) and debts held by related companies

#### 5. Collateral asset COLLATERAL: define the collateral asset type, quality, structure, ownership, etc., as well as any required insurance. These elements shall be regulated by the "Procedure on Collateral Requirements".

# Rationale for GCF Involvement

## Value Added for GCF Involvement

Investments in renewable energy involve higher upfront costs and longer payback periods than both conventional investments and energy efficiency investments. The costs borne by developers over this payback period need to be minimized to maximize internal rate of return. So far, the project developers have been unable to secure financing from commercial banks for their projects from international investors, in the form of equity. However, to complete their project, they still are in need of loans. Market interest rates in Mongolia are prohibitively high and tenors are problematic. The current 10-year government bond rate is 9%, while the 3-month rate is 14%, which leads to extremely adverse deposit savings ratios (about 15-16% for local currency), which means that banks can only offer loans at a maximum of 8%. This compares with rates in China, where interest rates are 5 years, usually less than 3 years). Mongolia's government domestic bond rate is currently at 14%, while the 3-month rate is 20% (as of June 2016). The project developers face a lack of access to adequate funds for not only large scale projects but for the entire country itself. As XacBank is a commercial bank and not a development bank, and despite its efforts to lessen the burden on borrowers, it has been unable to provide loans at terms that are acceptable. XacBank is still not able to offer sufficient low cost, long term financing. Without the GCF's support, XacBank would be unlikely to finance renewable energy projects such as the project included in this proposal would be extremely limited.

The GCF provides access to lending at competitive terms. GCF involvement will allow XacBank to provide beneficial financial terms to sustainable development project implementers who have been interested in financing such projects but discouraged by high interest rates and long payback periods. With the GCF's ability to provide loans with loan support (competitive rates and longer tenures), XacBank will also be able to offer a loan that allows the project to go forward while not over-burdening the developer with terms that would extend beyond their payback period. Without such concessionary financing, the project would not be able to go forward.

In addition to the clear benefits of GCF involvement for XacBank and the project developer, GCF involvement also benefits the end-beneficiaries of this project in the Mongolian power sector. Access to competitive terms will allow XacBank to provide loans at rates that are more affordable than those currently available from other sources. For further information on the benefits of the program's implementation would provide to end-beneficiaries, including financial, economic, and social benefits. As a result of the GCF's support, XacBank is able to finance renewable energy projects such as the project included in this proposal would be extremely limited. Thus the benefits to end beneficiaries for GCF involvement are discussed further in E3.2.1 on the implementation of the project for end-beneficiaries in general, which can be found in E.3.

In addition, as part of the first batch of large scale on-grid energy generation, there are certainly risks affiliated with the project. Local financial institutions are wary of taking on such risks and lack expertise in renewable energy projects. The GCF's involvement will help to mitigate these risks and encourage local FI to increase their involvement in RE financing. Thus, in addition to pure financial needs of the project developers, there is a paradigm-shift value add for GCF-involvement in these projects. This is particularly true given the lack of experience with renewable energy projects by GCF's partner banks. XacBank has had some experience with developing project finance through GCF's financing in Mongolia but to be provided with such terms would be unprecedented and likely result in a very long payback period and/or onerous terms. Currently, the only other renewable energy projects in existence in Mongolia of comparable scope are the Darkhat wind farm (30 MW) and the Darkhan SPP (10MW).

Finally, the GCF has great value added in being able to support XacBank’s ability to develop the necessary capacities for this program through grant funding. This program represents a large increase in capacity building and technical assistance for XacBank, which will include on-board project financing specialists, a dedicated portfolio manager, as well as general capacity building and training of XacBank staff in the context of the GCF’s renewable energy implementation program. With these capabilities, XacBank will be able to mitigate first-mover risks and become an industry leader in renewable energy implementation capabilities, technical capacity that is sorely needed for the development of this sector.

### 2. Exit Strategy

Once the funding is provided, the GCF does not need to be highly involved in program operations. The Fund will only be required to provide an annual statement of financials to the parent plant. XacBank will report on the facility’s disbursement to GCF periodically to describe how funding is being used, including financial statements (balance sheet, income statement), GCF loan portfolio, and other relevant information.

The GCF’s exit strategy is quite simple, as XacBank will be paying the GCF back directly, based upon the terms that the GCF is able to offer. Exit risk is minimal. GCF exit will coincide with XacBank’s repayment of the loan, which will occur when the project has been successfully tenured. If there arises any NPL during the loan tenure period, XacBank will cover the risk; therefore, it presents no risk to the GCF.

### Contribution to creation of enabling environment

The project has great paradigm shift potential as it will serve as proof of concept for the viability of renewables in Mongolia. The implementation of the project will open up the market conditions for the establishment of other similar projects. Aside from these tangible enabling changes, a successfully implemented solar power plant would also provide an example of the technical and environmental viability of such a project to other interested project developers and FIs, thus stimulating the supply chain and creating a virtuous cycle.

In addition, the ability of the project to receive financing will send a message that despite high upfront capital costs, renewable energy projects can be bankable with long repayment terms and access to proper capital.

### Innovation

While solar power is one of the more developed renewable technologies worldwide, solar power infrastructure beyond small residential-use cells is certainly "innovative" in Mongolia. Most commonly, solar power systems are installed on rooftops for domestic use or as part of larger commercial applications. However, there is currently only one newly-operational 10 MW solar plant in Darkhan city. This project would be the second utility-scale SPP in Mongolia.

Aside from the relative innovation of the projects in Mongolia, there are numerous challenges in the Mongolian context that make the implementation of solar technology innovative as well. The first is the extreme temperatures. For example, few solar plants are designed to withstand -40 degree Celsius temperatures commonly experienced during winter months in Mongolia. This project will be the first utility-scale solar plant in Mongolia and will certainly be a Mongolia-specific innovation that will allow for the technology to spread to other regions in the country.

The second challenge is the extreme temperature cycling of the Mongolian climate. The comment from a technical risk mitigation standpoint that the projects included in this program have undergone thermal cycling tests that has shown the projects' ability to operate between -40°C and +85°C temperatures.

#### Selected Risk Factor 3: PPA and FIT risks

| Description | Risk category | Level of impact | Probability of risk occurring |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| FIT risks - risk of not being able to claim the full payments of the feed-in tariff (FIT) over the duration of a power purchasing agreement (PPA). | Financial | Medium (5.1-20% of project value) | Medium |

**Mitigation Measure(s):**

Renewable energy (RE) projects are to be compensated for the electricity produced and sold back to the grid according to the agreed upon FIT and time period (minimum 10 years) in the PPA. The compensation is drawn from a renewable energy fund which is specially designated to reimburse costs associated with RE projects. The compensation is paid out of this fund, and the project owner will be reimbursed full compensation according to the signed PPA during the early stage of operation as it was the first case.

The risk that the FIT payments are not made in full or on time is considered to be low because it would likely be the case that the fund does not have enough funds to be able to compensate all projects fully for the minimum of 10 years. This risk has been mitigated by the fact that currently, there is already a built-in cost of FIT payments in the electricity prices and the project owner will be reimbursed out of the moneys collected through this cost (not additional cost, already baked into the electricity prices) are pooled into the Renewable Energy FIT Fund.

#### Selected Risk Factor 4 - Currency risk

| Description | Risk category | Level of impact | Probability of risk occurring |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Currency risk: FITs set in USD may pose financial pressure for the government. | Financial | Medium (5.1-20% of project value) | Low |

**Mitigation Measure(s):**

The law makes FITs payable in MNT (calculated on the exchange rate of the central bank on the day of the payment) which greatly encourages foreign investors. However, this effectively passes the currency exchange risk onto the government. When the Mongolian currency depreciates, as it has done dramatically over recent years, the government faces a significant financial burden.

However, the currency risk for both the borrower and the Bank are mitigated greatly by the fact that the FIT is pegged to the USD FX rate.
