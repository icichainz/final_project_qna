## Coral reef ecosystems are critical in providing safety, food security and livelihoods to more than 1 billion people worldwide through the economic services, coastal protection and economic activities they generate.

Coral reefs provide a wide range of ecosystem goods and services that benefit millions of people around the world. Many of those directly impacted by these benefits are women. Coral reefs attracted an estimated $70 million annually with an associated expenditure of US $36 billion (prior to COVID-19). Women make up 85% of the workforce in coral reef-dependent communities, and they are disproportionately affected by climate change. In 2014, a global study found that women were more likely than men to be involved in fishing activities on reefs. In 2016, a study estimated that women contribute $3 billion annually to the global economy through their role as small-scale, traditional subsistence fishers; dependent on coral reef fisheries, with an estimated value of US $8.56 billion in 2011. Women also play a key role in protecting and managing coral reefs. In 2017, a study found that women were more likely than men to be involved in the protection of coral reefs. In 2019, a study estimated that women contribute $35 million annually through their role as small-scale, traditional subsistence fishers; dependent on coral reef fisheries, with an estimated value of US $84 billion in 2016. Women also play a key role in protecting and managing coral reefs.

At the same time, coral reefs are among the world’s most threatened ecosystems by climate change impacts. In parallel, multiple local stressors such as overfishing, pollution, coastal development, and destructive fishing practices continue to degrade reef health and function.

Recent modelling exercises on corals show that protecting areas of the ocean where conditions are most stable is key for coral reefs to survive in these areas, or “climate refuges”, reliant in suitable habitat despite regional climate change and are likely to be critical in preventing further reef degradation and considerable loss of biodiversity. Their protection is becoming a priority as part of the global strategy on marine protected areas (MPAs) under the Aichi Biodiversity Targets and the climate change and Biodiversity adaptation program 10. Studies also show that better management of local stressors can help to protect reefs in these refuges, which are critical for the survival of coral reef species and the communities that depend on them for food, protection, and livelihoods. By protecting the identified priority networks of reefs that are most resilient to climate change, some ecosystems will survive the impacts of climate change and may help to repopulate neighbouring reefs.

To enhance the resilience and adaptive capacity of the communities, productive systems and businesses that depend on coral reef resources, it is essential to address the local stressors. The proposed project will provide investment that enables activities that ameliorate or eliminate existing local stress in the most resilient reefs and “climate refuges”. Restoration efforts will also be supported to accelerate the recovery of reefs that have the highest chance of survival.

To drive better management of local stressors and accelerate restoration, investments will focus across the following sectors:

1. Sustainable ocean production to address overfishing, destructive fishing techniques and enhance food security : a. Improved fisheries management (e.g., catch shares, fishing control & surveillance, post catch processing, supply chain traceability)
b. Sustainable aquaculture (e.g., shrimp, fish farming or shrimp culture)
c. Sustainable aquafeed (e.g., shrimp, alternative feed, etc.)
d. Coral reef nurseries and coral reefs markers, assisted evolution tech companies, coral gardening and sexual propagation venues

2. Ecotourism to address unsustainable tourism:
a. Sustainable ecotourism activities (which can include implementation of coral-restoration practices and technologies in the surrounding reefs)
b. Tourism infrastructure (e.g., hotels, cruise)

3. Sustainable fisheries waste and management to address pollution:
a. Plastic waste management (e.g. alternative material, collection and sorting, plastic recycling, AI and machine learning)
b. Sewage and waste water treatment
c. Green transportation (including e.g. port reception facilities to address liquid waste emitted by ships)
d. Clean energy off-grid tech

# IMPACT OF OTHER HUMAN ACTIVITIES ON CORAL REEFS

Climate change is not the only problem reefs have to contend with. Further to global warming and climate change, ambitions for economic development and a higher quality of life have led to unsustainable practices that degrade coral reef ecosystems.

These include overfishing and poor management of reef resources. A major driver of reef degradation is the impact of both artisanal and industrial fishing activities. These activities are often poorly regulated or unregulated, leading to decimated fish populations and damage to the reef structure. The problem is often compounded by the absence of a coherent management strategy for reef resources.

Intensification of coastal development also contributes significantly to coral reef degradation. This includes pollution from land-based sources (e.g., plastics and derelict fishing gear), the drainage of coastal wetlands, discharge of sewage, fertilizers and other contaminants into waterways, and the construction of coastal infrastructure such as roads, buildings, and ports.

These activities are often accompanied by poor waste management practices (e.g., untreated sewage effluent, chemical leaks). These local drivers of degradation can be exacerbated by climate change. In addition to these local stressors, there is a growing problem projecting from global causes for which the reef-dependent communities in the targeted countries have few other alternatives for their livelihoods and subsistence and coastal protection. Root causes of local degradation of coral reef ecosystems include:

- Overfishing and poor management of reef resources.
- Intensification of coastal development.

#### Poverty and the lack of economic opportunities
Reef-dependent communities often consist of vulnerable populations, mostly women, girls and indigenous communities who are disproportionately affected by climate variables, with few opportunities for income generation. In many cases, these communities live in poverty, with over half (51%) earning less than US$3.90 per day and over 323 million people (63% of total) live on less than US$5.50 per day. To support their families, individuals will resort to overfishing, destructive fishing, destruction of mangroves for timber and unsustainable aquaculture such as the use of cyanide or dynamite. These practices are not only environmentally damaging but also detrimental to supporting wealth creation for the poorest and most marginalized[1]. More sustainable models of aquaculture, fishing and tourism development are needed. In addition, diversified economic opportunities are needed for reef-dependent communities to reduce pressure on reefs.

#### Increasing coastal development
Development of coastal areas, including large tourist and infrastructure projects, often fails to take into account the impacts that these developments have on coral reefs. Coastal development has a direct impact through both direct destruction and increased sedimentation from poorly managed construction. Coastal developers must take into account the impact on coral reefs and associated ecosystems. In particular, we need to factor in the importance of the reef as a nursery for fish.

#### Unustainable or uncontrolled tourism
Most tour operators and managers have limited knowledge about the impacts of climate change on coral reefs and its potential impact on tourism and their livelihoods. Furthermore, a lack of understanding of these impacts can lead to poor management practices such as overfishing, destruction of mangroves for timber, poaching or breaking off parts of the reef. Overcapacity of sites can lead to stressful conditions for marine and coastal species. Responsible or regenerative tourism practices must be integrated to transition the sector away from coral reef degradation in tropical regions.

#### Pollution & waste
Communities in the targeted countries often have poor systems in place for the treatment of wastewater, stormwater, floodwater and agricultural runoff including erosion and sedimentation. This can lead to increased algal growth, which can smother corals and reduce their ability to reproduce. Coral reefs are also impacted by plastic pollution from renovations in water treatment, and stronger control of agricultural runoff are needed. The management of plastics and other waste is critical for the health of coral reefs as well as the wider marine environment and associated reef ecosystems. Educational campaigns and waste management infrastructure are needed to reduce litter reaching coral reefs, which compromises the health of the entire ecosystem.

#### Limits to our ability to restore coral reefs
Once degraded or destroyed, coral reefs are extremely hard to restore. Coral reef restoration is a slow process that requires significant investment in time and energy. At this stage, it is still difficult to scale-up and spread out coral reef restoration efforts to keep pace with the rate of degradation. There is a need for greater investment into restoring degraded coral reefs, including change resistant coral species and techniques to maximise their growth and recolonization in degraded reef habitats.

#### Local impacts on coral reefs can compound the impact of climate change
Direct human-induced negative impacts on marine biodiversity and coastal habitat exacerbate the impact of ocean warming and acidification. Increasing global climate and local anthropogenic pressures are leading to a decline in coral cover across many regions, including some of the largest parts of the world's tropical regions. Warm-water coral reefs are largely dependent on the physical and chemical changes occurring at their surface. Coral reefs are also vulnerable to invasion by non-native species such as crown-of-thorns starfish or invasive alien species, which further degrade the ecosystem. Uncontrolled tourism, land reclamion and poorly managed coastal development can all contribute to the demise of coral reefs. ## ECONOMIC VALUE OF CORAL REEFS

## B

### Adaptation Rationale

Coral reefs will need all the help they can get in the coming decades. As explained above, widespread loss and degradation of coral reefs are strongly influenced by the presence and management of local stressors. Although mitigating the global increase in sea surface temperature is critical to protecting coral reefs, it is also important to build resilience at the local level. The health of coral ecosystems and adjoining local anthropogenic stresses makes reef more resilient to the effects of climate change and better adapted to recover from bleaching events.

### Reef Adaptation: New economic models for coastal communities

In order to adapt to the threat of climate change, reef-dependent communities must transform their relationship with coral reefs in a way that brings together the sustainability of the ecosystem and ecosystem services and dependent livelihoods. New sustainable business models and practices, particularly in the tourism sector, can help to create new revenue streams from the value of reef resources while also protecting the resilience of coral reefs and thus the communities that rely on them. For this reason, additional investment, particularly from the private sector, may be needed.

### Prioritizing the reefs with the greatest potential to survive

To increase the resiliency of coral reefs and the ecosystem services they provide to climate change, significant global reductions in greenhouse gas emissions are urgently needed. At the same time and just as urgently, we need to focus on improving resilience at the local level through greater protection and acclimation of coral reefs. This will not only significantly enhance their adaptive capacity to climate change. These reefs then have the potential to act as source reefs from which corals can regenerate in the future if climate conditions can be stabilized. In addition, reef restoration projects can also help to increase resilience by reducing the impacts of bleaching events and other stressors on coral reefs. This will reduce the need for costly interventions such as chemical or destructive fishing, preventing overfishing of herbivorous fish and restoring places of ecological priority following stress events.

## B

### Preventing coral reefs from transitioning away from coral

We recognize that the impacts of climate change are likely to be so pervasive that for most reefs there is little chance of effective system adaptation irrespective of which emission scenario is followed. The reality is, that for many current reef ecosystems, they will be locally forced transition to another ecological state in which corals are no longer dominant. This is because coral reefs are the foundation on which communities rely for income and subsistence. The GCFR will focus on interventions that prevent this transition from happening and mitigate its impacts where it does occur. In doing so, the adaptation pathway for coral reef needs to be attempted to avoid such systemic transformation of basic ecosystems.

### Identifying those reefs that can act as climate refuges

Identifying and protecting areas of the ocean where conditions are most stable a key role for adaptation to climate change. As was the case during historical periods of climate change, climate refuge areas retain their coral cover while other reef systems become degraded or even disappear due to climate change. Climate refuge have been recommended by numerous authors as a key component of any strategy and biodiversity conservation and ecosystem-based management (EBM) approaches to climate change adaptation. Coral climate refugia should be incorporated into Marine Protected Areas, which can provide a safe haven for species in a changing environment.

### Deploying investments to ensure resilience

Deploying investments to ensure resilience ultimately form the foundation on which climate dispersed and local scale management and adaptation. ## The Grand Window (GW) and Twin Window (TW)

The Grand Window (GW) and Twin Window (TW) will operate independently but complementary and coordinated roles to maximize impact of the GCF. The GW aims to establish an enabling environment for the IW, more broadly to financing core infrastructure needs, including water, energy, transport, health, education, agriculture, etc., through grant funding from the GW targets technical assistance, capacity development, emergency grants, and monitoring and evaluation, and contributes to pipeline incubation and sourcing for the IW. The GW also has a philanthropic mandate such that out of all the projects it funds, at least 20% must be in support of Least Developed Countries (LDCs), Small Island Developing States (SIDS) Trust Fund (UNDP and GFCS), the German, French and UK Governments, and private foundations Paul G. Allen Family Foundation, Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, Global Environment Facility (GEF), International Finance Corporation (IFC), World Conservation Society, WWF, Conservation International, MAB Fund and Blue Finance (collectively, “the Commissioning Agents”). Although the GW is not a lending window, it will provide grants to support IW activities in LDCs, SIDS, fragile states and least dependent communities. To this end, the objectives for each Window and the governance structure of the overall fund have been designed to ensure that the IW and GW work in tandem to deliver key synergies that will maximize the impact of the GCF.

The integrated dual Window model enables the GCF to build on the respective strengths and rents of the two Windows, and ultimately to achieve its twin goals of climate change mitigation and adaptation. The GW is a critical enabler for IW programs in achieving adaptation to climate change. This collaboration materializes in an iterative, reinforcing dialogue that includes:

- **IW sharing inputs to help identifying GW priorities:** While sourcing its pipeline and engaging with Grant Window Commissioning Agents, the IW will provide inputs on the most pressing needs of LDCs and SIDS, including the pipeline gaps (sectors, technologies, infrastructure, etc.), needs for capacity building, or need for support in policy reforms and institutional strengthening.
- **GW supporting development of pipeline for the IW:** The GW, in coordination with Commissioning Agents and key stakeholders, will provide inputs to the IW on how to address these needs. This could include providing technical assistance for the fund portfolio, working for instance with local accelerators and incubators, providing financial support to small and medium enterprises (SMEs), start-up companies and NGOs to develop their commercial activities, and undertaking site-based studies to identify drivers of degradation and potential business models that could address these drivers.

### Assessment of the independent Technical Advisory Panel

#### 1. Impact potential: Safe; High

1. Coral reefs are an important ecosystem for underwater life, protect coastal areas by reducing wave energy, resist and storm damage, and provide food security and livelihoods to more than 1 billion people worldwide through the ecosystem services, coastal protection and economic benefits.
2. However, climate change is warming the oceans with devastating consequences to coral reefs, which are experiencing increasingly frequent mass bleaching events that can eventually lead to their death. The global temperature increase by 2050 is projected to grow. According to the proposal, even under the moderate emissions scenario of representative concentration pathways (RCPs), the average surface air temperature in the tropics is expected to rise to 1.8°C and 1.44°C by the end of the century, eliminating most warm-water coral reefs by 2050. The global mean sea level is projected to increase up to 30 per cent of former cover at 1.5°C warming and to less than 7 per cent at 2°C warming (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services, 2019).
3. Coral reefs are also under threat from indirect anthropogenic pressures from different sources. The combination of local anthropogenic disturbance and ocean warming means that up to 75 per cent of reefs are already considered threatened or degraded, reducing their ability to provide valuable ecosystem goods and services to people.
4. Coral reef ecosystems are highly vulnerable to climate change impacts such as the overexploitation of fish resources, poverty and lack of economic opportunities, increasing coastal development, unsustainable and uncontrolled tourism, poor pollution control and waste management, and limitations in the capacity of local communities to adapt to these changes. Although the global outlook for coral reefs is bleak, there is evidence from a range of field studies and modelling that some reefs can be resilient under certain conditions if they are managed well; ameliorate the impact of rising temperatures and ocean acidification. In other words, from a reef management perspective, the impacts of climate change increase the need to abate other stress factors on reefs.
5. On the other hand, local drivers of degradation are deteriorating the resilience of coral reef ecosystems and changing perceptions with respect to what the real reef ecosystem is.

#### 1.4.4 Project presents several adaptation barriers
- These are common to most of the selected countries, including low awareness of the economic importance of coral reefs that has led to a lack of political will to protect them.

- Weak policy frameworks and limited enforcement mechanisms make it difficult for countries to have a clear strategy to invest in areas where the countries are willing to take action to protect ocean resources.

#### 1.4.5 Limited private finance
- There is also limited private finance for reef adaptation and conservation.

#### 1.4.6 Investment in coral reefs are very limited
- Increasingly, private investment capital and innovative finance tools are needed to efficiently scale-up initiatives to save coral reef and other marine resources.

Every two weeks, women from the group collect bottles and other recyclable materials for recycling. ## 3.2 Gender-Responsive Stakeholder Engagement and Consultation

### Stakeholder Consultation Provides a critical opportunity for a project developer to engage with stakeholders in a gender sensitive manner and to share and promote understanding about and a sense of ownership of the project. It provides an opportunity to examine views on risks (and mitigants), impacts, benefits and opportunities. It provides a valuable feedback mechanism during the project design and outcomes and help the project developer to identify and control external risks.

Stakeholder engagement and consultation should consider different gender relationships and roles. Women and men typically fulfill different responsibilities and opportunities depending on the context and the country. In some countries women are more likely to be informal workers. Because women often are assigned lower societal status relative to men, women tend to have lower confidence, less influence and less involvement in the design, decisions and engagement projects. Projects should therefore consider how best to engage with women. It is important that all stakeholder consultation processes reach a wide range of community representatives in ways that ensure equal and effective participation of women and men in consultation, and that gender issues are fully integrated into comprehensive social and environmental impact assessments.

### Engagement

Gender-responsive stakeholder engagement should take place before and throughout project implementation so it can inform the design of the project to ensure that women can participate in the design phase. In addition, engagement should be promoted throughout the implementation of the project. Gender-responsive stakeholder engagement is a continuous basis. Gender-responsive stakeholder engagement shall include the following activities:

- Understand the gender context to identify engagement approaches:
  - Engage with the community, in particular women, to determine the best way to communicate.
  - Engage with women in focus group settings facilitated by a female to capture views and information.
  - Use participatory techniques to capture gender specific information e.g. gender

## SCOPE AND APPLICATION OF THE GENDER POLICY*
The main objective of the Gender Policy is to strengthen stakeholders' responsiveness to the multiple, culturally-derived principles of gender equality and women's empowerment and to address the inter-related links between gender equality, natural resources management and environmental sustainability.
The Gender Policy will apply to all projects in the Global Fund for Coral Reefs (GFGR) and supports the GFGR Gender Action Plan and the Environmental Social Management System (ESMS).

Gender analysis is a systematic way to identify context-specific key issues and factors that contribute to gender inequalities. It may consist of primary or secondary research, or a combination of both. The purpose of conducting a gender analysis is to provide an overview clearly understanding gender issues within the targeted population. A gender analysis enables project developers to understand the implications of gender on climate change mitigation, adaptation, and resilience strategies, as well as the potential for gender-sensitive and inclusive interventions that can benefit all members of a community. It also allows the project developers to develop indicators to better assess how initiatives have engaged with or impacted different groups.

Gender analysis explores individual, relational and structural factors, within both public and private spheres, as individuals are likely to experience gender differently, or hold different roles.

## Step 5.a Establish and Action Plan and Project Gender Goals (Actions)

Based on the gender analysis described in Step 4.c project developers shall develop a project-level gender action plan which can address the following:

- Collect, analyze and apply sex-disaggregated data and using gender indicators to inform gender-responsive monitoring, evaluation, reporting and learning on programmatic activities;

- Analyze risks that the project may experience or pose, putting measures in place to ensure that these are mitigated. This includes addressing risks related to preventing gender-based violence, and seize opportunities to address gender gaps and support empowerment of women;
- Structure and manage gender-sensitive project teams that demonstrate appropriate capacities and technical expertise to support gender-responsive action;

The following questions can guide the project developer to focus on selecting appropriate indicators:

- Will the project positively or negatively engage women and men?
- Will it impose increased time or care burdens on women relative to men?
- Is there a process in place to track and verify gender impacts? (positive and/or negative)
- Is there an improvement in the ratio of women to men engaging in the project decisions and outcomes?
- Is there an increased sense of confidence and empowerment among the project beneficiaries?
