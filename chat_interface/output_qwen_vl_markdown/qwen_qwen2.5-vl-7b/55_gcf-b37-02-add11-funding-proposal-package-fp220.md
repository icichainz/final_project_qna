# A.2 Executive Summary

A progressive but rapid shift in agricultural practices in Africa will be critical as harsh climatic conditions will increase in most countries of the region and new climatic threats for crop production and livestock keeping are projected to emerge.

By 2035, the current climate change adaptation strategies that have been developed or are being implemented will no longer be suitable for current practices. The lack of adequate and appropriate private sector financing for Climate Change Adaptation (CCA) in Africa is a major barrier to scaling up CCA interventions across the continent, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa. Currently, there is a gap of well-functioning and replicable, significant scale private sector CCA finance platforms that can be scaled up by the public sector. The existing private sector CCA finance platforms, which are often driven by public sector, on the other hand, are often grant-based and have limited capacity to leverage sustainable long-term market-driven solutions.

The Africa Rural Climate Adaptation Finance Mechanism (ARCAFIM) AFM is aimed to introduce a practical, widely implementable model for achieving a paradigm shift towards systematic large-scale use of private financial resources for CCA in Sub-Saharan Africa. The ARCAFIM will be implemented as an innovative component of the Green Climate Fund Programme.

The key implementing/financing partners of AFM are the regional ARCAFIM 'Host Banks', which are the lead banks of the four regional ARCAFIMs, and the Global Environment Facility (GEF) Secretariat as the International Entity. On-lend the funds provided for ARCAFIM by GCF and other international financiers and be the lender of record of the ARCAFIM to the Host Banks. AFM will disburs the funds for ARCAFIM on lending to the regional host banks, which in turn will provide loans to smallholder farmers and agribusinesses to access CCA finance and resources. Supported by appropriate tracking and monitoring arrangement, AFM will monitor and oversee that Host Banks are providing loans to smallholder farmers and agribusinesses to access CCA finance and resources.

The ARCAFIM is designed as a multi-year programme with phased implementation in four regions of Sub-Saharan Africa. The ARCAFIM will be implemented by the GCF through its implementing partner, AFM, which has been established beneficiaries based on agreements and definitions of the Loan Agreements between IFAD and the Host Banks.

Operating with a projected total budget of USD 500 million, the ARCAFIM overall development goal is "Wider use of climate-resilient agricultural practices in Sub-Saharan Africa, enhancing food security and livelihoods through climate resilient livelihoods". To achieve this, the two programme Outcomes are: (i) ARCAFIM IPs expand access to CCA finance and resources for smallholder farmers and agribusinesses; and (ii) ARCAFIM IPs enhance capacity of rural communities.

The ARCAFIM will be implemented in four regions of Sub-Saharan Africa. The ARCAFIM is designed as a multi-year programme with phased implementation in four regions of Sub-Saharan Africa. ## Background

Under the ARCA/IFM framework, the same model, programme structure and key activities will be used in each region to achieve the programme targets, with adjustments to the local climatic, economic and financial sector environments. The objective is to demonstrate a replicable approach for scaling up climate-smart agriculture (CSA) financing by designing small producers and rural poor. The core objective is to achieve a demonstration effect and paradigm shift by designing approaches and methodologies for private sector CCA lending targeting small producers and rural poor, and pilot of concepts.

## Climate Baseline

2. In East Africa, the climates covering the programme's target countries (Kenya, Rwanda, Tanzania and Uganda) are diverse, ranging from arid to temperate or tropical. Temperatures are high and characterized by little seasonal variation in most areas. The highest temperatures occur during the dry season when solar radiation is at its maximum. In Kenya, the highest temperatures are experienced in the drier interior regions towards the north and east, where average summer temperatures can reach 30°C (86°F) or more. In Uganda, the highest temperatures are found in the equatorial regions (much of the area described), there are two rainy seasons with peaks in October and April; in the northern highlands, there is a single rainy season from March to May.

### Climate change vulnerabilities and impacts
- 6. Climate variability and change pose major obstacles to resilience in East Africa, where increasing temperatures and changing precipitation patterns are expected to have adverse economic growth, small producer livelihoods, food security, health, and ecosystems.
- The region is characterized by rapid urbanization at about 4.5–6% annually for much of the region; higher than the global average (1.2%). Urbanization is projected to increase from around 30% in 2010 to over 70% of the population, however, still be in rural areas where population growth continues and people rely heavily on agriculture.
- The region has experienced a significant decline in crop productivity since the mid-1980s. This is attributed to a 24% drop in all the regional countries. Projections indicate that reduced crop productivity in much of the region is expected to continue, with the most severe impacts being felt by smallholder farmers who are already among the poorest producers.
- The combination of urbanization and declining agricultural productivity and pastoralism will adversely affect food security for millions of people across East Africa. This is projected to lead to an increase in poverty to almost 40 million people across Kenya, Rwanda, Tanzania, and Uganda. In recent years, heat stress has already affected maize production in Ethiopia.
- The region also faces the threat of increased incidence of vector-borne diseases like malaria as poles are poised to expand into the warmer Eastern African highlands and Lake Victoria region?

## B.3. Project/programme description

### ARCAFIM Overview

1. Overall ARCAFIM Approach: Rural small producers are responsible for estimated average of 80% of food production in Sub-Saharan African countries**, while MSMEs involved in food systems provide services and value-added products to the rural population. Due to their limited access to resources, financial capital, economic, social and political factors, these small producers and MSMEs disproportionately experience the effects of climate change. Despite their significant contribution to global food production and agricultural productivity, they urgently need dedicated investment resources to build stronger resilience to climate change, but have been unable to access them due to a lack of collateral and other financial constraints. Therefore, support for adaptation activities by small producers in ARCAFIM target countries is unknown, evidence suggests that this is an area where there are significant opportunities to increase food production** under the global CC financing which includes both adaptation and mitigation resources***.

2. As discussed in B.1 above, various barriers, including both demand and supply side challenges, affect the economic viability of small producers and MSMEs in accessing finance for climate change adaptation (CC). The Financial Mechanism (ARCAFIM) will address these barriers causing limited CC financing from formal financial sector lenders. ARCAFIM seeks to provide a viable alternative by providing access to finance for small producers via the mainstream private financial sector in each participating country combined with targeted technical support delivered through the project. This will enable small producers and MSMEs to access finance more easily, thereby doing so. ARCAFIM seeks to achieve a demonstration effect and paradigm shift towards large-scale improvements in food production and resilience of rural communities among the economically active low-income rural populations and small producers, notably women and youth.

The ARCAFIM is designed as a blended finance structure with a focus on mobilizing private sector financing and minimum concessionality, without crowding out private finance.

97. **Reinforcing markets for CCA under the DFI market development principle:** The ARCAFIM financial inclusion component will support a market-based additional credit line to smallholders, while providing adequate risk mitigation through equity investment, personal guarantees, or other forms of collateral. This is provided by IFAD with the financial support of international financiers (partners). Thereby, local institutions are contracted to on-lend directly or indirectly to small producers and rural MSMEs for CCA investments. The ARCAFIM will be designed in such a way that it can address lack of sector-specific capacity and strengthen the potential for greater private sector participation over time, and eventually create and drive bankable demand in the target groups in collaboration with the Host Bank.

## F. RISK ASSESSMENT AND MANAGEMENT

### F.1 Risk factors and mitigations measures

#### Selected Risk Factor: Weak CCA Finance Market Development

| Criteria | Probability | Impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Technical and operational | High | Medium |

The financial sectors in the ARCAFIM target country have not yet established CCA finance frameworks for the mainstream financial operations and private firms have limited access to CCA finance portfolios. This leads to the core operational risk for any CCA finance programme in the region: limited demand by small producers and MMDMs for appropriate financing, as well as a lack of awareness among these groups about the availability of CCA services by financial institutions. The risk often results in low disbursement volumes of CCA schemes particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa.

(Mitigation Measures)

To mitigate this risk, substantial TA resources are included under ARCAFIM to eliminate both the demand and supply side barriers for CCA finance. To address the demand side barrier, IFAD will provide technical assistance to the Host Bank to develop appropriate CCA financing products. High-class TA will be provided to achieve high staff capacities in the Host Bank to develop appropriate CCA financing products. The TA will also include capacity building on financial literacy and awareness raising for small producers and MMDMs. To address the supply side barrier, IFAD will provide technical assistance to the Host Bank to develop a robust disbursement system. This will be adequately coordinated and resourced. Further, CCA investment progress and performance data collection and reporting systems will also be developed in-house at the Host Bank. Residual risk after the mitigation measures is expected to be moderate to low.

# Selected Risk Factor S: Over-indebtedness of Small Agribusinesses

| Category | Probability | Impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Credit | Medium | Medium |

## Description

Risk of over-indebtedness of small producers and agribusinesses can materialize, if in a relatively large scheme such as ARCAF/IMF, loans are provided to the low-income borrowers that their repayment capacity. Even if such practices can provide short-term relief against the adverse CC impact, the overall longer term consequences to the target group can be negative and increase instability in the sector.

## Mitigation Measure(s)

To mitigate the risk of small agribusinesses taking loans beyond their repayment capacity, the aim of ARCAF/IMF On-lending is to provide a loan that is affordable for the borrowers. This will include CCA options with benefits, appropriate financial services, business planning capacity, investment start-up and financial management skills of the borrowers. The aim should be to ensure that the loans are provided in such a way as to enable the borrowers to assess their potential borrowers, to help inform the PPFs of the real investment risks and opportunities. The capacity building activities will also include training on business planning, financial management, and secured financing in the rural agricultural sectors, thereby reducing the risks of over-indebtedness of the borrowers. After these mitigating measures, the over-indebtedness risk is considered to be low.

## Assessment of the independent Technical Advisory Panel

### Impact potential: Scale: Medium-High

1. Increased weather unpredictability and frequency of extreme weather events will continue to be a major challenge for smallholder farmers in Africa and other target countries, as they do not have the capacity to adapt and respond to climate change.
2. New and innovative financial products are needed for the smallholders and MMEs, as current practices will no longer be suitable.
3. Agricultural investments in Africa, regardless of climate change, have always been viewed as risky by the local banking sector due to the high level of risk associated with smallholder farmers. However, the perceived risk of the smallholder agriculture sector in Africa has been exacerbated by the increasing unpredictability and frequency of extreme weather events and its variability; investments in climate-resilient and low-emission agriculture are perceived as high risk, especially by the local banking sector there.
4. There is currently a limited financial product available for smallholders and MMEs that can provide products with higher interest rates, collateral requirements, and short repayment periods.

The programme will support the development of new and innovative financial products to address these challenges in Eastern Africa. The programme will provide CCA loans to smallholders and rural MSEs through a leading commercial bank in the region (CCA loan products, which will aim to reduce risk for lenders by providing guarantees).
5. 40 per cent of the loan programme will be distributed by the host bank (Equity Bank) by providing direct micro-loans to smallholder farmers.
6. 30 per cent of the loan programme will be distributed by the host bank and subsidiaries by providing direct loans to small and medium-sized enterprises; and
7. 30 per cent of the loan programme will be distributed by providing wholesale loans to MSEs, which in turn provide micro-loans to smallholder farmers and other small-scale enterprises.

As a result, the programme will strengthen the resilience of the target beneficiaries in the nascent CCA financial sector and thereby support sustainable agriculture, food and water security, and poverty reduction.

Equity Bank will have to submit inclusion notices to motivate successive disbursements. The TAP notes that the combination of the below-market interest rate combined with the first loss mitigation of the (IFAD) GCF facility could lead to a distortion in the market for microfinance loans (interest charged). (The Operational Manual only stipulates that “…1 each Qualifying Loan must be provided at an interest rate not exceeding 20% per annum, excluding any fees and charges.”) The TAP notes that the combination of the below-market interest rates with loan sharing products provided by similarly mandated financial institutions, however, not below market interest rates, could lead to a distortion in the market for microfinance loans (interest charged). The Operations Manual, and ultimately the loan agreement between IFAD and Equity Bank, should contain a provision making it obligatory for Equity Bank to provide evidence that it has passed on these rates to its clients. Such evidence could include collation of data from equity bank’s own balance sheet or other evidence that IFAD had funded these loans from its own balance sheet, or other objectively verifiable market benchmark.

(b) IFAD should also foresee implementation of an annual audit to determine whether Equity Bank has passed on concessional terms to its loan clients. These audits would involve checking the interest rates charged and compliance with the conditions set by IFAD.

# ARCAFIM GENDER EQUALITY AND SOCIAL INCLUSION STRATEGY

## INTRODUCTION

The overall development goal of ARCAFIM is "to upscale in rural communities across the target countries rendered climate vulnerable through increased CCA investments, CA capacity, employment creation and rural incomes." The key ARCAFIM objectives are: (i) increased climate change resilience of small-scale producers and rural micro-, small- and medium-sized enterprises (MSMEs) active in the local food systems through CCA investments across agricultural value chains; (ii) improved access to finance for MSMEs, particularly women-owned businesses, to adapt to climatic changes. An underlying ambition of the programme is to achieve a paradigm shift towards systematic large-scale use of public financial resources for rural CCA investments.

The programme aims at a balanced mix of programme resources for women-led investments and to investments generating employment for women (and 30% for youth), and for advisory services to achieve a gender balance among the beneficiaries in service delivery. This annex sets out the main parameters of the gender and social inclusion (GSI) action plan for ARCAFIM. Part 1 provides an overview on the role of governments, civil society organizations, and financial institutions in the context of climate change adaptation (CCA) and financial inclusion. Particular attention is paid to the importance of identifying and addressing gender norms which often act as key barriers to the financial inclusion of women, youth and people with disabilities.

Part 2 presents an overview of the GSI action plan setting out the main actions and targets. These will be further developed during early implementation.


## PART I: GSI ANALYSIS

Whilst modest gains are being made by East African rural women in improving their livelihoods, gender inequalities persist in many areas, such as political representation, access to education and training, access to quality and affordable health care services, and access to credit. The situation is exacerbated by the absence of laws that ensure the presence of progressive legal and policy frameworks promoting gender equality; their implementation is weak. In 2021, East Africa ranked lowest in the world on the Global Gender Gap Index (GGI), which measures the gap between men and women in four key areas: health, education, economic participation and opportunity, and political empowerment. The GGI also ranks countries according to the gender inequality index, which reflects gender-based inequalities in health, empowerment and economic dimensions. Contributing factors include entrenched discriminatory social norms, which are reflected in the OECD’s Social Institutions and Gender Index (SIGI). Rwanda has the lowest levels of inequality and discrimination in society in the East African region.

Climate change is not only one of the greatest environmental and development challenges facing the world today, but also a major driver of gender inequalities. Women and girls are disproportionately affected by climate change because they have less access to resources such as land, water, and fuel. Women and girls suffer disproportionately from the effects of climate change due to a variety of social, cultural, and economic factors. Rising temperatures, irregular rainfall patterns and seasonal uncertainty will severely disrupt all aspects of life for women and girls in East Africa. These include increased vulnerability to food insecurity, loss of human and animal diseases, dislocation of human populations, and destruction of property and infrastructure. As livelihoods become increasingly precarious, women are more likely than men to be displaced from their homes and villages.

There is an ever-growing body of evidence that identifies the critical role women must play in climate change mitigation and adaptation: women are innovators, leaders, entrepreneurs, workers and customers in climate

As global temperatures have increased and shifted weather systems in ways that occur less frequently than in the past, extreme events such as droughts, hurricanes, wildfires, floods, and heatwaves have become more intense and unpredictable. Women and girls are particularly vulnerable to the effects of climate change due to factors such as entrenched poverty, limited social and cultural norms, a lack of entitlements to land and other credit assets, and unequal access to land, water, and productive assets, compounded by limited mobility and decision-making power in many countries. They are also finding innovative ways to adapt to climate change impacts on their communities and environments. Women’s participation is critical to the success of agroforestry, conservation agriculture or impact or organic soil management techniques, or leading community-based reforestation and restoration efforts.

The AGRAfem is a timely innovation to extend funds for rural adaptation to small producers in food systems and rural value chains. As smallholder producers*, women tend to utilize smaller lots of less productive land than men and have fewer resources including labour, limited technology and inputs. They focus on the subsistence production of food crops for their own consumption or sale as well as providing services and engaged with markets. With insecure tenure over land and other natural resources, they are reluctant to commit to CCA measures and unable to access finance.

As enterprises, women tend to be small-scale producers (e.g., microenterprises), women typically engage in low value addition activities, utilizing low-cost and labour-intensive technologies. The majority will be active at the lower end of the scale of MSMEs (with under 5 employees and less than US$100,000 turnover).

Larger enterprises are more likely to be owned by men. Women tend not to own land or property where they may experience gender gaps in pay, terms and conditions of employment, opportunities for advancement and career progression.

Although many women are active members of VSLAs and SACCOs, they save and borrow small sums of money.* They often have a weak voice in intra-household decision making regarding the use of resources. Women face significant barriers to accessing finance including lack of collateral required for women to open a bank account, obtain loans, enter into a contract or register a business.

Women’s ability to invest in CCA technologies and practices, either as smallholders or entrepreneurs, is compromised by their limited access to land, financial services, markets, technology, information on ownership, limited financial literacy, and a lack of business skills to develop sound CCA investment proposals. With insecure land tenure, a lack of an identity document, an absence of credit history and no formal savings account, women are often unable to access finance from banks or other institutions.

Women have an aversion to risk (typically regarding relatively new investments such as CCA) and prefer investments offering benefits in the short term. As a result, women often end up being offered very small short-term loans, which they cannot repay. Women also face significant barriers to accessing land, where women are needed for women entrepreneurs to move from micro-enterprise status to realize SME status, which is critical if they are to access finance and other resources that will enable them to invest in CCA to boost their productivity, enhance their income, and improve their climate resilience and ability to cope with a variety of shocks.

Financial institutions often hesitate to regard women smallholders and small entrepreneurs as “safe” borrowers. This is despite evidence showing that women-owned small businesses have the lowest proportion of non-performing loans, and that women-owned businesses tend to be more prudent in their use of credit than men’s enterprises. Financial institutions are not always able to overcome the specific needs of these groups. Indeed, gender-blind products may inadvertently reinforce gender inequalities.

Women’s organizations and federations have limited representation and engagement in decision-making processes relating to climate change adaptation and mitigation, rendering their views and priorities invisible.

### Youth

Populations in East Africa are youthful, with approximately 20% aged 15 – 24 years old.[6] Farming and self-employment in the agri-food sector are the dominant categories of youth employment in rural areas.[7] In regions where there are few employment opportunities, entrepreneurship presents a real opportunity to better their livelihoods. However, young people face many challenges when trying to start up or expand businesses; they can face many challenges from inappropriate education systems and inadequate skills, limited training opportunities, a lack of access to credit (and sometimes land), a lack of access to goods and services, and financial exclusion (they are often not seen as bankable). In turn, this leads to low levels of entrepreneurship, financial exclusion, poverty, and child labour in agriculture.[8]

Youth is a heterogeneous group (in terms of gender, age, ethnicity, education level, employment status, etc.) and consists of a mixture of actors over time. Young women face a double challenge of being young and being female, due to gender-based norms, a higher chance of being married and pregnant at an early age, limited physical mobility, and more household responsibilities. Since youth represent a significant proportion of the population, it is essential that priorities and opportunities cater for their different livelihood options.

PART II: GENDER AND SOCIAL INCLUSION ACTION PLAN

The programme has set a target to invest 50% of programme resources for women-led investments and to investments generally targeted at women, and for the advisory services to achieve a gender balance among the end beneficiaries and service delivery. In addition, 30% of beneficiaries are to be the youth.

The objective of the ARCAFIM GESI action plan is to ensure that women, youth and people with disabilities have equal opportunity to access and benefit from CCA financing as men and to secure climate-resilient livelihoods. The action plan has four principal audiences:

- end-users of CCA finances and ARCAFIM programme services: smallholders, MSMEs and their communities, including women, youth and people with disabilities;
- financial service providers: Participating Financial Institutions (PFIs) which will be offering CCA financing to the target beneficiaries; and
- other advisory providers such as extension and extension staff, input suppliers, agribusinesses, value chain actors; and
- programme management: ARCAFIM Facility Coordination Unit at IFAD, PMU at the host bank HQ and ARCAFIM focal points at country subsidiaries.


Purpose

The purpose of the ARCAFIM programme is to make rural climate change adaptation finance work for Africa's future:

- To catalyse and scale up private sector climate change adaptation financing targeting smallholders and rural and rural MSMEs in the agricultural sector in Africa.
- To address the environmental, technical, economic, social and political factors faced by smallholder producers and rural MSMEs to adapt and build stronger resilience to climate change and thereby bring about growth and opportunity.

# ARCAFIM GENDER AND SOCIAL INCLUSION ACTION STRATEGY

## Activities
- **Impact Statement:** The ARCAFIM Program will seek to deploy innovative financial products along with Technical Assistance (TA) and capacity building for rural finance through the International Finance Corporation (IFC) in East Africa (EA) countries to address the unmet needs of rural producers involved in MSME food systems. ARCAFIM will mobilize US$ 270 million in total, including US$ 150 million from IFC and US$ 120 million from other sources. The Program will aim to identify, mitigate and adapt or increase their resilience to the negative impacts of climate change. Concurrently, ARCAFIM will seek to promote gender equality and social inclusion by ensuring that women and youth are provided equitable access to the benefits of the project, including employment and income generating activities.
- **Outcome Statement:** ARCAFIM will secure climate change-resilient livelihoods with the benefits of food and water security (including reduced workloads associated with household tasks), improved business and market opportunities for rural producers, increased productivity and efficiency in production processes and outputs, including income generated, and reductions in harmful practices and domestic violence. ARCAFIM will report on the extent to which women and youth are provided equitable access to project benefits, as well as how vulnerable groups are protected against harmful practices such as child labour and exploitation, in order to meet both gender equality and social inclusion objectives.
- **Gender Responsive Actions:** ARCAFIM shall play a crucial role in providing an avenue to connect target and empower women/youth/PSHs, built on their agency and operationalize this G4 Action strategy through strengthened awareness of climate threats and risk reduction processes as well as providing access to financial products and capacity building. ARCAFIM will ensure that women and youth are provided equitable access to project benefits, including employment and income generating activities, and in turn provide access to training, collaborative efforts on tackling different aspects of climate adaptation such as water management, soil conservation, and improved agricultural practices. A more detailed list of organizations can be accessed in Annex 4.
- **Mitigation:** ARCAFIM shall play a crucial role in promoting a balance of control between men and women program end beneficiaries over the factors of production, thereby facilitating their participation in decision-making processes.
