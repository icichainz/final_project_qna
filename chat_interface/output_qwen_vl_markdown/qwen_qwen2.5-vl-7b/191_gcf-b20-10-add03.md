### PROJECT / PROGRAMME SUMMARY

#### A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

The project is proposed to address South Tarawa’s water supply vulnerability due to climate change. The island faces significant challenges as its water supply is entirely dependent on underground freshwater lenses, the quality and quantity of which are seriously threatened by climate change-induced inundation and prolonged drought. Should these lenses fail, the island will be forced to rely on desalination plants that yield only 10% of water to five years. Taking a precautionary approach, the lenses cannot be relied upon as the main source of water in a future with climate change.

This project will provide the entire population of South Tarawa (estimated to be 62,298 people in 2018), more than half of Kiribati’s population, with a reliable, safe and climate resilient water supply. To achieve this, the project comprises:

- A 500 kWp solar photovoltaic (PV) power plant.
- The construction and rehabilitation water supply network infrastructure to reduce leakage and ensure that all residents can access the new clean water source.
- Building of a community center building and long-term performance-based contracts for operation of the new infrastructure; and
- An awareness program on climate change, water, sanitation and hygiene awareness program with strong involvement of local civil society organizations.

The project will lead to avoided GHG emissions through the following: households will no longer need to boil water using kerosene or diesel generators, pump and treat water due to reduced losses from the network, and the solar PV plant as opposed to diesel generators.

This project will be transformative for Kiribati and promote a paradigm shift in both the knowledge and practice of how small island developing states can increase the resilience of their water supply systems to climate change. This project will have significant co-benefits for other Pacific Island countries, particularly those with similar freshwater resources and households benefiting most. The cost of adapting South Tarawa’s water supply to projected climate impacts plus the investment in a range of adaptation mitigation impacts is US$80.9 million, 48% of the total project cost, and GCF’s contribution is critical for it to proceed.

### Water sector, resources and supply

As with all Pacific states, the sourcing and provisioning of water to the population is a priority and a challenge. The Government has been working on several initiatives aimed at improving access to safe drinking water and sanitation, including the population of South Tarawa. In 2008, Government adopted the National Water Resources Policy and the National Sanitation Policy. These policies were developed in response to the increasing demand for water and sanitation service demand created by South Tarawa's growing population. Government has further developed the Tarawa Water Master Plan 2010-2030 (Government, 2010) and the Tarawa Water and Sanitation Roadmap 2011-2028. These planning documents provide a strategic framework for addressing water supply and sanitation challenges in South Tarawa and a roadmap to meet South Tarawa's future water needs.

The existing sources of fresh water for consumers on South Tarawa are: (i) the Bonriki Buses lenses located at the eastern end of the island, which serve most population centers; (ii) lenses harvested by households and by small community groups; (iii) household groundwater wells (drawing from small lenses located below residential areas); (iv) small-scale boreholes; and (v) rainwater harvesting. The main sources are Bonriki Buses lenses for households, islands, seasons and year. The following can be observed (Government, 2010):

- **Bonriki and Buotla lenses**: Combined, these two lenses can currently provide an estimated 2,500 m³/day of good quality fresh water to the population on South Tarawa. However, this is not a sustainable source because they are by far the largest source of freshwater available. Water is abstracted from a grid of 22 galleries on Bonriki lens and 8 galleries on Buotla lens. The lenses are connected to a network of pipes that deliver water directly into the reticulated water supply network.

- **Rainwater harvesting**: This provides a useful but small-scale source of freshwater. It is estimated to provide up to 50 m³/day of good quality fresh water. However, in recent years, collection tanks have often been left empty for up to six months at a time.

- **Groundwater wells near people's homes from local lenses**: In recent decades, rapid population growth on the land over these lenses, and localized animal farming, have led to the contamination of the water in these lenses. In most cases, this has resulted in the abandonment of these lenses by households. It is generally estimated that the water from the local groundwater wells is unsafe to use and continues to deteriorate; however, some households still continue to use it.

- **Small-scale boreholes**: These are small-scale sources of freshwater. They provide a useful but small-scale source for only very small scale and can only meet a very small proportion of overall demand. Further, the plants have proven unreliable (typically due to lack of maintenance) and are not able to supply water at all times or in sufficient quantity for the population; and

- **Imported water**: This provides good quality water. However, this remains a very small source, notably because it is too expensive for almost all of the population.

At the operational level, the main actor is the Public Utilities Board (PUB), a state-owned enterprise. PUB was established in 1977 to provide water supply and sanitation services on South Tarawa and its outer islands. PUB operates the infiltration gallery pump stations in Buotla and Bonriki, the water treatment plant and the transfer pumps. PUB is responsible for the maintenance of these assets, and the provision and regulation of water supplied through South Tarawa.

PUB supplies the water from Bonriki and Buotla lenses through a reticulated water network. This includes pumps, treatment facilities, distribution pipes, reservoirs and distribution and dissipation points. It is estimated that the network currently reaches around 69% of the population in South Tarawa. The network is designed to provide water at a fairly high level of demand. The limited sustainable yield from the lenses, as well as very high physical losses from the network, mean that the quantity of water available for consumption is significantly lower than the demand. PUB supplies water through an intermittent service, typically for two hours every two days. The water is delivered at a very low pressure and there is much wastage due to leaks in the distribution system. This means that only about 10% of the water supplied can be used for consumption. A large majority of residents therefore boil water from the network.

#### Climate, water climate and climate change

Climate, Kiribati has a hot and humid tropical climate. Kiribati's climate varies considerably from year to year, driven largely by the El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO).

As temperatures are closely related to the temperature of the surrounding oceans. Average temperatures are relatively constant year-round, with changes in the temperature from season to season no greater than approximately 1°C.

(PACCSAPP2015)

Rainfall is strongly affected by the movement of the South Pacific Convergence Zone and the Intertropical Convergence Zone. These bands of heavy rainfall are caused by air rising over warm water where winds converge, resulting also in thunderstorms. The average annual rainfall for the period 1947-2016 was estimated to be 2,563 millimeters (mm). These observations show a slight upward trend during this time frame.

There is significant interannual variability – from a minimum of 398 mm in 1950 to a maximum of 4,356 mm in 1993.

Monthly rainfall is also characterized by extreme variability with monthly rainfall ranging from 0 to 825 mm. The overall monthly average for the period 1971-2000 was 175 mm. There are two wet seasons: a wet season from December to April and a dry season from May to November (White, 2011).

Drought, White (2011) provides a definition of drought in the Tarawa context as a function of rainfall in the preceding 12 months. Using this definition, a drought period may be sustained over several months or even years. There were 3 severe droughts between 1974 and 1985, with the most severe being from July to December 1976 when only 10 mm of rain had fallen in the preceding 12 months.

There was a severe drought period from June 1979 until May 1983. There were two severe droughts between 1985 and 1994, with the most severe being from July to December 1990 when only 21.7 mm of rain had fallen in the preceding 12 months.

The time between successive severe droughts also increased over this period (White, 2011).

Wave-winds in Kiribati are strongly influenced by both north-eastern and south-easterly seasonal trade winds, and the location of the South Pacific Convergence Zone, and by the El Niño Southern Oscillation from year to year. In Tarawa, waves are generated by wind action over the sea surface and can be classified as either wind-generated or swell-generated.

Swell propagating from extra-tropical storms in the North Pacific and South Ocean (PACCSAPP 2015).

A wave parameterization model was developed for use on land only. This model uses meteorological data, groundwater, dissolving freshwater resources, contaminating water in the lenses. NIIWA (2008) assessed and modeled meteorological and oceanic parameters in order to determine how wave height, wave direction, wave period, storm surge, storm tides, waves set up on land, and other factors affect coastal erosion.

### Drought:

There is far less certainty with regards to how climate change may affect the occurrence and intensity of future droughts. Although the climate change models (PACCSAP 2015) project overall that droughts will be less frequent, there is no consensus on whether they will become more intense or last longer.

### Climate and Abstraction Impacts in Atoll Environment (CAIA 2016) modelled the impacts of drought on the available yield from Bonriki lens catchment. The results were:

- (i) the impact of prolonged droughts lasting for one year occurred in 1998 – 2000 but persisting for six years and
- (ii) the impact of five droughts equivalent to the 1998 – 2000 drought occurring over a period of ten years.

The results show that prolonged droughts lasting for one year would result in an approximate 40% reduction of the available yield from the lens that would last for several years. Although these prolonged and repeated droughts could be considered as extreme events, they are realistic scenarios given existing knowledge of climate change and how it may affect droughts on Tarawa.

## C.3 Project / Programme Description

### Objective

The overall objective of the project is to provide South Tarawa's population with reliable access to a safe, resilient and low carbon water supply under a changing climate.

### Description of both climate change adaptation and climate change mitigation objectives

First, a desalination plant will be constructed to provide a reliable, climate-resilient source of fresh water for South Tarawa. Second, the water supply reticulated network will be renovated and expanded to ensure that there is constant supply of safe water to households on South Tarawa. This will reduce the need for households to boil water before consumption thereby regaining trust in the public network water and reduce or stop current practices of boiling water – thereby reducing GHG emissions. Finally, a PV power plant will be constructed to offset almost all of the GHG emissions associated with running the desalination plant.

The project's outputs are expected to meet the climate change adaptation and mitigation objectives. These outputs will be supported by the necessary capacity building, technical assistance and consumer outreach to meet both climate change adaptation and supply objectives.

#### Activity 1.1 - Construct desalination plant

Based on projected climate impact (see Box 1) and other factors, water demand forecasts until the year 2041 have been prepared (refer Annex B). To meet the projected demand, the next most climate resilient option is to construct a new desalination plant with an initial capacity of 4.0 million m³/day in Nia Makon. West Tarawa in the north of South Tarawa will be supplied by this plant and will replace existing plants with a fresh water source, while the balance 2.000 m³/day will cover increasing demand.

The plant's capacity (4.000 m³/day) is based on assuming a minimum of 57 liters per capita per day (lpcd) for consumption in the year 2031 and an increase to 68 lpcd by 2041, with a further increase to 90 lpcd by 2051. The demand is drawn down to around 25% of below). The project has adopted World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations of a minimum consumption level of 37 liters per capita per day for drinking water and other essential uses.

An additional 2,000 m³/day is added for commercial and institutional consumption. Finally, an additional 2 lpcd is needed to account for the impacts of climate change on water demand (e.g., increased evaporation rates), population growth in the region and population (in number and behaviour), and the level of uncertainty that comes with these. The desalination plant has a modular design which allows it to be expanded up to 6,000 m³/day as required by future demand.

The plant is also designed with selected provisions to enable expansion of the plant up to 6,000 m³/day total capacity in the future, without the need to upgrade supporting infrastructure. These are considered robust engineering design features to better enable PUB to undertake necessary expansion works in the future, as the population and demand increase.

## Box 1 - Translating Climate Change Risk in Water Supply into Design Parameters

As determined in the previous sections, the threat of sea overtopping and drought to South Tarawa is one of the most significant risks associated with climate change. After a period of rapid global temperature change, a sea overtopping or a drought event could catastrophically reduce the tenure yield. The duration of such an event would vary from 5 years up to 10 years (Box 2). The return period for such events is unknown; however, it can be estimated that the return period lies between 10 and 50 years.

There would be no water available from the leases for PUB during these events. In other years, that is, in non-event years, there would be sufficient water available to meet the demand for PUB.

To translate this climate vulnerability and risk into design parameters, the network planners and the project design team made the following assumptions:

1. The population will grow at a rate of 2% per year (the 'worst' event years).
2. To meet basic demand in event years, an alternative water supply is needed. This alternative water supply has to be capable of meeting the demand for PUB and also be capable of meeting additional demand due to population growth in non-event years; and
3. The number of event years over a period of 50 years are not even. During these years, a slightly higher volume of potable water capacity will be available to the population.

Recent studies (described further in F:2) concluded that seawater reverse osmosis desalination represents a practical and cost-effective option for supplementing water supplies in South Tarawa. Desalination is also considered an innovative and climate-adaptive technology, given its capacity to improve resilience to water quality degradation and drought conditions. The first project of this type in Kiribati was the development of a seawater reverse osmosis desalination project in another densely populated atoll—Ebevei, in the Marshall Islands—which has been applied in the development of STWSP (South Tarawa Water Supply Project) to provide water for domestic and municipal uses, including sewerage, precluding municipal-scale wastewater recycling as a viable potable water supply option on the island.

Energy consumption is one of the critical factors in determining the cost-effectiveness of incorporating desalinated seawater into the water supply network. The energy consumption per unit volume of water produced depends directly upon the plant. The use of saltwater wells on Ebevei, as opposed to an open intake or beach well which are typically used in desalination plants, has resulted in a significant reduction in energy consumption. In addition, the seawater from the saltwater wells has been naturally filtered to remove suspended solids and also diluted with rainwater, resulting in a total dissolved solid (TDS) content of 10–25 mg/l. The results of these studies have shown that for every 100 m³/day of water produced by the plant, energy consumption is directly proportional to feedwater TDS; the resultant energy consumption per unit volume of water produced has been found to be approximately 3.5 kWh/m³.

Given that energy consumption of reverse osmosis is directly proportional to feedwater TDS, the resultant energy consumption per unit volume of water produced by the plant will be approximately 3.5 kWh/m³. This means that if saltwater from the wells has been naturally filtered, no pretreatment of water prior to the RO process is necessary (typically desalination plants will include a process such as Dissolved Air Flotation [DAF] or microfiltration as a pretreatment prior to the RO).

STWSP has also been designed to draw feedwater from wells below the atoll. Like Ebevei, the desalination plant will likely not require a pretreatment system (resulting in CAPEX and O&amp;E savings). This will be confirmed following the results of the pilot project on Ebevei.

The energy consumption per unit volume of water produced by the STWSP is estimated to be 3.5 kWh/m³. In addition, the energy consumption of the desalination plant in South Tarawa is estimated to be 3.5 kWh/m³, lower than most plants in the region (which typically range from 4–6 kWh/m³). The plant will be designed for flexibility based on individual RO trains and multiple borehole pumps, which means that production can be easily increased decreased based upon demand. In addition, the plant has been designed to allow for future expansion of the water supply network; the plant has also been designed to facilitate future expansion (for example by allowing additional space and sizing power generation equipment). The plant will have a 20-year design life and is fully pre-funded and will allow PUS to pursue further expansion in the coming decades with minimal service interruption.

The plant will have its energy consumption offset by solar PV. A recentIRENA study concluded that solar photovoltaic-powered seawater desalination plants are cost-effective for providing water supply in South Tarawa. The project will be the first desalination project at-scale in the Pacific with renewable energy offset.

The current high rate of water losses in South Tarawa is another consideration in introducing a relatively expensive water supply network. Water losses have been estimated to be 30–40% and are currently only introduced into the network zones that have been rehabilitated. The desalination plant will be located in Betio, the most densely populated area on Tarawa (population around 17,200), and with the highest rate of water loss due to disease. Prior projects will be planned to reduce water losses by improving the quality of water supplies to this area.

The project includes performance-based contracting for both the desalination plant and the network. Particularly in a development context, PBCs are a tool of emerging importance for supporting sustainable operational improvements through private sector engagement. For the desalination plant, the firm, cost-outourced O&amp;M contract could include performance-based targets on (i) water production; (ii) energy consumption; and (iii) timing of membrane replacement, etc. For the water supply network, the PBCs will be based upon performance-based targets for (i) quality of supplied water; (ii) quantity of supplied water; and (iii) energy efficiency, and these combined will help to achieve the project's mitigation benefits.

# Methodology

The economic analysis of the proposed project was carried out in accordance with ADB’s guidelines for conducting the economic analysis of investment projects. The economic efficiency of the investment was determined by comparing the economic net present value (ENPV) using a discount rate of 9% and internal rate of return (IRR).

For the economic analysis, the domestic currency numbers has been adopted. Taxes and duties have been removed from the cost estimates, which include all physical work, supply, international and local labor. Cost estimates reflect end-2017 prices and are held constant throughout the period of analysis (to 2041), incorporating a 3.5-year implementation period. The exchange rate used is the official exchange rate for the year 2016. To convert to the currency of the foreign cost estimates, the shadow exchange rate factor is estimated to be 1.0. Due to high unemployment in Kiribati and in the capital city, a shadow wage ratio factor of 0.8 is applied to the estimated unsalaried local labor inputs.

As is common when undertaking the economic analysis of investment projects, numerous assumptions were used to develop the cost estimates for the project. The following are some of these assumptions: Conservative assumptions were adopted where assumptions were necessary, to avoid over-estimating the expected benefits of the project. This is possible that the analysis under-estimated the true economic benefits of the project.

## Outcome of the economic analysis

The cost estimates and benefits are presented in Section B and the benefits in Section E.3.1. The project IRR is estimated to be 13.0%, comfortably above the 9.0% threshold. The ENPV of the project calculated at the 9.0% discount rate is $11.8 million.

Sensitivity analyses showed the economic efficiency of the project to be robust in the face of increased costs or decreased benefits. When all capital and O&M costs are increased by 20%, the ENPV remains positive at $0.5 million and the IRR drops from 13% to 9%. Similarly, when all benefits (both direct and indirect) are reduced by 20%, the ENPV is $-817.6 million and the IRR falls below the ADB social discount rate of 5.1%.

The project appears most sensitive to changes in savings on fuel costs related to transportation, but estimates show that any increase or decrease in these savings will not significantly affect the economic efficiency of the project.

Sensitivity analyses also showed that the project is robust against increases in the cost of health care and treatment costs (i.e. productivity losses, and fatalities from water-borne diseases) by 20.0% cuts the ENPV to $-817.6 million and the IRR drops below the ADB social discount rate of 5.1%.

It is important to note that the preceding analysis excludes any economic valuation of consumers’ willingness-to-pay for safe drinking water, which could be significant in a developing country such as Kiribati. This is because the project scenario is resulting in high incidences of waterborne diseases. To illustrate, valuing willingness to pay at a “willingness-to-pay” price of $0.50 per liter (as suggested by Annex B) projects the project’s estimated ENPV is $19.6 million, with an ENPV of $20.5 million. Depending on the results of a survey assessing how purely these benefits will be properly valued and included in the economic appraisal of the project.

# H.2 Arrangements for Monitoring, Reporting and Evaluation

To be able to measure the midterm performance (the CO₂ emissions reduced or avoided) of this project, a clear Monitoring, Reporting and Evaluation (MRE) framework is required. The MRE will be implemented from the first disbursement. The monitoring and reporting report will be submitted on annually as part of the annual performance report by the ADB to the GCF.

## Monitoring

Monitoring and reporting for the project more generally will follow ADB’s Evaluation Policy. The Government is familiar with ADB’s processes with respect to M&E and reporting given the ongoing STSIP. The PMI will be responsible for monitoring and reporting. The MRE will prepare biannual reports on progress, on the current level for all indicators, on the implementation of corrective actions, and on any other relevant information as a focal point for monitoring and evaluation (M&E).

Overall responsibility for day-to-day project monitoring and implementation lies with the project manager. The project manager is responsible to ensure that the project is implemented in accordance with the approved project document and to ensure the efficient implementation of the project. The project manager will inform the implementing agency and the ADB of any delays or difficulties during implementation, including the reasons for such delays or difficulties.

The project manager will ensure that all project staff maintain a high level of transparency, responsibility and accountability in monitoring and reporting on progress against the approved project document and to ensure that they are fully engaged in the program.

Monitoring activities will take place as follows:

- A detailed project administration manual (PAM) is under preparation setting out the reporting, monitoring and evaluation activities, responsibilities, and budget.
- ADB will conduct a mid-term review every two years per to assess progress of project implementation activities, compliance with contracts and project agreements, and to monitor progress in achieving project outputs and sign-off on deliverables.
- ADB will undertake a midway review through project implementation or at any time that ADB and the Government consider it necessary. The midterm review mission (i) review institutional, administrative, organizational, technical, financial and other aspects of the project; (ii) assess the progress made in implementing the project and risks included in the design and monitoring framework; (iii) review covenants to assess whether they are still relevant or need to be revised; (iv) review the performance of the executing agency and the project manager to reformulate the project and the effects of this on the immediate objectives (purpose) and long-term goals of the project. The mid-term review will also determine if any changes in the project design are necessary, whether it is necessary or its immediate objectives will change.
- Project completion report will be prepared based on data arising basis ADB at ADB HQ (Manila) and the South Pacific Subregional Office (SPSO), in Suva, Fiji).
- With respect to the project completion report, MFEI will submit a project completion report to ADB.

Throughout the life of the project, MFEI as executing entity, will provide ADB with (i) quarterly progress reports in a format consistent with ADB’s project performance reporting system and (ii) consolidated annual reports including (a) progress achieved by output as measured through the indicator's performance targets; (b) key implementation issues and challenges encountered during the year; (c) corrective actions taken; and (d) updated implementation plan for next 12 months.
