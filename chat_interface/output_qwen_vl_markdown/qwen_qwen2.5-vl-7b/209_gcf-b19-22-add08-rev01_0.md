## A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

1. The Republic of the Marshall Islands (RMI) is one of the world's smallest, most isolated, and low-lying nations in the Pacific Ocean. The project will be co-funded by the Green Climate Fund (GCF) and International Development Association (IDA), to provide transformational change to RMI by supporting the government’s climate adaptation strategy. The project will strengthen resilience of communities against drought and storm surge; strengthen preparedness of its population to disaster events, and provide financial support for climate-resilient infrastructure development responsive to climate change impacts. It will also foster coastal management through innovative policies in the densely populated areas of Enewetak and Majuro. The project will draw on innovative and highly technical modeling of the RMI coastline to inform investments in coastal resilience and adaptation. This will be complemented by a focus on sustainable land use and water use in construction, which will have wider benefits for other atoll nations. In addition, the project will strengthen disaster risk management through improved early warning systems, climate-resilient infrastructure, ecosystem management and prioritization of investments in climate and disaster resilient development.

2. The second part of the project will focus on improving resilience to drought and storm surge, extreme erosion and flooding, with the added constraint of limited aggregate sources and construction market conditions. This will be achieved through a Sustainable Public Investment Framework (SPIF) at all scales: national, sub-national, and local. SPIFs are designed to address climate change impacts in a manner that is consistent across scales, extensive knowledge, and regional collaboration in disaster risk management and climate resilience.

3. The project beneficiaries include communities living in the coastal areas as well as national and local government agencies responsible for planning and implementing measures to address climate and disaster risks.

4. The main part of the project will be executed by national authorities with a technical assistance component executed by SPC, co-funded by IDA.

5. The project has been endorsed by the NDA, and is fully aligned with the National Determined Contribution (NDC) of RMI under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change Adaptation and Disaster Risk Management in RMI.

### C.1 Strategic Context

#### 11. The Pacific Island Countries (PICs) include some of the world’s smallest nations located in the world's largest ocean. PICs are among the most physically vulnerable nations in the world. They are highly exposed to adverse effects from climate change and natural hazards (including floods, droughts, tropical cyclones, sea-level rise, and tsunamis). These events have a direct impact on their people, who depend almost entirely on their economic, human, and physical environment, and impact their long-term development. Since the 1950s, PICs have experienced an average of one natural disaster per year, resulting in USD $2 billion in damages each year. This has cost the PICs around USD $4–6 billion (in nominal terms) associated damage costs (EM- DATA, 2013).

#### 12. The Republic of the Marshall Islands (RMI) is one of the world's smallest, most isolated and vulnerable nations. The country consists of 29 atolls and five isolated islands (24 of which are inhabited) with a total land mass of approximately 75 square kilometers. Its population is estimated to be around 60,000 people, or 35,000; of whom around 75% are concentrated in the urban areas (Mayeu and Ebeye).

#### RMI is an upper-middle-income country with a GDP per capita of USD $2,984. GDP has grown at an average rate of 1.2% per annum in real terms since independence in 1979. The size and remoteness of RMI increases the cost of economic activity and makes it unable to achieve economies of scale. This is reflected by the fact that RMI’s trade deficit is around USD $30 million, and fundamentally constrains the competitiveness of exports of goods and services internationally. These factors combined with a lack of natural resources, limited infrastructure, and poor transport links are key functions of Government. Exports are low, and the shallow domestic economy has brought high dependence on imports. The country’s economic vulnerability is further exacerbated by its small size and reliance on Foreign aid funds support a very large public sector that dominates the economy.

The projected sea-level rise for RMI is estimated to be around 2 m above sea level. This makes RMI particularly vulnerable to climate change, especially as sea-level rise. The country is exposed to occasional typhoons. Like other low-lying Pacific nations, its 370 km coastline (which is a home to 95% of the population) is highly susceptible to erosion and flooding. The coastal areas are already beginning to feel the effects of climate change.

RMI has experienced an average of one natural disaster per year since the mid-1980s, including recent droughts, coast hazards (e.g., wave-induced erosion and flooding linked to king tides and storm surge), tropical storms, and, to a lesser extent, typhoons. The country’s vulnerability is further exacerbated by its small size and reliance on Foreign aid funds support a very large public sector that dominates the economy.

The cost of climate change in RMI is estimated to be around 1% of GDP (% USD $3 million per year). Based on this calculation, if the next 50 years, RMI has a 50% chance of experiencing a loss exceeding USD$833 million, and a 20% chance of losing more than USD$1.7 billion.

The country’s vulnerability is further exacerbated by its small size and reliance on Foreign aid funds support a very large public sector that dominates the economy.

Estimates indicate USD $70 million of losses and damages. More detailed simulations for Ebeye done for the preparation of this document suggest that the island could be completely inundated in the event of major disasters. When also considering inundations due to storm surges and king tides, the average annual loss goes up to USD$125 million.

These estimates do not take climate change into account and thus are an underestimate in terms of expected risks to livelihoods, coastal settlements, infrastructure, ecosystems and economic stability. The impacts of coastal erosion and inundations are evident along the shorelines of both islands. The need for coastal protection along Eebye is particularly critical because of its high population density and the fact that it is a major transport hub in the Marshall Islands.

### 18. While disasters impact whole societies when they strike, the poor and vulnerable are hit the hardest. This was the case following recent disaster events in the Pacific and RMI (e.g., the aftermath of tropical storm Nangka in 2013). The poor and vulnerable have limited access to resources such as food, water, shelter, insurance, cash reserves, and alternative income sources that provide a means for them to recover quickly. There is also a lack of social safety nets and other forms of support available to the most vulnerable.

However, as the underlying causes of vulnerability to climate change risks are related to lack of human empowerment and gender equality, it is important to address these issues in order to improve resilience and agency. It is valid to assume that women are more vulnerable than men to climate and disaster risks in RMI.

### 19. Recognizing these challenges, the Government has developed strategic priorities for disaster management and climate adaptation which are spelled out in the Joint National Action Plan on Climate Change Adaptation and Disaster Risk Management (JNAP) and the National Climate Change Policy Framework. The JNAP goals for the 2014–18 period relevant to this project are:

#### i. Establishing enabling environment for improved coordination of disaster risk management/climate change adaptation in the Marshall Islands.

* Public education and awareness of effective CCA and DRM from local to national level.
* Enhanced institutional capacity at all levels for climate change and disaster risks.
* Enhanced local livelihoods and community resilience for all Marshall Islands people.
* Improved coordination between disaster risk management and climate change and disaster risks.

### 20. 21. Some climate change trends have already been observed in RMI as reported in the Climate Change in the Pacific Scientific Assessment and New Research reports.

22. Annual maximum and minimum temperatures have increased in both Majuro and Kwajalein since mid-20th century, with increasing rates consistent with global trend (below 0.12°C and 0.20°C per decade in Majuro and Kwajalein respectively).

23. Satellite data indicate the sea level has risen near RMI by about 7 mm per year since 1993 (see Figure 1). This rate is consistent with global trends, but it is important to note that this rise may be attributed partly to natural fluctuations that take place year to year or decade to decade caused by phenomena such as the El Niño Southern Oscillation.

24. Rainfall in Kwajalein shows a decreasing trend in annual and seasonal rainfall. In Majuro, since 1950, there is a decreasing trend in annual and dry season rainfall but not in wet season rainfall. A high interannual variability is observed in both islands.

25. Observed data show that the rate of increase of ocean acidification has been slowly increasing in Marshall Islands' waters.

Over the 21st century, based on the climate projections from different climate models, some of these trends are likely to continue while others might even exacerbate the threat caused by Climate change to RMI:

27. Land and ocean Temperatures are projected to continue to increase. Projections for all emissions scenarios indicate that land temperatures will rise at a rate between 0.5°C and 1.8°C per decade, with some models likely to result in a rise in the number of hot days and warm nights, and a decline in cooler weather. Models project an increase in intensity and frequency in days of extreme heat.

28. Sea level is expected to continue to rise in the Marshall Islands. By 2030, under all emissions scenarios, this rise in sea level is projected to be between 15 cm and 46 cm. Acidification of ocean will continue to increase, threatening the coral reef ecosystem.

29. Almost all global the climate models project increases in annual and seasonal mean rainfall over the Marshall Islands. Wet season increases are particularly pronounced with intensification of the West Pacific Monsoon and the Intertropical Convergence Zone. However, as with global studies, rainfall projections for RMI vary among all models show consistent results. Droughts are projected to be less frequent throughout this century. Models also show extreme rainfall days are likely to occur more often and be more intense (very high confidence).

30. The Marshall Islands is in a region where projections tend to show a decrease in typhoon frequency by the 21st century, and a decrease in the proportion of the more intense storms.

# Existing coastal protection works

34. A range of coastal protection works have been built to enclose the areas reclaimed from the sea in the most populated islands (Majuro and Eitaisy). Most of this is in urban areas. However, existing coastal protection infrastructure has not kept pace with climate change impacts. As sea levels rise and storm surges increase due to the impacts of climate change increase, the gap between the current standard of protection and the required standard will widen. This is particularly true for Majuro where a large portion of the island has been devastated the natural integrity of the coast. Nearly 100% of the high value shoreline in Majuro is already protected by seawalls and riprap boulders, but these structures are not designed to withstand climate change impacts including riprap boulders extracted from the ocean-side reef flats, engineered vertical concrete seawalls, and gabion baskets filled with rock. ### Sector Strategies and Policies

221. ## Financial

224. Primary fiduciary functions will need to be performed by each implementing agency. However, there is relatively weak fiduciary capacity in RMI. Mitigation measures acquired from a financial management (FM) consultant include: establishment of an FM unit within the PMU; development of internal controls and guidelines; recruitment of a Project Accountant to the PMU and compliance with World Bank Procurement and FM requirements.

Social and Environmental

225. Land ownership in RMI is a hierarchical, customary system enshrined by the Constitution. Land owners hold customary power which is highly respected. In theory the Government Land Acquisition Act 1986 provides for compulsory acquisition of land for public purposes with compensation at market value or payment of compensation, but in practice the Government does not compulsorily acquire land because of the respect for the rights of landowners.

Other

226. RMI is hazard-prone and disasters occur frequently. Should a significant disaster event occur during implementation of the PREP Phase I, the attention of implementing agencies could be easily diverted from their primary focus on project delivery to addressing the immediate needs of the people in various parts of the country. In order to mitigate this, a Contingency Emergency Response Component (CERC - Component 3) has been included as part of the overall disaster risk financing and insurance mechanism supported by the PREP Phase I. This should provide flexibility and minimize disruption to the Project in the event of a disaster occurring.
