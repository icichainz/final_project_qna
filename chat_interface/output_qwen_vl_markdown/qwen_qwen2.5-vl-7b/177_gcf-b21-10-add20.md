The Green Climate Fund (GCF) is a multilateral climate fund established under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). The GCF aims to address market constraints, play a catalytic role with a blended finance approach, increase climate-related investments in the Southern Hemisphere and least developed countries, and support the transition of developing countries towards low-carbon and tenor extensions to catalyze private sector climate investments. The CFF will be a first-of-its-kind application based on the Green Bank model, adapted for emerging market conditions. ## Green Bonds

South Africa's first green bond (the first on the continent) was launched by the Industrial Development in 2012—a R6bn bond for investment in clean energy infrastructure. The City of Johannesburg followed as the first local municipality to list a green bond, which was issued in July 2014 and raised R5 billion for projects such as the Green Geyser Initiative (City of Johannesburg, 2014). More recently, the City of Cape Town's R1bn Green Bond – which was launched on the JSE in June 2017 – is a green bond that aims to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and increase the city’s total water availability. The City of Cape Town green bond, which is already listed on the JSE’s bond market, was certified by SABS as meeting the Green Bond Principles (GBP) and has been assigned a BBB+ credit rating with a stable outlook.

The projects to be funded by the green bond are a mix of adaptation and mitigation initiatives and include the procurement of electric buses, energy efficiency in buildings and water management initiatives like water meter installations. The City of Cape Town also plans to use proceeds from the bond for the upgrade of reservoirs. The City raised R1 billion for these projects when it issued the 10-year bond in July 2017.

The Climate Finance Facility (CFF) Programme implemented by DBSA will serve South Africa and the broader Southern African region and is tasked with financing low-carbon and climate resilient infrastructure projects in partnership with private lenders to catalyze greater overall climate-related investment. The CFF will focus on projects that are commercially viable but lack access to finance due to their small scale or high risk.

The CFF will finance clean energy projects, water projects that have climate benefits, and other revenue-generating climate-smart infrastructure projects in South Africa. The CFF will also support the DBSA’s broader portfolio of green project support across Southern Africa. This effort will also support the DBSA's broad goal of 'greening' the entire organization.

By design, the CFF is intended to incentivize the private sector to scale up private investment in low carbon energy and clean water infrastructure. The CFF will be operated based on Green Bank principles, as a public-purpose finance institution dedicated to mobilizing market gaps by leveraging public and private resources. The CFF’s innovative financing model will help fill the current investment shortfall into South Africa's NDCs and provide adequate supplies of sustainable clean drinking water. The CFF will be designed to maximize total investment, using limited public funds to leverage a much larger amount of private sector capital. The CFF will aim for an overall leverage ratio (L) such that L is equal to or greater than 1.5 (project leverage ratios will vary within this range). That is, if every R1 invested by the DBSA in the CFF results in R2 being mobilized from the private sector.

The DBSA will create the CFF using best practices at existing Green Banks that have successfully catalyzed markets using innovative financing models. The CFF will be structured as a separate legal entity and its investments will be supported by several key elements designed to ensure that private capital is crowed into desired markets (vs. provided directly). These include:

* **Investment Criteria –** CFF investment criteria will focus on selecting and creating transaction structures that maximize leverage ratios, while ensuring that the DBSA’s limited resources also create deals that would be unable to access financing absent CFF's involvement in the transaction.
* **Metrics –** The CFF will track total project costs enabled by CFF (Cumulative ZAR) and total committed funds from all investors (Cumulative USD).
* **Reporting –** The CFF will provide quarterly reports on the private mobilizing ratio of total project costs to CFF Funds

# D.1. Value Added for GCF Involvement

Please specify why the GCF involvement is critical for the project programme, in consideration of other alternatives.

As highlighted earlier, South Africa has made substantial commitments to responsibility address climate change. Achieving these will require collaboration across public and private sector, and innovative funding channels to scale high-impact projects that can deliver on this commitment. The GCF can play a key role by providing catalytic support for the private sector, lenders and support climate mitigation/adaptation interventions across the target sectors on a significant scale.

The GCF funding will enable GCF to overcome the following key barriers:

* **Low cost capital:** Access to financing at low interest rates is one of the biggest obstacles to MSEMs in capital formation. With access to concessional finance, we can reduce cost of borrowing for MSEMs. Lower financing costs (and longer tenors) help facilitate lower end-user prices (e.g. PFAAs) that are more attractive to customers, spurring increased uptake.
* **Private funding:** By blending the GCF funding with private investor funding, we will enable private funders to lower their cost of capital and reduce risk. This will make it possible for commercial banks to participate in projects without GCF participation.

The GCF will complement existing climate-friendly finance capacity by adding an institution specifically focused on scaling up investment in small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) for renewable energy production through off-grid, micro-grid and IPP via engaging independent businesses. This will be additive to existing energy policy in South Africa (affecting the entire country), but will also provide a more targeted approach to financing projects that are not well served by a stated need of the region's commercial banks to reduce risk and make climate-friendly projects more bankable.

Existing energy policy and investment in South Africa has focused on implementing the Integrated Resource Plan. South Africa is currently in the process of developing a new Integrated Resource Plan (IRP) for 2014-2036, which will include contracts with Eskom for large scale renewable energy generation. Beyond the recent changes to the IRPP due to the Renewable Energy Independent Power Producer Procurement Programme (REIPPPP), there is limited capacity around scaling up investment in smaller projects and independent power production for distributed generation. These issues affect the broader Southern African region, as Eskom is the largest member of the SAPC, and has a strong influence on the South African market.

Many of the climate mitigation and adaptation components emerging in Southern Africa are starting to promote new entrants into these banks regard as venture and development capital in nature. There is limited equity available from project sponsors and developers, and given the perception of these businesses as risky venture start-ups, commercial banks are not inclined to provide funding for such projects. This is particularly true when it comes to projects with a 5-10 year horizon.

The GCF will address these market barriers by playing a catalytic role by a blended finance approach with a focus on providing concessional financing and technical assistance to support the development of small-scale climate related financial or investment support. This focus will include resilience and adaptation projects such as local waste management, water recycling and energy efficiency in buildings.

Key added value interventions to complement the role that commercial banks can already play will include: longer tenor loans; first loss and subordinated tranches; project aggregation for smaller projects so they can better access concessional funding; co-financing and project aggregation; guarantees; and providing a dedicated, focused and efficient public financing entity.

GCF support for this initiative will therefore be quite transformative in releasing significant amount of funds from commercial banks to support mitigation and adaptation projects mostly spearheaded by emerging companies.

## The Climate Finance Facility and Green Bank Model as a Replicable Model for Emerging Economies

National Green Banks—structured either as new purpose-built institutions or adaptations of existing institutions—can help reduce barriers to private sector investment by addressing market gaps and drive public and private climate finance investment. Green Banks are typically not banks, but rather public development finance institutions that are dedicated to green investment, embodying the pure focus and local-market-oriented approach needed to help local private investors bridge the gap between their risk tolerance and the scale of projects they can invest in. By leveraging innovative finance and market development solutions, Green Banks address barriers that currently restrict clean energy market growth.

The strategy notes that there are no local exceptions to extreme events (see next section).
- **Higher temperatures:** South Africa is predicted to experience a drying trend: While future climate models predict a drying trend with fairly high certainty, studies based on historical data fail to agree on whether a drying trend is already visible. ## F1. Economic and Financial Analysis

Please provide the narrative and rationale for the detailed economic and financial analysis (including the financial model, building on the assumptions provided in section B.2). Based on the above analysis, please propose a comprehensive financial justification (both qualitative and quantitative) for the concessionality that GCF provides, with a reference to the financial structure proposed in section B.2.

### Supporting Documents for Funding Proposal

The financial modeling of the CFF is based on a cash flow projection covering the period from financial closing through final maturity of the proposed GCF financing with detailed assumptions and rationale, and a sensitivity analysis of critical decision points.

The financial modeling of the CFF is intended to demonstrate how concessional GCF funding will underpin the CFF at the facility and project levels, and how it will help unlock market barriers to support low-carbon energy and water projects.

The market for sustainable infrastructure in South Africa remains mixed in risks (both real and perceived) that lead to high costs of capital. The current market conditions are characterized by a lack of liquidity, which can result in higher interest rates and negative impacts on project uptake, leading to issues such as high PPA prices and escalators relative to grid prices, or the inability to secure debt financing at all. In this context, concessional GCF funding will help unlock market barriers.

The CFF provides a level of pricing efficiency necessary to accelerate the adoption of more clean energy and water projects that are in line with South Africa’s national development priorities. The CFF is structured as a non-profit-making financial vehicle with a strong focus on concessionality, which creates an underlying financing structure that provides more risk protection to private capital providers and preserves greater savings to end consumers.

The GCF participation will help projects in the following ways:

* **Higher project viability:** Projects are more attractive for commercial investors. The CFF’s low-cost, subordinated position will help bring down the costs offered by commercial lenders (due to risk mitigation), as well as lower the final blended debt offered by projects.
* **Lack of liquidity to finalize projects:** The CFF, with its mandate to invest in climate projects that are near-bankable, with a be first-mover absorber to offer additional financing where it is needed most. Projects that need additional financing will benefit from the CFF’s ability to provide concessional funding at an earlier stage of development, before the remaining finance and round out the total debt needed at the project level.
* **Low interest rates:** The CFF can help lower interest rates for projects by providing a more stable source of funding. This will allow equity sponsors to offer higher IRRs for project equity sponsors. Equity IRRs often need to be high in recent markets in South Africa, and these improved financial terms will make it easier for equity investors to participate.
* **Longer tenor that are currently unfeasible:** The CFF will facilitate longer tenors for projects where necessary. Longer tenors can help reduce the cost of debt (by reducing interest payments over time) or reduce the total debt (and costs) to be relative lower to total costs before an energy or water upgrade. This helps projects be “cash flow positive” from day one.

The financial analysis of the CFF was developed through a bottom-up approach, wherein several expected project profile types were modeled in order to understand the impact of GCF capital on the overall economics at the project level and at the facility level. ### Subordinated Position

In this structure, the Off Debt (at which GCF's contribution is the central anchor) is structured as a subordinated position to commercial lenders. The subordinated position is necessary in order to catalyze commercial lenders' participation and to provide risk protection for commercial lenders. This can be achieved by providing them with new distributed generation business models, etc.). Fundamentally, subordination provides the risk protection commercial lenders need to participate in projects that are not creditworthy on a standalone basis but which become so when senior debt is added. The GCF's concessional participation in CFF, which will take subordinated positions, is both essential for providing this subordination and for ensuring the CFF does not expose itself beyond the senior debt.

### Program’s Expected Performance

Through the example of a solar PV project, it can be shown how the GCF concessional participation impacts a typical project. The inclusion of concessional capital in the form of more favorable financing, carry less overall risk will be more cost competitive with existing options and return lower savings to consumers. For instance, a typical solar PV project has an expected payback period of 10 years. By including GCF concessional capital into the structure, the payback period is reduced by two years.

The model exercise was conducted in collaboration with developers and commercial lenders and market analysis from a leading local NGO, points to the need for tenor extension and subordination as a critical tool for unlocking risk and increasing access to finance. Overall, the blended rates of the CFF are lower, thereby expanding the pool of assets that can be financed within the 12-year window. The lower overall cost of capital is achieved by including concessional capital into the structure and leveraging it to reduce risk for the CFF. ## Project Completion Report (PCR)

Project Completion Reporting (PCR) is a critical part of the project cycle and provides a wealth of information for reporting requirements. It serves as an internal self-evaluation mechanism which will be utilised by the GCF, to assess the performance of projects upon completion. This mechanism is, therefore, distinct from the monitoring close out report. The monitoring close out reports provide information purely on the outputs whilst the PCR is meant to detail the outputs and immediate outcomes
