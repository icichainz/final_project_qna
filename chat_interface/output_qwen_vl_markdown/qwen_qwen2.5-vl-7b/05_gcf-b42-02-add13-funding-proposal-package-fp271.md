A.21. Executive summary (max 750 words, approximately 1.3 pages)

India's energy transition challenge

1. India is the world's third-largest CO₂ emitter, with over 94% of its primary energy supply in 2023 still derived from fossil fuels. This heavy reliance not only drives greenhouse gas emissions but also contributes to severe air pollution and health impacts. The country faces significant economic and social vulnerabilities as climate impacts intensify, including more frequent heatwaves and grid disruptions, fossil fuel dependence, and the need for energy access in underserved regions.

2. India's clean energy transition is at a critical juncture. While it has committed to 50% of non-fossil energy capacity by 2030 and net-zero emissions by 2070. With significant strides being made in scaling utility-scale solar and wind, the country's climate goals demand much broader and deeper decarbonization across multiple sectors. India now faces a challenge in ensuring that its energy transition is inclusive of all regions, including rural areas, agriculture and transportation – and which comprise nearly two-thirds of the country’s energy, and underserved regions which have not yet benefited from the growth in renewables.

India’s energy transition will not continue at the price needed without technologies critical to deep decarbonization and electrification such as **Brown-Beck (BC)**, carbon capture storage and utilization technologies (CCS), decentralized renewable energy (DRE), and electric mobility. As India's share of variable renewable energy (VRE) grows, new challenges have emerged—grid curtailment, weak transmission, and limited storage have constrained the integration of VRE. To address these issues, a suite of complementary solutions has been identified, along with green hydrogen and compressed biogas (CGB), are essential for industrial decarbonization but remain underdeveloped in India. The decentralized renewable energy transition is also lagging behind due to the lack of enabling decentralized level technologies like solar-powered irrigation and cold storage are vital for rural electrification and climate resilience. However, these technologies are not yet widely deployed across India’s vast geography. Moreover, a lack of centralized financial products to support small-scale developers (SMMEs). Likewise, the electric mobility transition is slow in rural areas due to high upfront costs, poor charging infrastructure, and constrained consumer finance.

The high initial cost of technologies, long-term loss in use experience, high risk premiums, and borrowers that cannot fulfill collateral or need credit enhancement, together also make the above solutions “hard-to-finance” by traditional financial institutions. The lack of a robust enabling environment is further exacerbated by the absence of affordable, long-term, and risk-tolerant capital. Financial institutions remain focused on low-risk, utility-scale renewables, leaving smaller borrowers and emerging technologies underserved. To close this gap and accelerate India’s energy transition, innovative financing solutions are required that can provide flexible capacity-building are essential. The India Green Finance Facility (IGFF) is designed to deliver precisely this shift—bridging the gap between the demand for climate action and the supply of finance.

Without strategic intervention, India risks being locked into a carbon-intensive trajectory with rising energy demand outpacing clean energy deployment. By 2030, the country’s energy demand is expected to increase by nearly 50%, which will require an additional 17 GW of power generation beyond their current capacity. This poses to play a pivotal role in the global clean energy transition. Building on the momentum of earlier international commitments, India's COP29 commitments focus on accelerating green hydrogen deployment, expanding energy access and electrification, and grid resilience to support large-scale renewable integration.

## Proposed Intervention

### IGFF, proposed by the Asian Development Bank (ADB) in partnership with India’s leading development finance institutions (DFIs), seeks to catalyze a systemic shift in this landscape. It will leverage $200 million in concessional financing from ADB to leverage $1.9 billion from ADB and leading Indian development finance institutions (DFIs). The IGFF is designed to provide flexible, long-term capital for the deployment of climate solutions. The program aims to accelerate low-carbon development, enhance energy access, and mainstream climate-aligned financial practices.

### The facility will deploy GCF resources in conjunction with ADB financing to extend lines of credit to leading Development Finance Institutions (DFIs) such as NABARD, REI, and IFCL. These funds will be directed towards the accelerated deployment of high-impact clean energy solutions, including:

* **Hydrogen production:** The IGFF will provide reliable, 24/7 clean power.
* **Decentralized solar energy applications to enhance rural livelihoods and climate resilience.
* **Electric mobility for two-and three-wheelers' targeting rural and peri-urban areas to support low-carbon transport.
* **Green hydrogen production for industrial use, enabling the decarbonization of hard-to-abate sectors; and
* **CGB for sustainable waste management.

### A $5 billion risk sharing facility (RSF) will be established under the IGFF to provide partial credit guarantees and promote financing for MME developers for the deployment of CCS. Additionally, technical assistance will be provided to build capacity for DFIs, MME developers, and other financial institutions to institutionalize green lending practices within their operations.

8. Key highlights of IGFF include:
   - Targeting underserved technologies and geographies: IGFF is uniquely positioned to direct capital towards innovative technologies that remain underfunded despite their mitigation potential—such as RTC renewables, green hydrogen, CBG, decentralized renewables, and 5-Maturity. It also prioritizes investments in the energy sector.
   - Empowering DFIs as climate finance leaders: Anchored in global recognition of development banks' pivotal role in scaling up clean energy technologies, IGFF leverages the long-term investment and financial capacities of DFIs. It equips them with long-term, low-cost capital and practical tools to embed climate considerations into their lending operations, enabling them to take leadership in financing India's energy transition.
   - Deploying tailored, catalytic financial instruments: The facility combines concessional lending with a risk-sharing mechanism for high-risk projects, such as green hydrogen. IGFF also provides guarantees on market-based schemes like CBG. These instruments are designed to reduce capital costs, address access credit constraints, and unlock private sector participation.
   - Mobilizing large-scale private and public capital: By de-risking investments and demonstrating commercially viable models, IGFF is expected to crowd in over USD 2.8 billion in total investments. This multiplies the impact of its concessional lending by leveraging a larger pool of resources for scaling up clean energy markets.
   - Institutionalizing green finance practices: IGFF mainstreams climate risk management within India's financial architecture by developing green lending taxonomy, credit appraisal frameworks, and disclosure tools across all DFIs. The facility also fosters capacity building among DFIs to ensure a systemic shift is consistent with the global agenda led by the Network for Greening the Financial System (NGFS), which emphasizes steering capital toward sustainable outcomes and managing climate-related financial risks.

### Climate Results/Benefits

9. IGFF is designed to deliver transformative climate impacts by addressing mitigation needs and supporting adaptation as a co-benefit. A key outcome of the facility is the mitigation of greenhouse gas emissions, with support for 836 MWe of new renewable energy capacity expected to reduce 50 million metric tons of CO2 over its lifetime. By reducing reliance on fossil fuels, IGFF will help India achieve its climate goals and transition away from a heavy reliance on imported fossil fuels and stabilizing power supply for critical sectors. Beyond environmental benefits, IGFF is also expected to yield significant economic and social benefits by improving air quality, reducing hospital admissions due to respiratory diseases, ultimately lowering healthcare costs. Furthermore, IGFF will help build internal systems and capabilities in DFIs that can be scaled up across the financial sector. By promoting a green finance culture within India's financial system, IGFF is expected to accelerate the scaling of five emerging clean energy technologies, enabling economies of scale that drive cost reductions and enhance long-term commercial viability.

### Paradigm Shift

10. Catalyzing Indian Energy Finance Transformation: IGFF is designed to address deep-rooted structural barriers in India’s clean energy finance landscape by introducing catalytic financial instruments such as concessional aligned-principal loans, risk-sharing mechanisms for high-risk projects, and a dedicated RFS program. IGFF unlocks private sector financing for high-risk sectors like RTC renewables, green hydrogen, and MSME-scale CBG. Transitioning from fossil fuels to clean energy technologies will help India meet its climate goals while promoting climate-aligned investments, institutionalizing green lending and setting the stage for long-term capital flows. By aligning with NGFS principles and incorporating sustainability reporting (carbon footprint), IGFF helps embed sustainability into the core of India's financial system.

#### B.1 Climate context (max. 1000 words, approximately two pages)

**Emissions context for the energy transition**

India's rapid economic growth has led to a substantial increase in greenhouse gas emissions, with net emissions nearly doubling between 2005 and 2019 across all sectors except the energy sector. Between 1994 and 2020, total CO₂e emissions rose by 144%, while net emissions, including the land use, land use change, and forestry sector as a carbon sink, increased by 98%. Between 2015 and 2019, India accounted for only 4% of global emissions, and its per capita emissions, at 2.48 tCO₂e per person, were the lowest among major emitting countries in this period.

Within this context, electricity production alone accounts for 40% of national emissions, making it the highest-emitting sector globally. Despite India's rapid economic growth, India's historical per capita emissions are among the lowest globally, highlighting its minimal contribution to climate change.

To achieve the target, currently installed capacity and capacity under construction would need to more than double over the next five years.

## B.2 Project/programme description (max. 2500 words, approximately 5 pages)

### Overview
57. The IGFF is a blended finance facility designed to accelerate India's energy transition by targeted financing to scale up emerging clean energy technologies in the country. It leverages concessional capital from the GCF, complemented by co-financing from ADB and other DFIs. The IGFF will provide concessional loans for these technologies. Additionally, the IGFF will deploy technical assistance to strengthen project pipelines, address market barriers, and promote best practices in climate-related financial disclosure frameworks while advancing gender-responsive financing practices to promote women's empowerment.

58. For the purposes of the IGFF, a subproject is defined as an ADB-financed operation that will provide financial intermediary loans to one or more DFIs. Such bases include those with GCF resources (GCF loans) and with ADB resources (ADB loans). Each subproject will enable DFIs to provide loans (DFI loans) or de-risking support for investments in eligible clean energy technologies. Subprojects may be implemented using either a single technology or multiple technologies. Each subproject will be presented for approval to the ADB Board and/or other applicable ADB approving authorities on a transaction-by-transaction basis.

### 73. The RSF structure includes the following parameters:

- **Total project investment:** US$57 million.[1]

  - **Coverage:** The facility is designed to cover 50% of payment defaults (covering both principal and interest on a pari-passu basis). This is lower than typical MSME guarantee schemes in India (which often cover 60–85%) but is justified by the fact that the RSF will be operational for only five years.
- **Term and duration:** Each credit guarantee will have a 12-year term. This long duration accommodates the high-risk nature of MSME lending, which typically has shorter terms (e.g., three to six months). The first year is for grace period and expiry.
- **Overall RSF duration:** The RSF will be operational for 30 years—the first five focusing on active guarantee issuance and portfolio build-up, followed by two years of claim reimbursement and financial monitoring, and the final three years of loan repayments to CDFIs.
- **Leverage ratio:** The RSF has a leverage of with a leverage of 3, meaning the US$56 million guarantee fund can mobilize up to US$189.60 million in PFI loans, potentially supporting a total project investment of around US$57 million.

### Impacts and benefits of the RSF

- The RSF includes targeted credit enhancement and risk sharing. It specifically targets loans to MSME CSG borrowers, reducing risk for PFIs by guaranteeing 50% of each firm’s exposure on a pari-passu basis. This mechanism directly addresses the high-risk nature of MSME lending, which typically has shorter terms (e.g., three to six months). In case of defaults, losses are shared between the RSF and the PFI, ensuring that the financial burden does not fall solely on the lender.

- The RSF design builds on precedents from existing MSME-focused guarantee schemes in India and enhances these with a clear focus on CSG projects. Existing schemes such as the Credit Guarantee Fund Trust for Micro and Small Enterprises (CGFT) have been successful, but they are often underfunded and lack sufficient capacity to absorb large-scale participation. Beyond direct financial support, the RSF serves as a market-making tool. As banks gain confidence from successful transactions in lending, the overall risk perception of the MSME CSG sector is expected to decline, paving the way for additional financial incentives in the future.

B.5. Justification for GCF funding request (max. 1000 words, approximately 2 pages)

116. GCF is anchored in the urgent need to accelerate deep decarbonization across India's economy by broadening the range of clean energy solutions that can drive this transformation. As outlined in Section B.1 of the funding proposal, while India has made significant progress in scaling up renewable energy capacity, additional complementary investments are required to strengthen grid reliability, de-carbonize high-emitting sectors, and extend clean energy access.

117. Financing is also heavily skewed toward domestic sources (53%) and debt instruments (60%), with 84% of domestic financing coming from the government. 100. India’s clean energy transition is central to its commitment to achieve net-zero emissions by 2070 and reduce emissions intensity by 45% by 2030, as defined in its NDCs. Achieving these targets requires a systemic scale-up of energy infrastructure investment, including the deployment of new technologies and systems that are cost-effective and scalable to decarbonize hard-to-abate sectors and ensure energy access in underserved regions. Despite strong political will, India faces significant barriers to scaling up clean energy finance gap, requiring USD 175 billion annually and over USD 2.5 trillion by 2030 across mitigation and adaptation needs.

101. However, macroeconomic conditions significantly constrain India’s fiscal ability to meet these needs. The Government of India’s 2022–24 Union Budget estimates a fiscal deficit of about 6% of GDP, and debt-to-GDP ratio projected to exceed 95%. With limited room for further fiscal stimulus, and fuel and electricity prices at the highest level in years, there is limited fiscal space for public investment in emerging clean energy technologies. Private investment, meanwhile, has been constrained by high costs, long payback periods, and low risk-adjusted returns. The cost of capital is lower. New technologies face prohibitive financing costs and underdeveloped enabling ecosystems. Key underfunded climate change-related sectors include:

- Round-the-clock renewable energy (RTC-RE): Higher capital costs for battery storage in comparison to standard solar PV and limited long-term debt makes RTC-RE financially unviable without concessional finance.
- Hydrogen: Production costs remain at 3.5–5 per kg, while break-even is below USD 2 per kg. Existing subsidies are not sustainable or scalable.
- Compressed biogas (CBBG): High capital costs, inefficient biomass supply chains, and long payback periods limit consumer adoption.
- EV infrastructure: High financing costs, interest rates 1–9% higher than ICE vehicles, and shorter loan tenures limit consumer adoption. Adoption concentrated in urban areas. Solar pumps and DLEs Limited private financing, weak revenue streams, and high perceived risks hinder scale.

# Needs of DFIs

102. The barriers to bridging this financing gap are limited by structural lending constraints. Currently, most DFIs offer loans at interest rates ranging from 6% to 11.5%, with tenors typically capped at 15–10 years—insufficient for capital-intensive clean energy projects that require 20–25-year loan horizons. High cost of capital (7–13%) further hinders project viability, especially in the case of RTC-RE and hydrogen. DFIs are also constrained by limited access to blended finance instruments, and aligning with disclosure frameworks such as BSRIN—critical for accessing international climate finance. Lack of adequate risk management and technical support, DFI cannot scale investments in early-stage technologies or underserved geographies.

# Needs of projects proponents (private sector and MSMEs)

103. For project proponents—particularly MSMEs and small enterprises—barriers are more acute. These include high interest rates, long payback periods, and limited access to credit guarantees or project development support. Sector like CBBG, rural-mobility, and DLE remain excluded from mainstream financing due to financial constraints and lack of market demand. The sector is also characterized by low-exchangeable exclusion, as women entrepreneurs face lower financial literacy and weaker access to networks and finance.

# How IGF addresses these needs

104. IGF is structured to systematically address these barriers through a blend of financial, institutional, and regulatory support:

- 1. Blended concessional finance (USD 20–ADB GCF) covers the cost of capital, improves tenors, and enhances bankability for high-impact, low-carbon technologies, supporting the creation of USD 2.8 billion in clean energy projects.
- 2. Risk-sharing mechanisms, such as the USD 65-million RSP for CBBG, catalyzes lending to MSMEs by offering partial credit guarantees and lowering collateral requirements, with a projected leverage ratio of over 1:3.

## F1. Risk factors and mitigations measures (max. 3 pages)

### Description of Financial Sector Risks

#### Selected Risk Factor 1 – Forex risk

| Category | Probability | Impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Financial | Medium | Medium |

**Description:**

- 199: GCF and ADB’s loan finance to the DFS is extended in hard currency (USD). DFIs will on-lend in local currency (INR) to their clients, which include SMEs and corporates. For the Risk-sharing facility, the DFIs or its subsidiary will be required to provide a 10% margin of foreign exchange risk coverage (FEC) to ADB/GCF for USD loans.
- ADF/SCF will be in USD. A deviation of INR against the USD will therefore increase DFI’s service costs due to higher interest rates and/or lower returns on investments.

**Mitigation Measure(s):**

200: For ADF, in most sovereign loan projects, the sovereign government assumes currency risks before passing on loans to projects in local currency. This also provides adequate comfort on this risk, with sovereigns better able to manage their foreign exchange exposure than DFIs. The Fund will establish a Foreign Exchange Risk Reserve Facility (FERF) to mitigate the currency risks within the RCF corpus. Each year, the FERF will accumulate up to a “maximum reserve” amount equal to 25% of the total loan commitment in local currency. This reserve fund will be adjusted for exchange rates and the amount adjusted to the base year exchange rates. The FERF will allow the DFI to capture residual cash flow from foreign exchange fluctuations, which can then be used to offset any losses that may arise if the gains are realized in the preceding year. Even in years with a foreign exchange fluctuation gain, 25% of that gain shall be transferred to the FERF in the following year. This cumulative reserve fund will effectively mitigate the foreign exchange risk associated with the receipt of local currency funds from this Facility.

## G.1 Framework for Environmental and social risk assessment (max. 750 words, approximately 1.5 pages)

### 2.1 The GFF's overall approach to Environmental and Social risk assessment is guided by the Green Climate Fund Revised Environmental and Social Policy and all other relevant GCF E&S policies and commitments that have been referenced in this document.

#### 2.1.1 Activities which are considered high-risk under the GCF’s IP Policy. Due to the nature of the proposed sectors and activities, and the level of accreditation of ADB, a category II is proposed for GFF. Activities which imply resettlement and/or other irreversible E&S impacts will be further assessed in accordance with the GCF E&S Framework.

#### 2.1.2 IGF will finance climate-mitigated projects or interventions through DFSs. For each, an assessment procedure of Environmental and Social (E&S) safeguards of the proposed projects is required and will be embedded in their investment procedures. The project proponent will be responsible for conducting a preliminary EAS check and establishing mitigation plans and processes for any adverse impacts identified. Following this, the DFS E&S unit will conduct an initial screening according to a predefined exclusion list, and a categorization procedure based on the application of policy criteria as outlined in the GCF Environmental and Social Policy (2021). The purpose of this initial screening is to ensure that the project does not fall within exclusion criteria and has no high-risk E&S impacts. This will be followed by an Environmental and Social Health Impact Assessment (ESHA) and Gender Analysis (GA) for projects with a category B or C risk under the new ADB’s Environmental and Social Framework (ESF) moving forward. Following this screening, a decision will be made, either approval for continuation, requesting clarification from the proponent, or rejection of the project.

#### 2.1.3 Projects which are considered high-risk under the GCF E&S Framework will be subject to an Environmental and Social Health Impact Assessment (ESHA) and Gender Analysis (GA). For projects with a category B or C risk under the new ADB’s ESF moving forward, an Environmental and Social Health Impact Assessment (ESHA) and Gender Analysis (GA) will be conducted. Projects which are considered high-risk under the GCF E&S Framework will also be subject to a Specialized Assessment of Indigenous Peoples’ Rights (SAIPR), as well as appropriate mitigation measures in place which will form the basis for an Environmental and Social Health Impact Assessment (ESHA). The project proponent must also conduct a Specialized Assessment of Harassment (SEAH) and indigenous peoples) as well as appropriate mitigation measures in place which will form the basis for an Environmental and Social Health Impact Assessment (ESHA).

#### 2.1.4 Projects assessed to be category A or high risk under the new ADB’s ESF starting in 2023 will be excluded.

### 2.2 Once a project has passed this initial screening stage, it will move on to a full proposal assessment stage at the DFS E&S unit. The DFS E&S unit will conduct an environmental and social impact assessment based on the characteristics of the project. If the project is category C in Environmental and Social Policy appropriate to the size and nature of the risks and activities involved in the project, along with an Environmental and Social Action Plan (ESAP), and a Gender Analysis (GA) for projects with a category B or C risk under the new ADB’s ESF moving forward. As described above, an Environmental and Social Health Impact Assessment (ESHA) to mitigate any residual impacts identified, an ESGA will be conducted. The DFS E&S unit will also conduct a Specialized Assessment of Indigenous Peoples’ Rights (SAIPR) or mitigation plans (ESEP) to mitigate any residual impacts identified following the ESHA. The ESHAS and ESEP will be subject to review by the GCF E&S team.

### 2.3 IGF, through its PUIs will then review the proposal, checking the relevant documentation created (e.g., ESSM, ESIP), perform random checks and sample screenings to confirm categorization of projects, and ensure that all proposals meet GFF requirements and are aligned with GCF objectives and priorities. Once these have been ensured, IGF will provide a final decision on whether or not the project is approved for funding. Projects will be categorized and categorized for each DFSI, and the first category B project if this does not fall within the first two sub-projects, will be subject to an Environmental and Social Health Impact Assessment (ESHA) to ensure correct categorization.

### G2. Gender assessment and action plan (max. 500 words, approximately 1 page)

#### 218. Women are critical stakeholders in India’s shift to renewable energy and require greater financial inclusion.
While women hold 32% of renewable energy jobs globally, in India, their participation is lower—for example they represent only 11% of the workforce in rooftop solar companies. Like other countries in the region, there is a growing number of female entrepreneurs in the sector, but they are still underrepresented at higher levels of decision-making, where they have the highest participation rate (21%). Despite this progress, persistent gender biases and women’s limited access to finance continue to hinder their full participation.

#### 217. According to Government of India data, female entrepreneurs own about 20% of businesses and only about 6% of tech startups have women-only founders, but only a fraction operate in the energy sector.
While data specific to renewable energy is limited, women are underrepresented at all levels of decision-making compared to men. Significant barriers include lack of collateral, bias in lending, and limited access to financial information. Female representation in the solar sector is also low; according to the Solar Energy Corporation of India (SECI), women hold about 17% of senior role management positions in the solar industry. In a survey of Indian rooftop solar firms, only one-third had female CEOs or directors, while only two out of ten had more than one woman. Women rarely head engineering, project management, or operations teams in energy companies.

#### 218. Despite challenges, women are participating in the energy transition by founding startups and social enterprises—from solar solutions companies to clean cooking ventures—but often face more significant hurdles in scaling up and accessing finance compared to men.
Women entrepreneurs have a critical role to play in increasing India’s renewable energy value chain (for example, as distributors of solar lamps or operators of local energy kiosks). The government has set an ambitious target for 50 GW of rooftop solar by 2030. The India Green Finance Facility (IGFF) is a large-scale initiative designed to accelerate India’s transition towards clean energy by mobilizing concessional funding from development finance institutions (DFIs). The programme aims to unlock funding at scale for emerging clean energy technologies and expansion into underserved areas. The programme will address these challenges by supporting DFIs in scaling up emerging clean energy technologies through the provision of concessional lines of credit for clean energy sub-sectors, including solar photovoltaic (PV), wind, small hydroelectric projects, and other borrowers to finance initiatives that promote deployment of technologies/solutions facilitating the long-term transition towards a low-carbon economy. ### ESS 3: Resource efficiency and pollution prevention

The programme is designed to contribute to energy conservation through the utilization of solid waste and agricultural residue in the production of biogas and other renewable energy sources, including small-scale hydroelectricity generation. However, some of the subprojects to be supported may also involve the generation of greenhouse gas emissions (e.g., methane from livestock farming), air emissions (e.g., ducts from construction activities) and odour and fugitive gases from compressed biogas plants and ammonia production) and wastewater and other effluents (e.g. dairy farms and agro-industrial facilities). The EMIF proposes that subprojects will implement pollution mitigation measures to ensure compliance with national and international environmental standards, as defined in the ILO’s Core Labour Standards. ### ESS 4: Community health, safety and security

The programme will exclude subprojects involving the construction of large-scale infrastructure such as power plants or other infrastructure that would require further technical assessment and safety studies as they would have significant impacts on community health and safety. The potential environmental impacts of these projects would potentially expose local communities and workers to visual exploitation, abuse and harassment, noise pollution, air emissions, water pollution, soil contamination, and the release of hazardous wastes and pollutants. This would require further studies on management, minimization and disposal of waste and other by-products in accordance with national and international environmental quality standards. The potential impacts of the programme on community health and safety would include exposure to construction site hazards, product (e.g., battery) safety hazards, noise pollution, air emissions, water pollution, soil contamination, and the design and operation of energy infrastructure. These community health and safety issues require thorough assessment and mitigation measures in accordance with national legislation for community health and safety risk assessments (e.g. construction site risks, infrastructure safety, product safety). The EMIF proposes that these environmental impacts will be addressed with mitigation measures in accordance with the mitigation hierarchy.

### Performance Risk for GCF

The AE has developed a financial model with various scenarios, including reserve capital requirements such as the composition and profile of guaranteed borrowers, recovery rates, and expected losses. It is projected that the guarantor fee will be sufficient to cover expected losses over time, provided that the model assumptions are met during implementation. The loans and guarantees will be provided in INR, while GCF disbursement and repayment will be made in USD. The GCF will also provide a Financial Performance Facility (FPF), funded by managed contributions from RGF revenues on an annual basis to mitigate the PFI risk of non-recovery of guaranteed loans. GCF relies on the RGF manager’s ability to efficiently manage the facility.

#### Risk-sharing facility structure and management

The Secretariat’s legal assessment notes that “the Accredited Entity is not currently able to complete its legal due diligence on the proposed structure of the risk sharing facility under Decision 1/CP.10.” The Secretariat further states that the design of the risk sharing facility would depend on the identity of the development finance institution (DFI) selected by the Accredited Entity.

The Secretariat notes concerns about the financial viability of some technologies, particularly RTG renewables where “the HFR [financial internal rate of return] barely exceeds the WACC [weighted average cost of capital],” and that this is based on assumptions around battery price reductions and off-take tariffs that remain unproven.

# 4.3. Gendered Disparities in Energy Access in India: Challenges and Opportunities

## 44. Energy access in India continues to exhibit significant gender disparities, particularly between rural and urban areas and among marginalized populations. Rural women bear the disproportionate burden of managing household energy needs, often spending an estimated 20–40 hours per week collecting firewood and other traditional fuels (IEA, 2021) [1]. This task is both time-consuming and physically demanding, impacting their ability to engage in income generation, education, and leisure. In urban areas, women have better access to modern energy sources such as electricity and LPG, but these disparities persist for rural populations. For instance, only 50% of households using LPG as their primary cooking fuel have consistent supply due to load barriers [2].

## 45. Despite significant progress in electrification through initiatives such as the Subhasini Scheme, women continue to rely on traditional biomass fuels in India—predominantly in rural areas—still relying on traditional biomass for cooking. The reliance on traditional fuels disproportionately affects women's health and well-being, leading to respiratory illnesses and other health issues, which result in approximately 500,000 premature deaths annually [3]. Limited access to electricity also impacts essential tasks such as lighting, cooking, and food preservation, further amplifying gender inequalities.

## 46. Men typically have greater access to financial and resources, allowing for more adaptive flexibility in energy use. In contrast, women face heightened constraints due to limited resource availability and lack of decision-making power over household energy management. Studies indicate that women own only 13% of land in India [4], which restricts their ability to secure financing for clean energy technologies such as solar home systems or biogas units.

The female literacy rate among Dalits (37%) and Adivasis (40%) remains below the national average of 65% [5]. Additionally, these women face substantial challenges in accessing medical care due to caste-based discrimination.

The clean cooking value chain presents significant opportunities for women's economic empowerment.
