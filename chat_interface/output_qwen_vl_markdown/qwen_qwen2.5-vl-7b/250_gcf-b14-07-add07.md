### A.2. Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

The Community-Based Natural Resource Management (CBNRM) program makes an enormous contribution to both ecosystem conservation and rural development in the communal areas of Namibia. Almost one fifth of Namibia's land surface is under CBNRM management, with over 150 communities participating in this program. Local institutions have developed and have gained skills and capacity, resulting in the diversification of livelihoods and the generation of economic benefits for their members. These CBNRM successes are threatened by CC impacts.

The proposed project is built on the strong institutional foundation of the Namibian CBNRM network, which consists of communal conservancies and community forests in the rural communal areas of Namibia. These existing institutions are already well established and have been working together for over 20 years to manage natural resources sustainably for the past 26 years. It is expected the project will increase the resilience towards climate change at almost 15,000 direct beneficiaries (including women) in communal conservancies and community forests.

The project will run over five years and consists of two complementary components that will empower rural CBNRM communities to adapt to climate change in terms of livelihoods, additional productivity and rural development. The two components are:

- **Component 1: Capacity Building and Community Support:** This component is essential for the access and sustainability of climate change adaptation action. It involves awareness raising, capacity building and support to development and implementation of climate investment plans at the local level.
- **Component 2: Climate Change Adaptation Investment Plans (CCAIPs):** This component will empower rural CBNRM communities to increase their resilience to climate change through direct access to climate finance for the implementation of CCAIPs. It involves capacity building, technical assistance and strengthening rural CBNRM livelihoods through grants in three defined investment areas.

C.1. Strategic Context

4. About Namibia: Namibia is situated in South-Western Africa and covers a land area of 825,418 km² with a population of approximately 2.3 million (2013 figures) and an average population density of less than 1 person/km² (source: World Bank). Namibia is the second least densely populated sovereign country in the world (after Mongolia). Namibia gained independence from South Africa on March 21, 1990. It has a GDP per capita of $5,348 (2016) and is classified by the World Bank as an upper-middle income country. Yet, Namibia faces certain unique challenges due to, among other things, its long history of colonialism, political instability, and a severe income gap illustrated by a Gini Coefficient of 0.53 – one of the highest worldwide (UNDP, 2014).

5. Namibia's climate: Namibia is the driest country in Sub-Saharan Africa. The median annual rainfall ranges from less than 50 mm to 250 mm in Namibia’s hyper-and southwestern coastline and peak at 350 to 500 mm in the sub-humid north-eastern parts of the country (Barnes, 1987). Most of the country is classified as semi-arid or arid (i.e. the sub-humid) (Mendelssohn, James, Roberts, & Robertson, 2002). Over most of the country, potential evaporation is at least five times greater than precipitation and rainfall is highly variable from year to year. This results in frequent and severe droughts, unpredictable and variable rainfall patterns, variability in temperatures and scarcity of water (GRN, 2011), lack of surface storage capacity for rainwater, and a high degree of vulnerability to climate change across the country’s borders. Of the total rainfall, 63% evaporates shortly after precipitation and 17% is available as surface runoff which of which only about 5% can be used by humans (GRN, 2011). The remaining 10% is lost through evaporation or seepage into the soil. Hence, only 2% (i.e. 5 mm) remains to be harnessed in surface storage facilities (FAO). Namibia is one of the world’s most arid countries and has a very low rainfall potential. The country's climate is characterized by high evaporation combined with a limited adaptive capacity (Brown, 2009). Approximately 70% of Namibia’s population live in rural areas where water scarcity is a major problem. The majority of the land area is characterized by low production risk due to adverse conditions, infertile soils and generally poor land use practices.

6. Conservation in Namibia: In total, almost 44% of Namibia's land area is under some form of conservation (NACSO, 2013). The largest proportion of this area is under the jurisdiction of the Ministry of Environment and Tourism. This area is under conservation through the Community-Based Natural Resource Management (CBNRM) Program (see Figure 4.1). CBNRM is a land use system that allows communities to manage their natural resources on behalf of about 200,000 residents. Of this area, conservancies manages 160,244km² which is about 19.4% of Namibia. Although conservation areas are managed by the Ministry of Environment and Tourism, they are owned by communities. Currently comprises 82 Communal Conservancies and 32 Community Forests on communal land. CBNRM is a conservation and development strategy premised on an incentive-driven philosophy that recognizes how conservation can be used as a tool for poverty alleviation (NACSO, 2013). The premise of the CBNRM model is that communities with responsibility and authority over natural resources tend to manage them sustainably in ways that also lead to reduction in rural poverty when they are in control of those resources and derive direct benefits arising from their use.

### 5. Climate change and CBNRM:
The majority of CBNRM income is directly linked to natural resource utilization, through wildlife tourism, forest products, and other activities that are affected by climate change. This can have direct or indirect impacts on the tourism resource base, through changes in habitat, landscape characteristics and vegetation cover; biodiversity loss, decreased water availability, increased frequency of extreme weather events such as droughts and increases in flooding, increased spread of vector-borne diseases. The efficiencies of the CBNRM program in conservancies and forest reserves, are those developing sustainable livelihoods based on resource management and tourism, stand to be severely impacted by climate change. This is because these communities depend heavily upon their natural resources for ecosystem services and goods that CBNRM provides to support livelihoods of more than 200,000 Namibians who derive income from wildlife and forest products. The National Climate Change Strategy (NCCS) (Ministry of Environment and Tourism, 2008) identifies CBNRM as an important Program that offers an opportunity for communities to diversify their livelihoods in response to climate change, while also providing a platform on which to integrate adaptation and mitigation strategies when responding to other environmental or socio-economic changes.

### 5.1 CBNRM Policies: Namibia, to date, has implemented several conservation policies aimed at delineating sustainable natural resource utilization areas. The first such policy was the Wildlife Act of 1963. The idea of a specific CBNRM support structure emerged in the early 1990s through the work of the CBNRM support organizations, the Ministry of Environment and Tourism (MET), the Ministry of Agriculture, Water and Forestry (MAWF) and the Living Planet Foundation (LIFE) project. International support was received through USAID, DRC, SIDA, WWF, GEF and World Bank. The objective was to "promote activities that demonstrate that sustainably managed natural resources can result in economic benefits for rural communities, while contributing to biodiversity conservation and sustainable government." This has certainly been demonstrated and the program has been successfully mainstreamed into national development plans such as the National Poverty Reduction Strategy (NPRS), Rural Development Strategy, National Development Plans, Harare Treaty Programme Plan and the Community Based Natural Resource Management Act of 1992. The legal provision enabling the implementation of CBNRM is fragmented and embedded in the legislative frameworks of various government ministries. The suggestion most relevant to this study is Nature Conservation Amendment Act of 1986 (Act No. 30) which provides for the establishment of Community Based Natural Resource Management Areas (CBNRMAs). These areas are managed by a Community Board, which is responsible for the management and conservation of natural resources within its area. Any Community Forest goes hand in hand with the registration of a corresponding legal institution in form of a Community-Based Natural Resource Management (CBNRM) Scheme (CBS), a term used throughout this funding proposal when referring to these CBNRM legal entities.

### 5.2 Project/ Programme Objective against Baseline
The CBNRM network comprises about 200,000 residents in 82 Communal Conservancies and 32 Community Forests. The majority of the conservancies are located within the Namib Desert and the Kunene Region. Communal conservances and forest reserves operate exclusively in rural settings in Namibia. This renders them particularly vulnerable to the effects of a changing climate as they are highly dependent on variable rainfall patterns associated with natural resource utilization. In addition, CBNRM communities have very limited access to the financial, technical and human resources required to enhance their resilience to climate change.

- Drought and low rainfall: More than 80% of people living within the CBNRM boundaries depend on natural resource use for their livelihoods as subsistence farmers, small-scale emerging farmers and horticultural farmers. These communities have been identified as having the highest levels of exposure because drought increases in frequency and intensity. Unsuitable supply of forest products and services. Decrease in employment and foreign exchange earnings through forest-based industries and trade.

- Inadequate access to climate information: This is a serious concern for CBNRM communities who depend on local knowledge and experience to manage their resources effectively, especially during extreme weather events. This is especially true as residents incur the direct costs of damage from increased extreme weather events.

- High temperatures: Extreme high temperatures over the past 15 years have been associated with the outbreak of certain diseases such as malaria, Rift Valley fever, and dengue fever; heat stress in livestock; and outbreaks of mouth disease, anthrax, and rabies. These diseases have profound impact on both livestock production and wildlife populations resulting in loss of revenue to support livelihoods. Livestock are more sensitive to high temperatures than small-scale farmers because they will not be able to seek shade or lie down for long hours in their fields if it is too hot. Small-scale farmers are less sensitive because they do not directly depend on livestock production.

- Biodiversity loss and species movement: There is indication that recent and ongoing climate change is leading to rapid changes in the distributions of species in Namibia's protected area network. Species movements into areas outside their historical range have been observed, including some species moving into areas where they once were, or a shift in the abundance and distribution of individuals within a species range (McGeary et al., 2010). Changes in fire and precipitation regimes continue to drive or accelerate shifts from one ecosystem state to another. The impacts of climate change on wildlife populations are likely to be more severe than those seeking to explore other habitats that offer more substantial populations and that have an impact on local livelihoods. The impacts of climate change will also lead to a loss in the capacity of plants and trees as primary producers to provide ecosystem goods and services, and increase levels of pressure on water resources as a critical natural resource for their livelihoods.

At the same time, CBNRM have been identified as critical institutions for local-level awareness and practical climate change adaptation strategies. The Namibian CBNRM program has therefore formed a solid basis for widespread community-based climate change responses at the local level in terms of knowledge, skills, and capacity building for creating and sustaining rural livelihoods while contributing to Namibia's mitigation and adaptation targets.

### Component I: Capacity Building and Community Support

#### Component I Objective:
**Build and Strengthen the Institutional Foundation for Effective and Sustainable Community-Led Local Climate Action in the CBNRM Network**

Component I of the project will build and strengthen the institutional foundation for effective and sustainable community-led local climate action in the CBNRM network. Activities under this component do not necessarily relate directly to immediate climate change impacts or adaptation, but they are required and need to be addressed to ensure the effective implementation of the proposed interventions and to enhance the adaptive capacity of the envisaged impacts under project component II.

Component I activities fall under the following sub-components:

1. **Climate change awareness creation**
   - A wide range of stakeholders will be targeted through awareness materials and dedicated materials in order to raise their awareness towards climate change and share information on possible adaptation measures.
2. **Local-level climate monitoring system**
   - A Local-level Climate Monitoring System tailored-made for CBNRM CBGs will be developed and implemented across the CBNRM network. A minimum of 30 CBNRM CBGs is expected to adopt the climate monitoring system. This will allow the CBGs to collect data on their own, with external support organizations to strengthen planning and adapt their activities and interventions accordingly.
3. **Strengthening CBGNRM governance**
   - Good governance is essential for effective resource management, benefit capture and distribution and is essential for successful implementation of community-based projects as provided under component II. Governance strengthening will be achieved through this sub-component.
4. **Capacity building for community-led initiatives**
   - Community-led initiatives to address climate issues are going to require capacity-building of a new set of skills that they have not necessarily had in the past. This includes, but is not limited to, how CBGs can be engaged in new aspects of environmental management – climate change adaptation measures – but they will also be required to identify appropriate interventions and develop and submit corresponding proposals under component II.

This sub-component will provide training and technical support to CBGs based on a prior assessment of specific needs.

Ecosystem-based adaptation is a framework that uses an integrated ecosystem approach to address the impacts of climate change on biodiversity and human well-being in the landscape scale. It combines multi-functional land uses and conservation of natural capital to enhance multi-scale benefits for people and nature while addressing the drivers of environmental change including climate change.
