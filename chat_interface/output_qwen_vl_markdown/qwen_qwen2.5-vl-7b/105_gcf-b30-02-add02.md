## A.21 Executive summary (max 750 words, approximately 1.5 pages)
1. Thailand is one of the countries most vulnerable to climate change impacts over the next 30 years. An extreme extension, one that substantially heightens vulnerability, will be experienced in the long-term future. The IPCC Fifth Assessment Report (AR5) indicates that in Southeast Asia temperatures overall have been increasing at a rate faster than the global average and are projected to continue rising. This warming is expected to lead to more frequent and intense heatwaves, with industrial levels by the end of the century, with differences between regions and microclimates within and across Southeast Asia. The frequency of extreme drought has risen due to increases in evapotranspiration combined with increases in rainfall variability (less frequent but higher intensity). This adversely impacts economic activities and livelihoods, particularly agriculture. In 2015, Thailand experienced the worst drought since 1948, which resulted in a significant reduction in rice production (an increase of precipitation is expected by 2050), as well as more extreme drought periods. In 2013, Thailand experienced its wettest year on record with over 700 mm rainfall and nearly 900 lives lost. In 2012, the World Bank reported Thailand suffered US$4.6 billion in total damages and loss of output due to flooding. The country is also vulnerable to extreme weather events such as cyclones and typhoons alone. The recent drought in 2015-2016 is estimated to have resulted in losses of US$3.8 billion. Future climate change will likely increase the frequency, magnitude and intensity of drought and extreme rainfall conditions.

## Climate impacts on agriculture will particularly affect poor households. Poverty in Thailand has a predominantly rural-urban gradient, with poverty rates of over 40% in the Northeast Region and falling agricultural prices, and droughts. In 2013, for example, 80 percent of the country's poor 7.3 million live in rural areas. The Northeast Region is also one of Thailand’s most vulnerable to climate change impacts. The Northeast and Northern Regions of Thailand have the highest levels of poverty. Sukhothai, Phitsanulok, and Uttaradit Provinces, which are part of the Northeast Region, have higher poverty rates than other provinces in the country.

### With extreme climates expected to increase, climate risk management and climate-smart water infrastructure development will be critical for reducing risks from floods and droughts. In Thailand’s National Adaptation Plan 2018, Flood Control and drought management are both highlighted as key priorities, with a focus on Chao Phraya River flood control and drought management in the Northeast Region. The project aims to complement its efforts by improving water management through strengthened infrastructure complemented by EIA measures. This will improve water availability for agriculture livelihoods and other sectors while also supporting climate change adaptation.

The project therefore seeks to adapt water management and agricultural livelihoods in the Yom River Basin to reduce climate-induced extreme weather events (droughts and floods), through interventions across three Outputs:

- **Output 1:** Enhance climate risk and make investments in the water and agriculture sectors through improved infrastructure, flood control, and coordinated coordination.
- **Output 2:** Improve water management through strengthened infrastructure complemented by EIA measures.
- **Output 3:** Reduce vulnerability of agricultural livelihoods in drought and flood prone areas through strengthened extension services, tool planning, investment on on-farm adaptation measures and greater access to finance and markets.

The project would directly benefit 60,000 people in the project site region. Indirect benefits reach 171,351 people living within a 20-km radius of the Chao Phraya River Basin. Further integration of EIA measures would have environmental benefits, while capacity building interventions of the project will support the Ministry of Agriculture and Cooperatives (MoAC) in its role as the lead agency for climate change intelligence to support climate-informed planning, precision agriculture for efficient water use and applies the internet of things (IoT) technology. The project also seeks to improve the resilience of smallholder farmers by supporting a sector towards an innovation-driven and interconnected sector. At the same time, the project also supports the MoAC in its role as the lead agency for climate change intelligence to support climate-informed planning (e.g., farm plans), small-scale equipment to support water saving farming practices (e.g. system for rice intensification (SRI) and drip irrigation), and capacity building of farmers on climate change adaptation measures, and the project will provide support to market access for products resulting from climate resilient agriculture.

UNDP and FAO currently support (through a BMU funded project) the support the Ministry of Agriculture and Cooperatives (MoAC) to enhance their sector agriculture sector adaptation planning, e.g. through an updated climate change strategy and action plan. The MoAC is implementing the Agricultural Strategic Plan on Climate Change (ASPC), 2017-2021 whereby RDT has been identified as a priority area for investment. The project will support the MoAC in its role as the lead agency functions as the regulating agency in proposing policies, formulating master plan on water resources management, recommending and implementing measures to address climate change adaptation challenges, and coordinating with other agencies in accordance with the Water Resource Management Act (2018). The CWRN has developed the 20-year Master Plan for flood control and drought management. This project will support the MoAC in addressing these problems. The Master Plan also stresses the importance of the need to bring in new ideas and technologies to address water related challenges which are exacerbated by climate change.

## B.1. Climate change and context (max. 1000 words, approximately 2 pages)

### Climate vulnerability baseline

7. Climate change is expected to change induced variations in precipitation patterns (rainfall volume and intensity) which challenge the functionality of existing water management practices through the use of existing canals and flood gates, while also increasing the risk of flooding due to increased runoff.

8. Thailand has 25 main basin systems (including 254 sub-basins). Of these river basins – there are two principal systems, the Mekong and Chao Phraya River systems. The Greater Chao Phraya River Basin consists of eight major tributaries with a total catchment area of approximately 108,367 km² and an average annual rainfall of 158,568 mm/yr with a population of approximately 25 million people. The average annual rainfall is 1.100 mm while the average annual evapotranspiration is 940 mm. The Chao Phraya River Basin has a total catchment area of 27,446 km², while the central area can store only c.38 million m³. The Chao Phraya River does not overflow its banks due to high discharges from the Yom-Nan basin.

9. The topography of the Yom-Nan river basin is part of the northern plains and there are no dams or reservoirs on an adjacent plain by Thailand. The Yom River (watershed area 23,600 km²) and Nan River (watershed area 34,300 km²) flow from the northern mountain system and join to form the Chao Phraya River at Nakhon Ratchasima. The total catchment area of these two rivers is approximately 13,000 km², built in 1974 was constructed for multiple purposes, including flood and drought protection, water supply, irrigation, navigation, and hydropower generation.

10. Thailand has experienced significant country-wide warming over the last four decades, as well as associated increases in mean temperature and extreme precipitation. The annual mean and minimum temperatures in Thailand as a whole have increased significantly by 0.86°C, 0.85°C and 1.45°C respectively over the 1955-2009 period (see Figure 1). For the same period, there has been an increase of approximately 30% in the annual number of days with maximum temperatures above 37°C. There have also been significant increases in the annual occurrence of warm spells, tropical nights, summer days, and the highest and lowest values of daily minimum temperature over this time period (see Figure 2). These trends are consistent with the fifth assessment report (AR5) from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), which projects that mean temperatures in Thailand will increase by approximately 1.0°C to 3.4°C for the next decade since the 1980s and predicts increases from 0.8°C to 3.2°C for the overall region by the end of this century.

# Water Management in the Chao Phraya River Basin

32. To cope with these additional climate-induced flood and drought risks, RID develops a 20-year roadmap for water management in the Chao Phraya River basin (see Box 1). The main components of this plan are:
- **Flood Mitigation**: In the wet season, the river is diverted to avoid flooding during the wet season and to ensure water availability during the dry season using canals and regulators, including reservoirs.
- **Drought Management**: In the dry season, the river is used for irrigation purposes by diverting a significant amount of water. In the middle section (low lying area), water is drawn into designated low-lying areas to store the water for future use.

## Project objective, outcomes, and impacts

The target communities of the proposed project are in three provinces of the northern region of Thailand: Chiang Rai, Phrae, and Uttaradit. These provinces were selected by RID under MIAC, in consultation with the Office of Natural Resources and Environment (ONE) and the Department of Water Resources (DWR), to address water management issues in Thailand.

The project's objective is to adopt water management and agricultural practices that are resilient to climate change impacts such as droughts and floods. The Fund level impacts of the project are an increased resilience of i) infrastructure and the built environment for communities, ii) livelihoods, iii) food security, iv) health and well-being, and v) food and water security.

The GCF-related outcome of the project is Strengthened climate-resilient livelihoods in the Yom and Nan river basins. The paradigm shift is to move towards climate risk informed water and land management practices that are based on local rainfall, support, and the adoption of innovative technologies and practices, such as ecosystem-based adaptation.

**116. The planned interventions target the Yom and Nan river basins in Ullaristan, Phulmokun, and Sukhotaha districts of Balochistan province. These are some of the most vulnerable areas to climate change impacts as they have a high proportion of the population living in the wider Chagai Phraya River Basin, including the Bahawalpur Metropolitan Region with around 10 million people. ### G.1. Environmental assessment and social risk assessment (max. 750 words, approximately 1.8 pages)

194. The project has the potential to cause moderate environmental and social impacts, and has been assessed as a Category B project. ### Gender policy

The accredited firm (AIF) has provided a gender assessment and a gender action plan and there is compliance with the requirements of the GGR Policy Gender Act.

The assessment indicates that Thailand has an enabling environment for women's participation in agriculture, but it also highlights some challenges such as women and men having different rights to use land, and making decisions regarding land and non-land assets. ### Activity 31: Precision Agriculture

Agriculture is increasingly moving towards precision agriculture or smart farming. This approach to agricultural production involves the use of information and communication technologies (ICTs) for the collection and analysis of data on soil conditions, crop growth, and other relevant factors. The goal is to optimize resource use by providing farmers with real-time information for the control of agricultural systems, integrating characteristics of soil, plants and climate. Through the use of these systems, algorithms executed by machines and based on parameters such as soil moisture content, temperature, humidity, etc., can be used to implement precision agriculture to address droughts, the evapotranspiration level of the specific crop, or other factors that may affect yield. In addition to the above-mentioned data, at least daily data, as against data on water availability at root zones and water deficit, are needed.

The project will also provide a platform for sharing information about precision agriculture across systems with the world of the aim of saving as much water as possible during the growing season. The Yom River Basin is one such example where this approach has been used successfully in the past. However, the same approach appears technically feasible but not viable in the economic sense.

### Activity 31: Project Implementation

Within activity 31, the project will install five Internet of Things (IoT) platforms, which would be connected to a central server. The IoT platform consists of sensors for soil moisture and temperature, general climate data, hardware and software for data processing, storage and communication, and technical support personnel. This approach is expected to provide farmers with real-time information that has the potential to increase production and reduce the need for inputs with large-scale and/or high-cost production. The use of such advanced technology by poor, vulnerable farmers has numerous benefits: it can be used in conjunction with organic fertilizers (e.g., biofertilizer or organic fertilizers), which can carry only 15 litres and would cost USD 20,000 each.

Recognizing that the adoption of precision agriculture is not a silver bullet for adaptation under climate change, there is no clear reason why agricultural activities including precision irrigation should be prioritized over other adaptation options. ## Legal and Administrative Framework Protecting Women and Gender Equality

From the perspective of key international commitments promoting women and gender equality, in 2000 Thailand ratified the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW) in 1985 and its Optional Protocol and endorsed the Beijing Platform for Action (BPFA), the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs), and finally the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).

Thailand has made significant efforts to integrate the international principles and instruments into the policy and legal framework from within the Constitution B.E. 2550 (2007), which has anti sex discrimination and gender equality provisions.

A Working Group for the Implementation of SDG 5 supports the implementation of the Goal Representatives from government agencies and civil society such as the Thailand Sustainable Development Commission, the National Council on Women, the Association for Persons with Disabilities (APWPDs), the Foundation for Women, Homenet Thailand Foundation for Labour and Employment Promotion and WeMove are all active in the Working Group. The Working Group allows government agencies to share information, exchange information, as well as discuss future direction in the implementation of SDG 5.

The Strategy for Women's Development Plan (2017-2021) is Thailand’s main mechanism for promoting women's development; it aligns with SDG 5 (Gender Equality). The Strategy has the vision of "[creating] social equality and economic empowerment for all women in Thailand." The Government also designed an action plan to implement the Strategy. The action plan focuses on community development, for example, the Vocational Training Program for Women and Families which provides training for unemployed, economically disadvantaged, and people at risk of sexual exploitation and human trafficking.

The Department of Women's Affairs and Family Development, of the Ministry of Social Development and Human Security, is the national women’s mechanism responsible for promoting gender equality and the empowerment of women. It was established in 2013 as a result of the recommendation from the National Committee on Policy and Strategy for the Improvement of the Status of Women, chaired by the Prime Minister and develops. The Office implements a five-year cycled National Women's Development Plans, which covers the period 2014-2018.

In 2015 Thailand passed its first law to promote gender equality, namely the Gender Equality Act B.E. 2558, which aims to provide protection to all people from gender discrimination. It was Thailand’s first legal instrument to specifically mention transgender people.
