# Introduction

The Paris Agreement, in which signatory countries pledge to keep global warming "well below" 2°C relative to pre-industrial levels and pursue efforts to limit it even further to 1.5°C, represents a response to the urgent threat posed by global climate change. In the world's developing countries, political progress and climate action are led closely to a sustainable economic transition, representing an investment opportunity for both public and private investors. The Paris Agreement is aligned with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Impact investing from the private sector is needed.

The SnCF Sub-Fund model represents a new approach that seeks to achieve a paradigm shift in the model for financialized climate finance. The SnCF's paradigm shift represents two major innovations: (i) the focus on mid-sized projects at the subnational level and (ii) most importantly, its combination of a "blind pool" investment model with an innovative financing structure that allows investors to invest directly into companies or projects over a period of years, combined with elements of public-backed funding such as grants, loans, and equity. The SnCF Sub-Fund model has generated significant excitement among NGOs, as evidenced by the 42 NGOS received to date and robust interest in the Equity pool from leading private investors, including foundations, endowments, institutional investors and high net worth individuals.

SnCF believes the anchor investment by the GCF together with technical assistance implemented by UNEP FI will be sufficient to attract a critical mass of additional private sector investors. These investors will allow the Fund to invest through privately owned special purpose vehicles in a multitude of climate change projects at the subnational level, including those that are not eligible for GCF funding. The equity investments into special purpose vehicles that hold interest in projects at the sub-national level, where there is no existing or planned public sector investment, will be made by the Fund and other private sector investors and economic returns. GCF and other third-party investors will make equity commitments to the Fund, with a total target size of $750-900 M USD, with GCF's commitment representing 20% of the total. A proposed structure for the SnCF Sub-Fund model is outlined in the following pages.

The SnCF Sub-Fund model allows the Fund to invest in projects that address a broad array of the 8 impact areas identified by the GCF pursuant to its Strategic Plan.

SnCF's "blind pool" fund model represents the opportunity to deploy capital efficiently and at scale. Potential projects will be filtered through multiple levels of review, both at the TA and Equity sides. As part of this process, the Fund will seek to identify and address any potential project risks, including those relevant barriers and opportunities. The intended result of this evaluation process is that only those projects that are deemed to have a high likelihood of success in terms of both climate impact and financial return will ultimately be funded by the SnCF.

The SnCF is a multi-country Fund that is set to invest up to 10 countries. Substantial effort has been devoted to developing the list of countries where they think their NOLs already and some who will end by to send the NOLs before the Board discussion; however, due to COVID-19 many NGOs have forwarded letter explaining that they are not able to attend the Board meeting in person or via Zoom. As such, it is expected that only a few NOLs will be received on time for the Board consideration and that some NOLs will be allowed to obtain additional NOLs that having been already made do not make it on time for the Board discussion. The Board will consider all NOLs that are received by 23 May, as well be presented to the Secretariat in due time and The GCF Board for their consideration as required. Should these additional NOLs will not prevent the Fund from starting investing as early as the first close.

## GCF’s Anchor Document

The GCF’s anchoring commitment to the Fund is essential to its success. GCF’s equity commitment will be in the form of a first-loss security (par pass on the upside), which is intended to catalyze equity contributions of an additional $600 - $750 MUSD from private investors ($730-990 MUSD total fund size), who have historically been reluctant to invest in projects with such high risk. The Fund will be used for 45 sub-midsize (>$15-$75MUSD) low carbon and climate resilient infrastructure projects in Africa, Asia, Latin America, and the Caribbean. The equity commitment is intended to provide a financial buffer primarily in the underlying of the projects, but may invest in debt or convertible debt on a case-by-case basis to ensure that the Fund can financially reasonable efforts to invest at least one project in each country for which the Fund receives an NOL.

Each of the individual projects in which the Fund will invest has its own capital structure, with the Fund holding equity and/or debt positions. The Fund’s equity commitment is intended to provide a financial buffer against parties providing the debt. ## Fund Strategy and Opportunity

Sub-national authorities have local responsibilities and are best placed to make decisions about nature-based solutions, including land use change and solutions related to water and sanitation; restorative agriculture/aequiculture; urban development solutions; waste optimization; renewable energy generation and distribution; and climate-resilient infrastructure. These projects also have strong co-benefits in terms of carbon reduction and adaptation effects. It is also locally that the social and environmental impacts of policies and programs are first experienced, and a greater opportunity lies to create positive feedback loops between policy design and investor point of view, or vice versa. However, there is an investment gap into this type of subnational middle size infrastructure. International financing institutions, such as MDBs, DFIs and the UN, are not well equipped to finance these projects which typically seek investment opportunities in excess of $100M USD. This program needs GCF support for three primary reasons:

- The geographies and the Subsectors where these projects will be built represent emerging economies where private sector capital markets will not operate (or will not operate without partners like GCF), which provide policy and programmatic validation along with financial risk mitigation.
- From an economic perspective, these projects are in countries that have a high level of energy poverty upstill. GCF support allows these types of projects to be completed many years sooner. For example, the transition from coal to natural gas in China is expected to take 20–30 years without GCF acceleration; the timing in developing countries, thanks to innovative finance vehicles that are supported by GCF, that transition (and the resulting pollution and GHG reductions) will take place at least a decade sooner.
- The GCF funding will help to support sustainable project design, including safeguarding, climate and environmental impacts assessments, as well as risk mitigation for projects like infrastructure.

Moreover, these projects are not currently being financed by the public and/or private sector because of several key dynamics and market failures:

- Uncertainties about the returns of investments, inadequate access to finance investments in, for example, energy efficiency, renewable energy generation, or transmission and distribution improvements that could help to strengthen climate resilience, may be disproportionate to businesses’ financial risk tolerance. The payback period on these projects is often longer than 10 years; climate resilient investments may only manifest over longer time frames, and the extent dependent upon uncertain climate impacts. They may also be complex to evaluate when part of broader investments.
- Financial institutions are reluctant to finance projects in developing countries due to limited information or higher perceived risks because of the lack of track record, borrowers’ inadequate capacity, and/or a lack of experience with innovative financing mechanisms.
- In the case of waste optimization, government policies have been slow to recognize the value in “waste” as an energy source. This is partly due to the perception that it is not economically feasible; and/or the system found in developed countries has been adopted, i.e., awarding contracts to haulers and processors on a per-ton basis.
- In the case of new renewable energy generation, market and technological barriers have delayed project development. These include: high upfront costs (e.g., solar photovoltaic panels are more expensive than production) and base-load needs (i.e., solar and wind are intermittent compared to conventional fossil-fueled generation methods) and related unfamiliarity with harmonizing grid access of clean energy.
- In the case of energy efficiency, there is a lack of awareness about the benefits of energy audit and bespoke retro tech technology solutions and contracting; for which contractors are not as numerous or experienced in developing countries. The private sector has been slow to develop themselves with savings over time, access to finance has been challenging. For example, cities may want to install LED streetlights but have no capacity to do so at all once, nor a willing partner to finance and fund a “leap from savings” capital model.
- In all cases, the lack of public sector investment in energy access slows skews slow/steady prioritization of these projects and/or makes them less financially rewarding.
- Knowledge gaps: Understanding climate vulnerability and risks and opportunities is essential to engaging with private sector actors. This includes understanding how to engage with the private sector as well as their ability to develop products or services that could help mitigate climate-related impacts.
- High discount rates dominate investment decision-making

## Gender imperative: [World Health Organization] The right to water and sanitation is recognized as fundamental human rights. However, women in many parts of the world face significant barriers to access to safe drinking water at home, 2.3 billion do not have basic sanitation and 1 billion practice open defecation. Women and girls are disproportionately affected by the lack of access to basic water, sanitation and hygiene facilities, which can lead to poor health outcomes for women and children. This is particularly true in rural areas where agriculture and domestic labour. This situation has implications for gender equality and empowerment.

One priority area is that of women's water-fetching responsibility and its associated time-out burden, and the implication this has on their ability to participate fully in society. Women are responsible for fetching water from five out of every six households where a drinking water source is off premises. Another priority is the growing evidence on sanitation-related gender-based violence faced by women and girls. Reports from Ethiopia, India, and refugee camps in Jordan highlight that women and girls are at risk of sexual harassment and assault when using public toilets or open defecation sites. In 2013, a study found that 45% of women in Somalia document physical and sexual harassment and assault by non-partners, fear of sexual violence and harassment is the most common reason for not using sanitation facilities.

Another priority area is that of women's participation in water, sanitation and hygiene decision-making and governance, leading to their social and political empowerment. One multi-country study found women's participation in key water sector institutions leadership as a significant predictor of functional and sustainable water services. There is evidence that women's participation in water management can enhance performance outcomes for the household and community.

### Specific Project Assessment: The Lange Safe Water Project The project employed a gender responsive approach to address the lack of safe drinking water access in rural, remote, and isolated regions. The project rehabilitates boreholes to provide clean water access to the local communities. In Lange, access to safe water is a major challenge for women and girls who spend up to 10 hours per week collecting water from distant sources. This forecloses communities to use unsafe water sources such as streams, in which the water must be boiled to treat. To boil the water, communities collect and burn firewood, which places greater pressure on dwindling forests and produces harmful emissions. The project aims to provide boreholes for clean drinking water and reduce day collecting firewood and water. Despite these efforts, boiling water alone still does not always guarantee that it is safe to drink, allowing women and girls continue to travel to areas of the forest to collect water.

### Gender Analysis: The Lange Safe Water Project used a gender responsive project design and elected to confirm the project's gender impacts with a third party auditor. The assessment and design were based on a comprehensive gender analysis to establish a gender baseline through a baseline survey and supplementary focus group discussions. The following are key findings from this analysis:

1. Time poverty – the amount of time women and girls spend collecting water greatly diminishes their ability to engage in income generation or education, increasing their economic dependence and decreasing their social status.
2. Individual and community empowerment - including meaningful participation and leadership, stronger social networks and agency that enables women more of a say in decision-making on key issues affecting them.
3. Exposure to gender-based violence in water collection – it emerged that women suffer GBV whilst travelling long distances to collect water and may also fall victim to violence at home as a result of delays in other household chores caused by the length of time it takes to collect water.

The project used the assessment to focus their action plan around the following project elements, in addition to addressing the issue of badly maintained boreholes:

1. The principle of shared domestic responsibilities to instil the idea that time women save on water collection should be spent on empowerment-focused activities, not simply redirected to other unpaid domestic tasks.

3.2 Gender-Responsive Project Design

It is impossible to integrate gender equality into projects or activities without clearly understanding gender issues within a given context. This requires an in-depth analysis of the social, economic and political factors underlying climate change-exacerbated gender inequality, and the potential contribution of women and men to mitigate and adapt to climate change in the project. The analysis also allows for the identification of strategic, action and indicator to better assess how initiatives have engaged with or impacted different groups.

Building on the safeguards and consultations, SnCF will require prospective projects to establish and commit to the following mandatory, minimum gender-responsive requirements:

- Collect, analyse and use sex-disaggregated data including gender indicators to inform gender-responsive project design, budgeting, staffing, implementation, monitoring and evaluation;
- Conduct gender analyses and applying key issues and recommendations identified to inform gender-responsive project design, budgeting, staffing, implementation, monitoring and evaluation;
- Analyse the potential impact of proposed activities on women and men, ensuring that these activities do not exacerbate existing gender-related inequalities, including gender-based violence, and seize opportunities to address gender gaps and support empowerment of women;
- Ensure that all staff involved in project design, budgeting, staffing, implementation have appropriate capacities and technical expertise to support gender-responsive action;
- Ensure that women and men have equal opportunities in terms of participation, decision-making and benefits, throughout the preparation, design, implementation, monitoring and evaluation of activities;
- Allocate sufficient resources for specific activities, technical support and/or other actions to improve gender equality considerations, including the meaningful engagement of diverse stakeholders and beneficiaries;

SnCF may direct and/or support an expert stakeholder opinion (with a specific emphasis on gender and environment expertise) be required to support the gender safeguards assessment or project design process, depending on project type, scale and context.

Gender-Responsive certification seeks to:

1. Build on the first three steps to conduct a comprehensive gender analysis and establish a Project baseline with gender goals and indicators.

WHY IS THIS IMPORTANT?

Gender analysis is a systematic way to identify context-specific key issues and factors that contribute to gender inequalities. It may consist of primary or secondary research, or a combination of both. It is impossible to integrate gender equality in projects or activities without clearly understanding gender issues within the targeted population. Gender analysis helps project developers understand the implications of gender on climate change mitigation, adaptation and disaster risk reduction interventions (and vice-versa), and design appropriate interventions that can benefit all members of a community. It also allows the project developers to develop indicators to better assess how initiatives have engaged with or impacted different groups.

Gender analysis explores individual, relational and structural factors, within both gender and non-gender dimensions, as these are likely to experience gender inequalities differently, or hold different roles, within different groups or relationships. Gender analysis attempts to answer at least three key sets of questions:

- What are the gender-based inequalities, discriminations and rights denials in each context? How do these issues intersect with other discrimination factors such as age, ethnicity, disability, class, etc.?
- How will gender relations influence the effectiveness and sustainability of the project? How can the project processes and activities be designed to reduce inequalities and increase equality?
- How will the proposed results affect the relative status of women and men? Will they exacerbate or reduce inequalities?

Step 5.b Establish meaningful gender performance indicators for the Project Gender performance indicators and sex-disaggregated targets can now be incorporated into the Project results framework.


WHAT ARE GENDER INDICATORS? Indicators play a crucial role in the gender responsive process throughout the policy cycle. Quantitative and qualitative indicators must enable ongoing monitoring and evaluation to ensure that the Project's gender equality objectives are being met and assessing the gender effect of Project activities. If objectives are not being met, it could be essential to re-assess the Project strategy and make amendments to ensure improvements. Gender indicators usually express how distant a certain situation is from gender equality and are usually expressed as gaps, ratios and generally as measures of "gender inequalities". Gender-blind indicators are those that do not allow the evaluator to uncover gender differences and are collected in a society because they are not collected or cannot be collected on a sex-disaggregated basis. For example, if participation is a gender-blind indicator but if collected separately for men and for women, can become a gender indicators. For example, the Programa Fase de Forestación (PROFAFOR) carbon sequestration project in Ecuador set a minimum plot size at 50 hectares, de facto excluding some smallholders, most whom are women. In Uganda, "the availability of land

# and capital of local farmers was a determining factor for participation, forcing smallholders without idle land into making the difficult choice between planting trees for carbon forestry or cultivating food crops. This approach ensures that projects have equal impacts on men and women, or at least do not negatively impact women or reinforce gender inequalities. Gender indicators also explore the nuances of gendered power relations through their likely influence in household decision-making. This goes beyond simple collection of sex-disaggregated data; rather the data point itself has a gendered component.

### REQUIREMENTS
Project Developers should consider these aspects and establish the Project's key gender indicators.

There are a few cross-cutting qualitative and quantitative issues that gender indicators can address:

- At the monetary level: How much money, dollar for dollar, reaches women and girls at the local levels of consultation and how are decisions made around disbursement, use, investment and reinvestments?

At the empowerment level: How many women and how many men are actively involved and engaged in reaching those decisions (about money disbursement, use, investment, and reinvestment) and in acting on those decisions? How many women participate in determining a valid indicator that best captures change for them?

— At the environmental/ecological level: How are access points to natural resources that poor men and women depend protected? How are men and women involved in the decisions and actions around stewarding natural resources for future generations?

— At the gender-differentiated level: What kinds of gender-differentiated evidence, perspectives, narratives and realities are captured, recorded and learned from as a direct result of their own measurements of progress?
