### A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

Please provide a brief description of the proposed project/programme, including the objectives and primary measurable benefits (investment criteria in section E). The detailed description can be elaborated on in section C.

The national grid in Indonesia has been dominated by fossil-fuelled generation. Based on PLN’s RUPTIL 2018, the 2017 generation mix was as follows: coal-fired power plants accounted for 65 percent of total capacity and generated 49 percent of electricity; oil-fired power plants accounted for 30 percent of total capacity and generated 25 percent of electricity. In Paris, Indonesia committed to a Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) at 29 percent reduction of GHG emissions by 2030. To achieve this target, the government aims to increase its renewable energy share from 17 percent in fossil power generation mix to 23 percent by 2026. The proposed Project aims to support a scale-up of investment in geothermal energy development and deployment in Indonesia. This will help (i) reduce greenhouse gas emissions; (ii) provide supply alternatives and diversifying its generation portfolio; (iii) reducing reliance on fossil fuels and exposure to commodity price volatilities; and (iv) ultimately lowering the energy sector’s emissions compared to the business-as-usual scenario.

The proposed Project supports the GoI in the establishment of a Geothermal Resource Risk Mitigation Facility, under which debt financing will be provided by the World Bank Group (WBG), including IDA (US$10 million loan), IBRD (US$253 million loan), and GCF (US$25 million soft loan). US$150 million reimbursable grant, and US$18.10 million grant. Specifically, the Project will support the establishment of a Geothermal Resource Risk Mitigation Facility to provide private developers for the financing, together with the corresponding portion of the IBRD Loan, of their resource confirmation costs (RC) and/or exploration costs (EC). The Project will also support the development of geothermal assets’ lifetime (to be cross-checked post drilling in a two-step approach to GHG accounting, see Section E6), an additional 10 years beyond the current 20-year standard. This will help to unlock private sector investment and increase private capital mobilized for investment in geothermal power generation. The Project will be implemented in tranches with the impact of the first tranche (a total of US$150 million from GCF) is expected to be around 60% of the impact from the total project.

Indonesia's financial markets are considered very shallow, and capital markets smaller and less liquid compared to the regional peers. The Indonesian bond market is dominated by government securities, with limited intermediation by non-bank financial institutions (NBFI)s with modest hedging and insurance facilities. Indonesia's equity market is relatively undeveloped and market capitalization of Indonesia listed companies is lower than that of its regional peers. The country's banking penetration - both conventional and Islamic - remains at a relatively low level.

Despite relatively stable gross domestic product (GDP) over the past decade, the shallow financial market in Indonesia has been characterized by limited participation from foreign investors in government securities, bonds, exchange-traded funds (ETFs), real estate investment trusts (REITs), options and futures for index and individual equities. The Indonesian bond market is dominated by government securities with only 10 listed debt capital markets issues out of around 20, compared to 215 in Malaysia, due to lack of participation of large state-owned enterprises in the secondary market. Indonesia's equity market is also relatively shallow as it remains heavily reliant on bank funding, whereas the market is constrained by limited asset base of domestic institutions. While basic banking services are widely available and accessible across the country, access to credit for small businesses relies on risky bank pricing, as well as a credible benchmark for corporate bond markets. Currently, the 5- to 20-year government bond yield is well above international levels; however, this is not a curve in the 10-year plus and greater than 20-year windows.

To finance development projects, the market will continue to rely on domestic institutions including developing long-term domestic institutional investors in the market, expanding investor base for bond and equity.

## Indonesia

Indonesia is the world's fourth most populous nation with over 260 million people, the eighth largest economy globally and the largest economy in Southeast Asia with a GDP per capita in terms of purchasing power parity of US$1,472, and a member of the G20. The country has experienced significant economic growth since independence from colonial rule in 1945, but this growth has been unevenly distributed across regions and sectors.

The national grid in Indonesia has been dominated by fossil-fuel generation. The total installed power generation capacity is estimated at 55 gigawatts (GW) at the end of 2017 to meet an estimated demand of 30 GW. The primary energy mix is such that coal accounts for about 68 percent, natural gas for 24 percent, and oil for 8 percent. The current power generation mix is coal 58.3 percent, gas 23.2 percent, diesel 6 percent, and RE 10.5 percent. According to the state energy plan, the national grid will need to add about 75 GW of capacity: 31.9 GW from coal, 24.4 GW from gas, 14.1 GW from hydro, 5.8 GW from geothermal and 1.2 GW from renewable energy (RE). The projected generation mix in the year 2026 is expected to be coal 53 percent, gas 0.4 percent, diesel 9.7 percent, and 23.2 percent RE.

PLN plans to achieve 99.7 percent national electrification rate by 2025 from a current rate of 93 percent. The expansion of transmission and distribution will include an additional 61,850 km of high voltage transmission, 278,351 km of medium voltage transmission, and 40,000 km of low voltage distribution lines.

Constant pressure to keep electricity cost low favors more coal in the generation mix, where new coal plants are expected to be in operation by 2026. Geothermal power is a baseload generation technology that can be developed with most renewable energy sources. Under the right conditions, it can be cost-competitive with coal or natural gas, which means that countries with such indigenous resources can depend less on imported fuels and increase their energy security. As a clean source of electricity, geothermal power also helps to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and furthering the country's climate change agenda. It can also contribute to expanding access to electricity, economic growth, job creation, and poverty reduction, particularly on the eastern islands where electrification rates are much lower and poverty rates higher than the national average.

Geothermal energy is developed through a multi-stage approach that begins with surface investigations followed by exploration drilling to confirm the availability of the geothermal resource, and its delineation drilling to define the extent of the resource. The total cost of developing a geothermal project can be very high because the resource potential must be estimated from seismic surveys, but can only be confirmed through an exploration-drilling program, which in Indonesia is estimated at US$ 10-25 million per well. This cost is usually borne by the developer, who may have to pay either the owner's equity or balance sheet finance, which is put at risk without a chance of recovery if the resource is not adequate. Therefore, it is important that developers have access to financing for the initial stages and the total cost of developing all stages of a geothermal operation, finding this initial capital can be challenging for developers.

The geothermal tariff system in Indonesia, while underpinned by the need to keep electricity prices low, has not been conducive for geothermal development, particularly for private developers. #### Exploration drilling risk also known as resource risk, is the biggest barrier to obtain financing as the uncertainties associated with the availability of productive and developable resources increase investors' requirements for return on equity. In order to address this issue, the Government of Indonesia (GoI) has initiated the Geothermal Energy Upstream Development Project (GEUDP) has begun to support the GoI in addressing this issue.

The project is financed by US$49 million contribution from the GoI through the Government's Infrastructure Fund (FIS) and US$10.5 million in equity investment by PT DMI, as well as a US$28.5 million technical assistance grant from the Global Environment Facility (GEF) (a project implemented by PT Darana Multi Infrastructure (PT DMI), the same entity that will be implementing this project). #### The project started in July 2017 and drilling is expected to start in the second half of 2018.

This would complement the government-sponsored drilling and crowd in private sector financing.

The MoEF Regulation No. 46/2016 concerning the Utilization of Environmental Services for Geothermal in the National Park Grand Forest Park and Natural Recreation Park provides a legal basis to enable geothermal power development in the adjacent areas of these parks, which are located within the boundaries of Taman Wisata Alam (TWA) Tanah Putih Parks (Teman Hutai Raya) and Nature Recreational Parks (Taman Wisata Alam) through the Environmental Service Permit (SPK) issued by MoEF. The SPK is valid for 20 years, renewable once. The areas covered under these permits include Cagar Alam dan Suaka Margasatwa. Core zones and core blocks of these conservation areas remain legally off-limits for such development.

Under Presidential Decree No. 28/1981, Pertamina (the state-owned oil and gas company) was provided the mandate to explore and operate the geothermal fields, sell steam to PLN (the state utility company), and enter into joint ventures with local private sector companies to develop geothermal power projects in Indonesia. Under this arrangement, when Pertamina was the only entity with the mandate to develop geothermal projects in Indonesia. Under JOC signed between Pertamina and foreign investors, the private partner (which could be a contractor and/or an investor) operates the area for steam production and power generation. JOCs have been operated under a Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) or Build-Own-Transfer (BOT) arrangement (i.e., build, operate, transfer). The first geothermal power plant in Indonesia was developed by Pertamina with Parntina and later transferred to PLN.

The first geothermal power plant in Indonesia was developed in 1980. Many projects were explored and developed under the Joint Operation Contract (JOC) model. The private parties involved in the JOCs included Chevron, Unocal, Amoco, and others. These JOCs have been signed with Pertamina and foreign investors to develop geothermal fields in Indonesia. These JOCs have facilitated the exploitation of some of the biggest geothermal fields: Darajat, West Java (200 MW); Sidoarjo, East Java (150 MW); Pamekasan, North Sulawesi (375 MW); and Banyuwangi, East Java (480 MW). The other JOCs include: Pamekasan, West Java; Wapayangan Uluwatu, Lampung (220 MW); Laherang, North Sulawesi (130 MW); Deng, Central Java (60 MW); Patuha, West Java (45 MW); Sidoarjo, East Java; Surabaya, North Sumatra (112 MW); Uluwatu, East Nusa Tenggara (10 MW); and Matarama, East Nusa Tenggara (2.5 MW).

There are several public developers in the market. First PPT Geo Dipo was created in 2002 as a subsidiary of PT Pertamina and PPT Indonesia. The company has developed two power plants: PPT Geo Dipo (140 MW) and PPT Geo Jawa (also expected to be 400 MW). In 2011, Geo Dipo was transferred into a state-owned enterprise majority-owned by MoEF, and assigned its operational fields Untukom Geothermal Central Java and Ajiyan Geothermal East Java. Second, Energi Indonesia (EI) is the company that has been developing geothermal power plants in Indonesia since 2013 when it inherited Pertamina's geothermal license. Third PLN, the state utility power, has a unit dedicated to geothermal development. State-owned companies such as PT Pertiwi and PT Perindo have also been involved in the development of geothermal projects and programs have been implemented by SOEIL The challenge lies in developing a risk-sharing model where SOEs can use government funds to finance the initial capital investment, while private sector investors provide the remaining equity budget to fund critical build phase, while creating a more conducive environment for private sector participation to help close the financing gap.

## C.6. Regulation, Taxation and Insurance (If applicable)

### The GoI issued Regulation No. 79/2014 on National Energy Policy (KEN), setting a target for renewable energy of 23% by the year 2025, including the national supply, and also providing medium term targets, largely dominated by fossil fuelled sources. Recent regulatory changes related to tariff setting and PPAs reflect the GoI's efforts to facilitate geothermal investments in India.

MEMR has issued several regulations to guide the development of the geothermal sector. The most pertinent to this Project include:

- Regulation No. 17/2014 which mandates PLN to purchase geothermal steam and electricity produced by geothermal power plants;
- Regulation No. 44/2016 concerning the form and procedure of placement and withdrawal of exploration commitment;
- Regulation No. 36/2017 which lays out the procedures for assignment of conducting preliminary survey (PSP), and for submission of results thereof to the project steering group (PSG);
- Regulation No. 37/2017 which lists the 67 WPLs for Indicative Use;
- Regulation No. 48/2017 which provides for the terms and conditions of power purchase sale and purchase agreement) which set the maximum period for Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) to be the same as the concession validity period;
- Regulation No. 50/2017 (previous No. 12/2017), which sets the reasonable level of investment returns, or ceiling tariff, at average electricity supply costs (baht per kilowatt-hour), or BPPF defined as PLN's avoided costs in the local power market, and provides a reference for a maximum allowable return on equity to be used by the developer for tariff negotiation.

Most recently, MOF Regulation (PM) No. 62/2017(1) provides the legal and regulatory framework for financing the operations of PSCs to support geothermal projects to be managed by SM. PSP was the original design of GFF to provide loans to addressees at high costs and with limited repayment terms, and to require them to provide collateral from project developers and were to be paid back in full even in the case of unsuccessful drilling. The design of the PSCs under the new MOF Regulation (PM) No. 62/2017(1), however, (among others), hinges on a more balanced approach to risk allocation in the overall geothermal development process.

In addition, MoF also offers fiscal incentives such as tax holidays and allowances and an income tax facility for the geothermal sector. The GFF Regulation No. 3/2014 provided for a 5-year moratorium on corporate income tax (CIT) provisioned a 30 percent reduction of corporate net income for 5 years for geothermal developers. At this stage there are no tax incentives for the Geothermal Risk Mitigation Facility and it will be explored further with the legal team during Appraisal.

Sub-borrowers should also ensure that they have obtained all of the needed approvals when seeking to access the Facility. Sub-projects will be accepted only when meeting eligibility criteria specified in the Operations Manual – including ensuring that environmental and social risks can be adequately mitigated.

It will be made clear to EE (SM) and sub-borrowers that no additional financing will be provided to cover applicable taxes.

- Facilitating external evaluations and ensuring that recommendations are implemented.

## 3.3.1. Environmental co-benefits and economic co-benefits, including gender-sensitive development impact

### Environmental co-benefits:

A significant scale-up of geothermal energy investment through the proposed Project is expected to deliver tremendous environmental benefits to Indonesia, mainly through the magnitude of avoidance of greenhouse gas emissions in Indonesia.

* First, it can be extracted without burning a fossil fuel such as coal, oil or air and there is no combustion in the geothermal development process. Geothermal fields produce only about one-sixth of the carbon dioxide that a relatively clean natural gas-fueled power plant produces. Binary plants release essentially no emissions. While direct extraction of geothermal energy does not produce greenhouse gases, indirect emissions are associated with the emissions by the thermal power plants. At the local pollution level, geothermal power has negligible emissions of sulfur oxides and nitrogen oxides compared to fossil fuel-fired power plants. In a closed-loop geothermal system, there are no CO2 emissions. With the most advanced technology and drilling techniques, the environmental impact is minimal.

* Second, geothermal energy can be used for non-power generation freshwater requirements. For example, a geothermal plant uses on average five gallons of freshwater per megawatt-hour (MWh), while binary air-cooled plants use no fresh water. Coal-fired power plants require 10 to 25 times more water than geothermal plants.

* Third, geothermal drilling has a smaller footprint compared to conventional energy extraction operations, as it is relatively less invasive and does not require the construction of large infrastructure such as dams or reservoirs. The use of binary plant techniques and programs will also allow for avoidance of critical conservation or protection forest areas and minimize habitat disturbance issues. The presence of major drilling management firms such as Halliburton and Schlumberger, which employ the most advanced and efficient technologies will be employed to ensure the most sustainable drilling techniques.

## F.1 Economic and Financial Analysis

### Economic Analysis

The proposed Project will contribute to Indonesia’s geothermal development goal of adding 5.6 GW by 2030, and by 2040, it is expected that the total installed capacity in Indonesia would be around 8 GW. The project will: (i) contributing to the achievement of the national energy mix target; (ii) contributing to reducing greenhouse gas emissions from the energy sector compared to a BAU scenario; An estimated cost-benefit analysis was conducted for the proposed project and sub-project candidates (the full development of a 55 MW and a 10 MW power plant), and the results are presented below.

### Cost-benefit analysis

The economic cost estimates were derived based on known or inferred relationships between costs and technical characteristics of geothermal wells. The total cost of developing a geothermal well is determined by the following factors: (i) size of the development (MW) determined by both resources availability and enthalpy; (ii) the enthalpy and depth of the resources; (iii) difficulty of access to the concession area; and (iv) cost and efficiency of drilling.

In terms of comparison, geothermal development comprises four types of costs: (i) drilling costs, a function of the number wells and the cost of each well; (ii) infrastructure costs for construction roads, well pads and other infrastructure facilities; (iii) operation and maintenance costs including ground subsidence; (iv) project management costs.

Drilling cost is a function of the following factors: (i) well productivity; (ii) success rate of drilling; (iii) well depth; and (iv) project management costs. Well productivity, in turn, depends largely on the enthalpy of the resources and well permeability (i.e., the ease with which fluids flow into the well).

Three entry scenarios were analyzed in the assumption: (i) low enthalpy, i.e., temperature between 180°C and 230°C; (ii) medium enthalpy, i.e., temperature between 230°C and 275°C; and (iii) high enthalpy with both high temperature and high pressure. The base case scenario assumes medium enthalpy for both fields. It was further assumed three wells will be drilled in the exploration phase.

## Financial Analysis

The Financial Analysis (FA) was carried out from two different perspectives: (i) one from the developer's perspective, assessing financial viability of the 55 MW and 10 MW developments on a with- and without-project basis; (ii) the other from the implementing agency's perspective, assessing its cash-in- and out-flows related to the Facility.

## From a Developer's Perspective

### Financing mix in Indonesia
The costs of geothermal exploration are typically born by the developer through full equity financing because debt financing is usually not available at this stage of the development due to the high levels of resource uncertainty. However, once the project has been proven and the well is drilled, debt financing becomes easier.

### Costing Financing
Each stage of geothermal development is associated with a certain amount of risks and capital requirements. Although the capital requirements are higher in later stages, the resource risks at early exploration stages are generally lower than those at later stages. The high level of resource uncertainty associated with the exploration stage would demand a risk premium compensating for this high resource uncertainty.

### Cost of Capital
A cost of debt at 8.0% and corporate tax at 25% have been assumed.

#### Outcome of the financial analysis from a developer's perspective:
- **Without Project Intervention:** The 55 MW and 10 MW geothermal developments are unlikely to be pursued due to high exploration risk, low resource certainty, and perceived geothermal development-related risks.
- **With Project Interventions:**
  - For the private investor, the 55 MW case is even for low scenario base cases. For the 10 MW plant, high or medium throughput scenarios are required in order to achieve a positive NPV at a discount rate of 8% and an internal rate of return (IRR) on S$24.
- **With Project Interventions:** The 55 MW case is even for low scenario base cases. For the 10 MW plant, high or medium throughput scenarios are required in order to achieve a positive NPV at a discount rate of 8% and an IRR on S$24.
