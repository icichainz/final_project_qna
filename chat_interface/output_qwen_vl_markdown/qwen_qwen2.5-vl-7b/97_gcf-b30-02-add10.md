A.21 Executive summary (max. 750 words, approximately 1.5 pages)

In line with the mandate obtained by the African Development Bank (AfDB) from the Heads of State of the GS Sahel countries to support the development and deployment of renewable energy in the GS Sahel region, the AfDB is assist the GS Sahel countries in mobilizing resources from various sources including the GCF to address the barriers that hinder these countries from adopting a low emission power generation path by seing the abundant solar potential of the region.

The Desert to Power GS Sahel Facility (DPF) consists of US$ 150 million GCF financing that leverages US$ 816.7 million co-financing including US$ 379.6 million from the AfDB and US$ 437 million from private sector co-financing.

The Sahel is a vast semi-arid region that lies south of the Sahara Desert and stretches from Africa's Atlantic coast to the Red Sea, covering parts of Algeria, Burkina Faso, Chad, Egypt, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Libya, Mali, Mauritania, Niger, Nigeria, Senegal, and Sudan. These countries are among the poorest in Africa with an average per capita income below US$ 1000. The region is characterized by a high dependence on agriculture and cattle-rising remain a central pillar of the economy and the occupation of a large majority of the population. The production of high-value resources such as oil, hydrocarbons, gold, etc., is limited.

All the GS Sahel countries are among the bottom 10 of the Human Development Index (HDI) and are among the top 10 poorest countries in the world. The GS Sahel Strategy for Development and Security identifies its members as having a high vulnerability to climate change and natural disasters, with low capacity to adapt and manage risks, and low state capacity as key contributors to the region's development challenges. # Impact of COVID on G5 Sahel Countries

According to the African Economic Outlook (AEO), 2021, African economies contracted by 2.1% in 2020 due to the pandemic and an estimated US$4.5 billion required to lift the newly extreme poor out of poverty.

The COVID-impact in the G5 Sahel countries is especially manifesting itself through subdued economic growth rates and a decline in public revenues, which are largely derived from taxes on goods and services (GST) and other revenue collections (taxes), and led to a re-prioritization of public spending to social sectors of health and social safety nets.

The electricity sector sub-sector in the G5 countries, as in most African countries, has suffered from disruptions to global supply chains, with access to lower demand in global markets for a wide range of African commodities. Low electricity access rates have also exposed the vulnerability of the public health systems in these countries as reliable power is required to operate and maintain medical equipment such as ventilators and other life-saving cold-temperature facilities for vaccines.

On the electricity project financing front, the COVID situation has seen some delays in the disbursement of funds from international financial institutions, which will have a negative impact on expected new generation and grid investment projects.

## Desert to Power GS Sahel Facility (DpF or the Facility)

The Desert to Power GS Sahel Facility seeks to enable the GS Sahel countries to adopt a low-emission pathway by increasing their access to solar energy.

### The Facility has the potential to deliver:

- A substantial climate mitigation dividend which is estimated at over 14.4 million tCO2eq/yr for the GS Sahel countries over the programme lifetime.
- This dividend will be reaped by adding one GW of solar capacity in the region, with a further 3 GW of solar capacity being added to the generation mix across the five countries.

### Proposed Approach:

The proposed approach will address the identified barriers through three components endorsed by the GS Sahel Heads of State:
1. The first component will provide grants and investments to de-risk Solar IPPs and pave the way for the uptake of a regional solar market.
2. A second component will provide concessional finance to solar IPPs as well as partial risk guarantees (PRGs) to cover investors against the risk of the government (or the government-owned utility) failing to perform its obligations under the power purchase agreements (PPAs).
3. A third component focuses on technical assistance (TA) which includes:
   - The development of capacity of local solar developers and IPPs.
   - The creation of a clear and reliable environment for IPPs to attract private sector investors in solar energy across the GS Sahel region.

### Expected Outcomes and Impact of the Facility

The Facility intends to generate the following outcomes: (1) A backbone grid with utility-scale storage for solar power integration and basic technical conditions in place for a regional solar market; (2) 500 MW of additional solar generation capacity through IPPs. The IPPs are expected to mobilize US$ 14.17 million in equity and US$ 389.6 million in debt financing, with the remaining US$ 389.6 million as concessional finance from the Power to Finance Facility. The creation of a clear and reliable environment for private sector solar project financing and the development of adequate capacity at national institutions in the GS Sahel countries to ensure the successful implementation of the IPPs are expected indirect beneficiaries estimated at 700,000 people. It is also estimated that 1250 jobs will be created during construction and 450 permanent jobs.

This is as a result of an estimated additional 20% of installed generation capacity installed as a result of the project activities but not funded by the project.
This is based on the assumption of 2.5 jobs per MW during construction, and 0-9 jobs per MW for O&M.

### B.1. Climate Context (max. 300 words, approximately 2 pages)

The overall climate context:

The Sahel region is characterized by some of the lowest rates of electrification and ownership of cooling devices (e.g., fans, refrigerators) in the world, despite the fact that almost 700 million people in Africa are living in areas where the average daily temperature exceeds 25 degrees. By 2040, this number will approach 1 billion. The region is also one of the most vulnerable to climate change and its impacts. The countries of the G5 Sahel states in particular have so far been minor contributors to global GHG emissions. The countries are now facing a significant increase in their energy demand and emissions throughout the Sahel region as it comes into the brunt of a changing climate.

Increasing electricity demand is driven by population growth, cooling demands and electrification of appliances, and not by industrialization or urbanization. The energy sector plays a pivotal role in contributing to the region’s climate mitigation and adaptation goals, a role it can only assume if it fundamentally transforms itself into a low-carbon energy supply system.

Progress towards meeting commitments to climate change is limited due to resource endowments on impacts on energy supply, transmission and distribution. Cross-sector impacts on energy from climate change can be seen across socio-economic sectors and include competition for water resources (in electricity generation), increased demand for cooling in buildings and transport, and the need for adaptation measures.

The countries’ high dependence on fossil fuels and exponential demand growth (average 10% annual increase) make them particularly vulnerable to future energy price volatility.

The G5 Sahel states are heavily dependent on biomass for cooking energy and the AFOLU sector currently represents the largest source of GHG emissions. All G5 Sahel countries have set unconditional and conditional national climate change mitigation targets, which include a mix of renewable energy technologies. Common priority sectors identified in both the countries’ economic development plans and Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) include climate-smart agriculture, sustainable land management, increasing access to energy with a strong focus on developing their renewable energy potentials.

Regional context

The G5 Sahel countries share common socio-economic and infrastructural characteristics, providing the opportunity to tackle their climate challenges in a concertied fashion.


Burkina Faso: Burkina Faso’s high dependence on climate-sensitive economic activities both at national and sub-national levels makes it one of the most vulnerable countries in Africa. The rural poor being the most vulnerable group. CO2 emissions per capita are 0.18 t (ICDI, 2019). In the business-as-usual scenario, emissions from energy use will increase by 35% between now and 2040. Burkina Faso has set a target of reducing GHG emissions by 7,800 tCO2e/annum per year in 2030, i.e., 6.6% when compared to BAU scenario. The country’s NDCs will reduce emissions by 14%, which is equivalent to 11,590 tCO2e/annum per year in 2030. The energy sector has been identified as a priority sector for emission reduction strategies, together with the objectives of reaching sustainable energy access for all.


Chad: Chad’s development vision is to become an emerging economy by 2030, driven by diversified and competitive economic sectors, including agriculture, livestock, mining, industry and services. It ranks amongst the lowest in the world. The GHG emissions of Chad are expected to rise from 18 million tCO2e/annum per year in 2015 to 46 million tCO2e/annum by 2030 (ICDI, 2019). The NDC mitigation objective is a cumulative 18.2% (unconditional) to 71% (conditional) reduction in GHG emissions by 2030 compared with the BAU scenario. This will be achieved through energy efficiency and renewable objective and 162,000 tCO2e/eq., for the conditional objective. In line with the unconditional objective.

Electricity Sector Context

The GS Sahel region is characterized by low energy access rates which are as low as 2% in the rural areas of Chad, high reliance on fossil fuel and biomass to cover the growing energy needs and aging electricity transmission and distribution infrastructure. GS Sahel countries share common features like a large population with limited access to electricity, poor quality power supply, generation from a steady and growing demand that is being served in ways not always friendly to the environment, Excluding Chad and Mauritania, which have made significant progress in electrification, most GS Sahel countries are expected to follow a similar path.

The total installed capacity of the region (about 2.5 GW out of which 72% is from non-renewable sources) is insufficient to meet the growing demand, resulting in frequent load shedding by utilities. Domestic generation is based mainly on costly heavy fuel oil. Peak demand is also met by utilities employing emergency diesel generators, which are not only expensive but also contribute significantly to GHG emissions, an approach which is unsustainable with regards to the emissions path the region could take given the expected energy demand growth. The GS Sahel countries will need to invest in cleaner technologies, in particular renewable sources of electricity generation, to reduce their dependence on fossil fuels and meet the region's GHG emission profiles, to expose revenue to oil price shocks, and to take advantage of global agreements such as the Paris Agreement that have been aimed at decarbonizing the power sector. Electricity Grids Context

Existing electricity networks across the GS Sahel countries were not designed to integrate non-dispatchable renewable energy. Power distribution infrastructure that is capable of accepting all ranges of acceptable voltage ratings, including additional variable renewables such as solar or wind to these dispatching grids requires careful consideration. These considerations include several technical factors such as voltage stability and harmonics, which are critical for the operation of power distribution systems, and the overall operational flexibility for dispatch centers including potential economic constraints. The GS Sahel countries have a high penetration of diesel generators in their electricity grids, making it impossible to dispatch renewable energy generation surplus across the countries, thus widely limiting the use of solar energy. In addition, the GS Sahel countries are characterized by low levels of electrification and limited access to grid power. Therefore, the development of new transmission lines and expansion of electricity grids and the integration of utility-scale storage options is required to establish the backbone for the deployment of renewable energy in these countries.

Access to energy

Low access to electricity entrenches fragility and represents a key challenge considering political insecurity in many parts of the region. The lack of reliable power supply hinders the ability of households, businesses and generating income, which impedes efforts towards poverty reduction. At the social level, energy poverty and inequality in access to energy in most GS Sahel countries achieve socio-economic targets and hinder the achievement of sustainable development goals. Energy is a key factor for their vulnerability to climate change, natural disasters, and pandemics, as energy is an important input for water, sanitation, health care, education, and economic activity.

Power generation context

Overall, the total installed capacity of GS Sahel is insufficient to meet the electricity demand, especially considering the rapid population growth. Mauritania is the only country in the region that has the ability to export electricity. A large portion of non-electricity energy needs is still covered by biomass, which has led to the development of a number of small-scale power plants with limited capacity and low potential for further power generation capacity. The Sahel has a tremendous renewable energy potential, and the GS Sahel countries benefit from some of the highest solar energy irradiation and PV potential in the world.

## Complementarity and coherence

The Facility will work in a coordinated way with existing and planned activities in the region. Individual initiatives will be aligned with the regional priorities identified by the GCF, as described in section B.4 of the funding proposal. A key criterion, in addition to country priorities, is coordination with other initiatives that are already being implemented or planned at national level.

The Facility will have to be in line with national sector development plans of the 5 countries and the Desert to Power Roadmaps, which were validated by all 5 countries as of the end of 2020 while noting:

* The GCF is supporting initiatives that are complementary to existing systems, a shield against desertification and degradation. DIP has been identified as a flagship initiative under the Climate Investment Funds (CIF) in the Sahel region, which will be implemented through the GGW corridor, putting in place conditions which will protect the new forests with a new paradigm on energy access and rural electrification. The GCF is also supporting initiatives that are complementary to modern use of electricity that promotes better land management.

* The Facility will have to be in line with existing and planned activities between partners and accelerate the implementation of projects, and notably the energy working group of the Sahel Alliance for Energy Transition (SAET).

* The West African Power Pool (WAPP) and the ECOWAS Centre for Renewable Energy and Energy Efficiency (ECREE). The WAPP aims to establish regional power integration and a common electricity market in West Africa. It is currently operational between 10 ECOWAS Member States and it is notably in charge of the development of regional solar parks in Burkina Faso, Mali, Niger and Senegal. The ECREE aims at promoting energy efficiency and the deployment of renewable energy and energy efficiency solutions.

* The UN Development Programme (UNDP) has been mandated by the GCF to promote peace, stability and inclusive, sustainable development in the entire Sahel region. DIP is coherent with the UN Development Assistance Framework (DAF) for the Sahel region which aims at promoting cross-border and regional cooperation in the region, building resilience to climate and improving management of natural resources, promoting access to renewable energy and empowering women and youth towards a more diversified economy in the Sahel.

* The AfCFTA Infrastructure Fund created by the AIBD and currently co-led by 28 African countries. DIP is coherent with the AfCFTA Infrastructure Fund which aims at financing and launching competitive procurement programs in the GS-Sahel countries which seeks to implement projects that are aligned with national sector development plans, have local content, and local content. * The DIP is also coherent with the Desert to Power Roadmap for West Africa and a Francophone West Africa LDCs which will provide additional solar capacity in Mali and Burkina Faso, thus will benefit from the GCF support. These initiatives are complementary as they aim at increasing access to electricity in these countries.

B.2. Theme of change (max. 1,000 words; approximately 2 pages plus diagram)

The power sector in the GS-Sahel countries is characterized by low energy access rates, especially for the poorest such as female-headed households, by a generation system that relies predominantly on fossil fuels and small-scale renewable energy sources, and by transmission and distribution networks that are inadequate to integrate larger shares of variable renewable energy sources. The proposed programme will address barriers that prevent the Sahel countries from transitioning their power sectors away from fossil fuels and towards renewable energy sources by harnessing their largely untapped solar potential. Technical barriers: Existing electricity networks in the region were not designed to integrate large shares of variable renewable energy, such as solar or wind, which is a major challenge for countries such as Mali from the perspective of de-risking IPP and distributed generation. ### Project description (max. 2000 words, approximately 4 pages)
The Desert to Power G5 Sahel Facility seeks to enable the African countries of the Sahel region to adopt a low-emission pathway by transforming their desert area into an opportunity to address their energy and climate challenges. The Sahel region is characterized by high solar radiation levels and vast areas of unused land, which could be harnessed for renewable energy generation.

The Desert to Power G5 Sahel Facility has unmatched renewable energy availability, and the G5 Sahel countries benefit from some of the highest solar radiation in the world. ## Technology and Innovation

Innovation is a key characteristic of the Desert to Power Facility, within which innovation will take place at three levels: i) the deployment of private solar PV at scale in the GS Sahel countries; ii) technology innovation in grid-scale storage.

### a) Deployment of private solar PV at scale in the GS Sahel countries

Currently there is only 170 MWp of installed PV capacity in the region, ranging from 5 MW in Chad to 85 MW in Mauritania. The Desert to Power Facility will increase this PV capacity will increase by over 300%. This deployment of private sector solar PV at scale in the participating countries constitutes a step change for the GS Sahel countries and will have a significant impact on the development of the grids-connected solar in the region. While these technologies have been deployed at scale in other countries, this innovation represented by Desert to Power relates directly to the scale and location of these systems.

### b) Technology innovation in grid-scale storage

While there is significant worldwide experience with pumped storage plants, other grid-scale storage technologies are still under development or yet to date been deployed in the region. By way of example, large-scale Battery Energy Storage Systems based on Li-ion systems are currently deployed at scale in some countries such as Germany and Japan. Other promising battery technologies, more suited to stationary and developing country settings, such as Sodium-ion, Lithium-ion, Vanadium Redox Flow Batteries (VRFB) and Molten Salt Thermal storage batteries have been developed but not yet deployed on a number of cases in developed countries. Another potential storage technology is Liquid Air Energy Storage which has recently been carried out and the first 50MW battery storage facility is currently under construction in the United Kingdom.

## G.2. Gender assessment

### Background

Women and girls in the GS Sahel countries are particularly more vulnerable to climate change and effects of energy poverty due to strong gender-based inequalities. They lack access to livelihood assets, division of labor, and decision-making power within households. Women’s participation in the energy sector is low; women are underrepresented in the workforce, especially in the renewable energy sector. Women’s roles as unpaid carers and their responsibility for household energy consumption and food preparation persist in the energy sector, especially in terms of gender disaggregated statistics and their consideration in decision-making processes. The GS Sahel countries have made progress in integrating the gender dimension into the Desert to Power project phase I, specifically recognizing women’s role in the energy sector as unpaid carers and their responsibility for household energy consumption and food preparation.

### Action Plan

To remove these barriers in the energy sector within the Desert to Power Facility program phase i., specific recommendations are made. The following actions will be taken to create a more enabling environment for the integration of women in the energy sector. Therefore, the following main actions are recommended:

- Strengthening gender-responsive capacity of institutional stakeholders (ultilizes, transmission system companies, and other relevant institutions) through training and awareness raising on gender equality issues.
- Evaluating and operationalizing gender-responsive national energy policies and sectoral regulatory frameworks to mobilize more private sector investments in IPPs in the GS Sahel countries.

### Women in the energy sector

There are significant opportunities available on women in the energy sector in the 45 SACE countries so far. However, the challenges met by a majority of women in the energy area in the African continent have certain similarities: (1) gender-specific problems in relation to roles in energy production and use; (ii) women and girls are disproportionately affected by lack of access to modern energy services; (iii) they bear the main burden of biomass collection; (iv) women are poorer than men (both in resources and time); and (v) women have limited access to information, education and training, and access to land, natural resources, credit, information and decision-making, at all levels.

Therefore energy poverty on the continent is indeed. Gendered. Indeed there are different gender-action roles in energy production and use. Women and girls bear a disproportionate burden of energy poverty. Therefore women and men experience energy poverty differently. Women and girls are forced to travel long distances for firewood and charcoal, which takes them away from productive activities. Women and girls also have limited access to clean and modern forms of energy. Some of the challenges faced by the majority of African women and girls include: (i) time poverty – that could be used for educational or productive ventures; (ii) lack of access to affordable and reliable electricity, which limits their ability to cook food from cooking with traditional biomass; and (iii) safety issues encountered while gathering firewood. Rural women are also more likely than men to be responsible for collecting firewood and other traditional biomass fuels such as wood, charcoal and agricultural waste.

Lack of access to energy sources for lighting, heating, cooking, transport and economic production inhibits the ability of women in Africa to participate fully in society at large. Yet women – as both consumers and suppliers - remain invisible in the energy sector.

In designing energy projects, it is crucial to take into account the realities of the differences in needs, constraints and opportunities between women and men in relation to energy infrastructure and the development of new technologies. This will help ensure that women are not left behind when the development of women-owned small-scale enterprises. That is why the gender action plan will focus on issues such as: (i) increasing access to modern energy services for women; (ii) promoting women’s inclusion especially through gender-responsive TA for LFs or local funds by providing trainings to women and girls in energy-related skills; (iii) ensuring that women are included in the design of credit lines dedicated to PUEs; (iv) appraise and lend to women and men including FIs for energy access projects; (v) ensure that women’s needs are addressed through gender-responsive tendering institutes; (vi) standardized gender-responsive loan document templates; and (vii) design financial products to address the traditional barriers for women-owned small businesses.

# Gender-based violence

In the program's countries, gender-based violence has long been seen as a taboo subject and some of them are even considered "acceptable" by different communities. They are multiple and come in different forms: physical, sexual, psychological and economic. They are also found in all walks of life within the family or outside it.

The main visible manifestations (in the following facts): FGM, gang-rape, touching and sexual harassment, psychological violence (including verbal abuse), domestic violence (in Mauritania), rape and intention to prostitution, deprivation of rights. This list is not exhaustive.

# Outline and process for developing these national level assessments and action plans

The preparation of country-specific gender assessments and gender action plans will follow established Bank's standards and best practices and will be informed by the gender-related specificities which are similar across countries.

The goal of the country-specific gender assessments and gender action plans is to deliver practical benefits to women and facilitate strategic changes in gender relations in the Sabel by providing a tool and mechanism for systematically addressing gender issues at all levels of project design, implementation, and components. The GAP will provide a tangible and explicit accountability framework for gender mainstreaming. The proposed GAP would include indicators, targets, activities, and design features to address gender equality and women's empowerment as well as the identification and measurement of tangible benefits from the project. In addition, the GAP will provide a road map for project implementation, monitoring, and evaluation.

The process to develop the gender action plans is defined below:

## Sub-activities

### Initial Information and wider stakeholder engagement
Conduct consultations with a broad stakeholder base comprising government and non-state actors, including women's organizations, civil society groups, rural households, youth groups, and other marginalized and vulnerable groups. This will provide information on the project activities, resources, responsibilities, and timelines.

### Develop a GAP implementation plan
Identify the key components of the GAP activities, targets, resources, responsibilities, and timelines.

### Baseline Data Collection and Setting Targets
Conduct baseline data collection to inform gender-based targets for the GAP and its monitoring framework. This will include collecting baseline data on gender equality indicators, project outcomes and outputs, and other relevant information.

## Conduct gender analysis
This process of the GAP preparation would include a broad set of activities designed to identify gender access to resources, including land, credit, inputs, and services (access to and control of resources), decision making participation at community-level, gender differentials in income and expenditure, and women's empowerment capacity. This analysis will be supported by data on gender-based access to education, health care, and renewable energy resources, and measure labour-intensity of activities and the extent to which women are a result of access to new technologies and renewable energy.

## Consultations and engagement
The GAP will be developed jointly with the local partners and stakeholders at all levels. Participatory approaches will be adopted to promote full ownership and understanding of the plan and its monitoring framework by all stakeholders, including women's organizations will be consulted in focus groups and other consultations.
