## Climate Change Problem

### Sub-Saharan Africa faces unique and acute challenges in the global fight to mitigate and adapt to climate change.

#### According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), temperature increases in Sub-Saharan Africa are expected to be higher than the global mean temperature increase, impacting health, food security, water resources, and infrastructure. Many countries in this region have populations that do not have access to electricity and must rely on kerosene and diesel generators for lighting, while another 200 million people lack access to clean cooking fuels and technologies, leading to indoor air pollution causing rapid deforestation and dangerous levels of indoor air pollution.

### Energy access companies are playing a major role, but now they find their continued existence in peril, due to the effects of COVID-19.

#### A recent survey found that 52% of energy access companies have experienced revenue declines of over 30%, with 48% reporting losses. The pandemic has led to widespread job cuts and a sharp increase in customer defaults as cash flow dries up. Over half of solar companies report inventory management as a cover concern, while the loss of sector's human capital threatens a downward spiral where service deterioration causes even more customers to stop paying, and inventory stockouts hamper a potential recovery.

### Proposed Intervention

#### The Energy Access Relief Facility ("EARF") is a concessional debt fund that is intended to provide energy access companies with vital liquidity during this crisis, in the form of low-interest, unsecured loans from the Green Climate Fund (GCF) through the GCF's Financial Mechanism Intermediary, Climate CV, which will participate in EARF loans to eligible companies operating in Sub-Saharan Africa.

### Climate change problem

1. Over the past decades, the rising consequences of climate change have become increasingly evident on a global level. However, its impact has been unequally distributed across different regions. In particular, Sub-Saharan Africa has experienced more frequent and intense climate extremes. These negative impacts will continue to increase if immediate action is not taken.
2. According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), Sub-Saharan Africa is expected to be higher than the global mean temperature increase, impacting health, agriculture and food security. With the aim of joining international efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, Sub-Saharan African countries have committed themselves to contributing to the global effort to mitigate greenhouse gases (GHG) emissions that have a direct impact on climate change. The goal is to limit the increase in average surface temperature below 2°C and to pursue efforts to keep it warmer than 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels. This trend also calls for increased financial support from developed countries to support climate action efforts of developing countries.

2. To attenuate the grim consequences of global warming in this region, it is essential to mitigate GHG emissions coming from fossil fuels. In Sub-Saharan Africa, around 53% of the population (548 million people) still lack access to electricity and are forced to resort to carbon-emitting energy alternatives such as kerosene lanterns, biomass stoves, and fossil fuel generators, all of which emit GHGs. The Green Climate Fund (GCF) is committed to supporting countries in this region that offer the fastest and most cost-effective way to reach millions of people who are not connected to the grid with clean energy.

3. The energy access sector, made up of businesses that manufacture, distribute, finance, and/or install solar panels, on-grid microgrids, clean cookstoves, and off-grid solar-powered productive appliances, is crucial to achieve the targets set by Sustainable Development Goal 7 (aiming to ensure universal access to affordable, reliable, sustainable, and modern energy services). In South Africa alone, Eskom had sold more than 180 million off-grid solar units worldwide, providing lighting and other energy services to 420 million people. The off-grid solar home system sector has rapidly grown into a $3 billion market in Sub-Saharan Africa (expected to reach $5 billion by 2020) and is projected to reach 300 million people with basic electricity access by 2030.

4. The growth of the sector has played a major role in reducing the emission of GHGs over the last ten years:
- 78 million metric tonnes of CO2 emissions have been avoided due to off-grid solar replacing kerosene lamps (which emit black carbon) with solar lamps or lanterns, or the equivalent of 19 coal-fired power plants going offline for a year. At least 44% of kerosene lantern users in Sub-Saharan Africa are now using solar-powered alternatives.
- An estimated 270,000 metric tonnes of black carbon are still emitted from kerosene lamps and stoves each year (about 15% of the United States’ CO2 emissions). If all kerosene lamp users were to switch to LED lighting, this would be equivalent to about 4.5 percent of the United States' CO2 emissions and 12 percent of India's. Eliminating these emissions would be equivalent to a five gigatonne CO2e reduction over the next 20 years.
- Off-grid solar home systems microgrids are reducing the use of diesel and petrol generators which are often highly inefficient in the country contexts in which the off-grid sector operates. For example, the collective dependency on and capacity of Nigeria’s grid.

#### Energy access sector is not only reducing CO2 emissions in the atmosphere, but contributing to the improvement of health and education, creating jobs and income opportunities, and helping consumers save money.

**Energy access at risk**

Today, much of this momentum of GHG emissions mitigation is at risk due to the economic and business impacts of C-19:

- Sales growth has slowed and revenues are down, but due to customers' inability to pay, stemming from job losses related to shelter-in-place restrictions caused by the virus.
- Financial institutions have been forced to cut back on lending in response to reduced demand and competing public health and economic support priorities for development finance institutions.

**A drop-off in revenues and an inability to raise new funds compels companies to rely on their cash reserves.**

But in a nascent, growing sector, few companies have long financial runways. According to a survey by the Energy Access Industry Association (EnAEE), 85% cannot survive more than 5 months if current conditions prevail. From this total, 27% have had a severe disruption, forcing them to significantly curtail operations. 43% of companies with revenues below $100 million have seen their revenues drop by at least half; 53% of companies that offer consumer finance have seen repayment drops by more than a quarter.

## Energy Access Relief Facility

The COVID Energy Access Relief Facility (EARF) is a sector-wide response to the global COVID-19 pandemic and its resultant economic downturn. The EARF’s goal is to provide liquidity to energy access companies that are attempting to weather the crisis, while also providing them with the resources they need to adapt their business models to achieve their goals. To achieve this goal, the EARF intends to issue concessional loans to energy access companies that are both:

- Combating climate change, either through enabling low-income households to mitigate emissions by switching to cleaner energy sources, or by helping low-income households adopt a new demand on their fuel consumption patterns.
- Demonstrating a clear need for liquidity support caused by the COVID-19 crisis.

This financing is intended to provide companies with the necessary liquidity to continue their operations and retain their workforce. It will also enable them to maintain their supply chain and inventory so they can grow post-crisis. Over 500 companies have now expressed interest in benefiting from the fund, demonstrating a strong demand for support.

Eligible companies are expected to apply through the EARF website and meet the fund’s eligibility criteria. Please see Schedule 1 of the Term Sheet on “Key terms of the Loan” for finalized eligibility criteria.

Any company that can apply must meet these criteria and is operating in the member countries of the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD) or the International Development Association (IDA). The majority of indicative requests (~74%) came from African companies. The focus of EARF will be on Sub-Saharan Africa and Asia, with GCF partnerships being considered for other regions. In addition to these criteria, there may also be additional regional carve-outs from other contributing entities in the EARF that have geographic relevance to their respective proceeds.

### EARF Structure and Supervision

The EARF is born from the collaboration of major donors and investors in the energy access sector, which formed the COVID Energy Access Action Network and its Steering Committee to catalyze action in the face of the obvious need for support. The EARF will be a separate legal entity with its own Board of Directors, funded by a Dutch foundation. It will be governed by a Board of Directors, Advisory Board consisting of EARF investors who opt-in to serve on an observer list, the Facility Manager (IFC), and an Investment Committee to make lending decisions.

The investment is expected to be provided through an intermediary, Clean CV, which will be a Dutch limited partnership. The Clean CV will enter into a Loan Agreement with each eligible company (LPC) and then enter into a Master Participation Agreement with the EARF. Clean CV participations will only be on loans to countries where the EARF has been accredited as a lending entity. This means that Clean CV will not participate in providing loans to borrowers operating in countries in which NoIs have been obtained, as the EARF may have a larger geographic target. Accruals will be the accredited lender, and will also invest directly into the EARF. SBA will act as the General Partner (GP) in Clean CV. The final structure will be set out on the eventual Term Sheet.

Final investment decisions and the decision to act on overdrawn payments will be made by an Investment Committee (see fig.).

1. The sub-Saharan Africa region experiences more frequent and intense climate extremes than other regions in the world. This is predicted to continue. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) projects that by 2050, the average temperature increase for this region will be higher than the global mean temperature increase during the next century, impacting health, agriculture, and water resources.

2. The region also faces tremendous development challenges. In sub-Saharan Africa, 548 million people, or 53 per cent of the total population, lack access to electricity and rely on the expensive use of kerosene for lighting. This is equivalent to 109 million households. In addition, 609 million people, or 85 per cent of the population, lack access to clean cooking fuels and technologies compared with rapid urbanization due to the increased use of biomass and indoor air pollution.

3. The energy access sector has shown remarkable progress worldwide. The sector is composed of companies that manufacture, distribute, finance, and/or install home systems, microgrids, clean cookstoves and/or solar-powered productive appliances. The off-grid solar energy market has grown at an average annual rate of 20 per cent from 2015 to 2019. By February 2020 the sector had sold more than 180 million off-grid solar home systems, with a total installed capacity of approximately 370 million kilowatts in sub-Saharan Africa alone (representing about 40 per cent of the global market). The off-grid solar home system sector has grown rapidly into a USD 1.75 billion annual market that supports 370,000 jobs. The sector is expected to provide 380 million people with access to electricity by 2030.

4. The contribution of the energy access sector to climate change mitigation is significant, especially in sub-Saharan Africa where off-grid solar and microgrids are dominant. Off-grid solar energy and microgrids powered with renewable resources reduce the use of diesel and petrol generators, which are often inefficient and expensive. It is estimated that 74 million tonnes of CO2 emissions could be avoided by 2030 through the widespread adoption of off-grid solar energy technology. This reduction is estimated to be the equivalent of taking approximately 16 million cars off the road.

## Long-term and sustainable development of clean energy access in sub-Saharan Africa is at risk due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Many energy access companies are struggling with limited resources. ### The Global Off-Grid Solar Market Report 2019 (GOGGA) is a debt fund to provide energy access companies with vital liquidity funding in the form of low-interest loans.
