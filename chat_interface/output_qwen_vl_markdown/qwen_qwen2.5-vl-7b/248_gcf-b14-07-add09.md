### A.2 Project | Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

Madagascar is one of the most vulnerable countries to climate change in the world [1]. Home to vast numbers of extremely vulnerable smallholder farmers, Madagascar faces significant challenges related to food security and poverty due to its exposure to extreme weather events.

The Project goal is to implement sustainable landscape measures to enhance resilience of smallholders, reduce GHG emissions and increase access to low-emissions energy. The project will also support the development of a national climate change adaptation strategy. The Project aims to demonstrate a replicable model for addressing smallholder vulnerability that mobilizes both the public and private sectors. Not only will this help to improve food security and livelihoods, but it will also reduce poverty and dependency on international aid.

The Project goal and innovative model make it attractive for private investors and will leverage private sector investment in climate change adaptation projects. The project is expected to generate 10 MCO2e of reduced emissions, a 33 MW capacity of renewable energy generation, 562 direct beneficiaries, including 114,000 with improved resilience and 488,000 with access to low-emissions energy, and 1 million indirect beneficiaries.

The public and private sector interventions will make major contributions towards Madagascar's adaptation and mitigation priorities as laid out in the National Climate Change Strategy. The project is expected to generate 10 MCO2e of reduced emissions, a 33 MW capacity of renewable energy generation, 562 direct beneficiaries, including 114,000 with improved resilience and 488,000 with access to low-emissions energy, and 1 million indirect beneficiaries.

One needs to access a different type of financing and especially longer term: lower interest rates (currently, for example, financing that covers 12 or 18 months at an average of 15% vs. 25% p.a.). PPS offers the Borrower a lower nominal interest rate (4% to 6%) but the Borrower agrees to share a portion of the profit with the Lender, allowing commercial interest-bearing parties.

On top of that, the GCF has a unique approach to financing projects: it does not require any upfront payment of funds and the nature of revenue (for instance, commodity exports are invoiced in USD, so US$ borrowing is more appropriate). As resources of the Fund are limited, the US$ will be used for TNC not related to MDGs. Borrowings will be put on a 10-year maturity.

As shown in annex 4, the proposed project has an internal rate of return (IRR) of 25% and an expected annualized Return on Investment (ROI), which corresponds to an estimated US$14 million in profit, net of fees. The lower IRR is based on two main reasons:

* Targeting a higher proportion of climate change impacts (both adaptation and mitigation), environmental and social impacts over financial profitability and thereby aims at disbursing a larger-than-usual portion of the value creation from the project to the environment and society.
* A focus on projects that are likely to have lower than average cost per tonne of CO2 emission reductions (see sections E.1 and F for more details about expected impacts). These will not be sold to voluntary or compliance markets, but rather used as a means to achieve the GCF’s climate change mitigation and adaptation goals.

It is important to note that this proposal has been built on a series of assumptions explained in annex 2, and the portfolio levels are scaled up from three example investments indicative of deals that exist in the pipeline. The actual level made will be determined by the final investment decision and will be based on the performance relative to expectations.

In this proposal mid-case scenarios are presented. Sensitivity analysis can be found in section E.4.3.

## GREEN CLIMATE FUND FUNDING PROPOSAL (PAGE 8 OF 82)

Access to credit is expensive and limited in Madagascar and there is no financing available at or below the cost of risk for climate-related activities such as sustainable land management, conservation, and access to energy. There are two main sources of financing for the private sector in Madagascar:

1. **Banking Sector:** Only 30% of Malagasy households (who hold 84% of total system assets) but only offer basic savings and credit products to a select clientele. Credit tends to be short- to medium-term in nature, with a maximum term of one year. The banking sector is dominated by foreign-owned banks, which have significant market power.

2. **Access to Financial Services:** By 2011 only 45 out of every 1000 Malagasy were a depositor at a commercial bank and only 16 out of every 1000 was a borrower. In 2016, the World Bank's Doing Business ranked Madagascar the 167th country and 189th in terms of ease of access to finance.

**Commercial banks are mainly serving private small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), excluding farmers and community organizations, and usually bear a high interest rate that prevents long-term investment into transformational changes of the economy.**

### Private Equity

There are eight active private equity investors in Madagascar: five of which are solely focused on the island while three have a broader focus. The private equity sector is still in its infancy in Madagascar with only $10 million invested capital in Madagascar*. They mostly invest in venture capital and growth capital, in fast-growing sectors such as tourism, agribusiness, and renewable energy. Private equity investors are not interested in investing in the Investment Fund (community-based sustainable agriculture production and access to energy) because of lack of knowledge as well as the fact that it is a new instrument for them. The private equity sector is also not interested in the proposed financial instrument*. Following meetings with members of the AMC, however there could be potential cooperation between the AMC and private equity investors. This would be particularly interesting when the Investment Fund finances the upstream of the supply chain (cooperatives of farmers, community organizations, etc.) and the downstream (retailers, hotels, restaurants).

In addition to the above, Foreign Direct Investment inflows in Madagascar have strongly declined as an effect of the global crisis and especially the political instability that has characterized the country since 2014. Despite the democratic election of Hery Rajaonarimampianina as President in 2014, investment levels have not yet recovered. In terms of its business environment, Madagascar is ranked 158th out of 190 countries by the World Bank*, highlighting the fact that significant private investment is unlikely to flow without dedicated mechanisms to reduce these risks.

# DETAILED PROJECT/PROGRAMME DESCRIPTION

## C.1 Strategic Context

This section on strategic context serves as a baseline from which the objectives of the project have been defined.

Madagascar is already one of the most vulnerable countries to climate related risks[1] and climate change projections threaten to exacerbate these vulnerabilities, particularly in relation to food security[2]. The country has a high population density, a highly vulnerable and food insecure population of smallholder farmers[3], deforestation leading to increased GHG emissions and degradation of biodiversity[4], and limited access to renewable energy sources such as carbon neutral and land-neutral energy sources (e.g. renewables) and [a] lack of capital to invest in aforementioned sectors.

### Climate and Project Context

Madagascar is a tropical country, with the southern being warm and while the east is cold. Humid average temperatures vary from 23-27°C on the coast and 10-19°C in the highlands. The rainfall in the Antananarivo-Zahamena Forest Corridor (considered by the French atmospheric scientist, Alfred Wegener as a "hotbed of climate change") is highly variable with an average annual precipitation of approximately 2500 mm per year with variation all year round. Rainfall in these landscapes is driven by the seasonally trade winds meeting the uplands. Both influence the amount and distribution of rainfall across Madagascar.

#### Climate and Project Context

Observational evidence shows that the minimum temperature in Madagascar has been consistently increasing over the last 75 years, while the south temperatures have been steadily increasing since the 1950s (2°C warmer in 2000[4] than in 2000) and drought has become more frequent. The average annual rainfall is projected to decrease by approximately 3% over the next century.

Future temperature changes in Madagascar have been projected using a regional climate model based on 13 global climate models (GCMs). The results show that the mean annual temperature will increase between 2-4°C, with an increase of approximately 5.5°C. Changes in precipitation were predicted based on its downscaled GCM and the projected median rainfall will increase by about 7% over the next century.

The climate change projections for Madagascar are as follows:

* **Warmer temperatures:** The average temperature is expected to rise between 2-4°C, with a projected increase of approximately 5.5°C.
* **Increased precipitation:** Projected median rainfall will increase by about 7% over the next century.
* **More frequent droughts:** Drought has become more frequent and is expected to continue.

### Climate Vulnerability

A climate vulnerable and food insecure population of smallholder farmers across the tropics, smallholder farmers already face many challenges. Climate change is expected to disproportionately affect smallholder farmers in developing countries[5]. Smallholder farmers are particularly sensitive to climate change because they are often semi-nomadic or entirely nomadic subsistence farmers who are particularly vulnerable to any shocks owing to their high dependence on agriculture for their livelihoods, chronic food insecurity, physical isolation and lack of access to markets. A study by the World Bank[6] found that 75% of households in forested landscapes in Madagascar reported that at least one adult member of the household had experienced a drought within the last five years.

### Deforestation leading to increased GHG emissions and degradation of ecosystem services

Madagascar has suffered significant deforestation and forest fragmentation over the last 50 years in large part due to unsustainable slash-and-burn agriculture (locally known as "ayoy") used by smallholder farmers. National forest cover decreased by almost 40 percent from 1973 to 2008, with an estimated loss of 6 million hectares of natural forests over this period. The rate of deforestation has slowed down since the mid-1990s and is now at a low level (representing only about one-third of the annual deforestation rates in the 1970s). However, Madagascar continues to experience significant forest degradation as a major source of the national GHG emissions but relatively little progress has been made towards improving forest management. The country's forests are also under threat from illegal logging and conversion for agriculture (e.g., slash-and-burn practices), particularly the continued use of "ayoy". As a result, Madagascar has severe problems with soil erosion that further aggravates the problem of deforestation and degradation of its ecosystems.

### Limited access to affordable renewable energy

Renewable sources of energy are also essential for sustainable economic development for both the rural and urban populations of Madagascar. The country's electricity sector is dominated by thermal power plants, which supply only 10% of the total demand in urban areas. In addition, recent years there has been little investment in the sector and the state-run electricity company, GED, has not been able to meet the growing demand for electricity. As a result, many rural households are still without access to electricity. The government has therefore launched several initiatives aimed at increasing energy access in Madagascar, including the National Energy Strategy (2013-2025), the National Electrification Plan and the National Agency for Rural Electrification (ADER). It has been established that Madagascar has improved energy potential from biomass, geothermal sources, solar and wind, and ADER has identified a portfolio of potential projects that can be developed with adequate financing.

### Private Sector Investment

As noted above, there are very few opportunities to access financing for climate-related conservation activities and for sustainable agriculture businesses. Viable, profitable businesses that generate wealth and employment are key to the long-term success of business models aimed at supporting rural livelihoods in Madagascar. However, private sector investment is constrained by a lack of private capital willing to take on the risks involved in Malagasy investments for the returns that are available. This issue is further compounded by the fact that many smallholder farmers have limited access to education and training as well as low education levels among the population and high climate-related risk (see section 6) and Annex B for further discussion of risks related to investment in agriculture). The lack of private sector interest in investing in rural development is also a challenge to note because of the low spending power of rural consumers and their history of low cost use of credit (if sometimes unsustainable) even when they have access to it. This means that many smallholder farmers are not able to take advantage of new technologies or to be rich and not easily accessible and profitable business models largely non-existent. Therefore a new type of investment model is needed for the private sector in Madagascar, one which can provide the necessary capital to support rural livelihoods while also taking the high levels of risk associated without necessarily being able to provide high rates of return. A pioneering Fund of this nature has been established by ADER and the World Bank in Madagascar with the aim of providing a platform on which proven, sustainable enterprises begin to scale and onto other country-wide factors such as political stability and infrastructure improve.

### Issues related to climate change

The government of Madagascar is committed to reducing its greenhouse gas emissions. The country's efforts are focused on increasing forest cover through reforestation and afforestation activities, improving land use management practices in rural Madagascar and building resilient households and communities. Appropriate and targeted investment in agriculture can support these efforts by promoting sustainable agricultural production.

Finally, as described in section B4, Madagascar lacks available capital for private investment to support climate-related activities, especially community-based sustainable land use production. The financial instrument offered to communities and farmers organized under the National Electrification Plan is not sufficient to promote a significant change of land use as well as to drive further replication throughout the country and countries with similar challenges.

### Sustainable Agriculture

Although 86% of rural livelihoods and 54% of urban jobs are in the agriculture, livestock and fisheries sector, its contribution to national production has been declining. The agricultural sector's share of GDP has averaged about 10%, while its share of employment has averaged about 1.5% per year (although modest advances were made in the 2003-2009 period). While the sector has contracted since the late 1980s, it is still a major employer and source of income for many rural households. The agricultural sector employs more than one-third of the population but it provides insufficient remuneration to give a way out from the grinding poverty that characterizes rural Madagascar (especially in the south). This situation has led to widespread migration of young people to urban areas, where they are often caught up in drug abuse and prostitution and a general decline in the quality of life.

Madagascar has been unable to attract direct foreign investment into its agricultural sector due to difficulties posed by high levels of rural poverty, lack of infrastructure, under land tenure, unstable government, and a high exposure to climate related disasters. The sector has had limited access to credit and inputs, and is characterized by low productivity and poor quality products. It produces a wide range of crops (e.g. clove, vanilla, essential oils, cocoa beans, sugar, beans, pepper, coffee, etc.) produced for export or also diverse, processed and manufactured products such as chocolate, soap, perfumes, etc.

### Energy

Improved access to energy is a priority in Madagascar with the country ranked roughly 188 out of 189 countries for access to electricity. Madagascar has a total installed capacity of approximately 200 MW, which is primarily through increased use of renewable technology, improved efficiency and improved stores (see Section E5 below for details).

Throughout the country, heating and cooking are predominantly fueled by wood and charcoal (80% of the population cook with charcoal or in the event of a shortage, wood). The majority of women spend up to 12 hours per week collecting firewood. This is particularly predominant for those who are women who are burdened with time spent on collection and suffer high rates of respiratory complications due to the poor quality of smoke from burning wood and charcoal. In addition, the use of wood and charcoal as a fuel source has led to deforestation in Madagascar. The Ministry of Environment, Forestry and Water (MINEF) and the World Bank are launching a new program that aims to take out of the use of ethanol stoves at a large scale by attracting investment in the supply chain (hardware, distribution, etc.) and training for users.

Only five percent of the rural population has access to electricity. Those that do manage their energy from diesel power plants in isolated areas or small hydroelectric projects. The cost per kilowatt-hour is very high ($0.35-$1.20) compared with $0.04-$0.06 US$/kWh). Since 2007, there has been a shift to include renewable energy projects with about nine small hydroelectric plants, five wind power plants and one solar plant in the pipeline. The government is also working on a number of projects with international NGOs. To date solar power has only been used for electrification of some social infrastructure and for single households, mainly in the rural areas.

The key barriers to access modern energy services in rural areas include:

* Lack of grid extension: There are plans to extend existing grids in urban or more densely populated areas within the coming decade.
* The Ministry of Energy and Water (MINEW) and the power distribution company Agence Nationale d'Electricité have a fraction of the budget enjoyed by the national power utility company (approximately 10/80%) and this has been restricted further since the 2009 political crisis, to the extent that it is now unable to finance private sector initiatives to raise capital to do finance rural electrification projects.
* Very high purchasing power of rural customers, which can conflict with the relatively high costs of renewable energy per kWh, due to costly imported equipment, etc.

## Outcome 1: Strengthened adaptive capacity and reduced exposure to climate risks (GCF ref. A7.0)

This outcome of the Project will address adaptive capacity within landscapes by promoting sustainable agriculture and introducing innovative approaches for reducing emissions from deforestation and forest degradation, while also strengthening relationships with the CAZ and CAFD landholders and using a gender-sensitive farmer participatory approach to identify climate risks and location-specific adaptation measures.

The Project will work closely with local communities, including women’s groups, to ensure that they are fully involved in the participatory planning; important habitats (e.g. vegetation providing erosion control, forest areas around water catchments), etc.) are protected and managed sustainably; and that the landscape approach is integrated into all aspects of project activities.

This outcome addresses the need for information and training on climate change. Only a limited number of institutional investors are able to directly invest in structures such as the Athelais Madagascar Investment Fund. Their fiduciary duties mainly justify the high weight that such institutional investors allocate to bond investments, which on average constitute 30% of their total assets. # E.4. Needs of the Recipient

## Vulnerabilities and needs of the beneficiary country and population

### E.4.1. Vulnerability of the beneficiary country (country level)

Madagascar is one of the most vulnerable countries to climate change in the world. For example, the risk analysis company Verisk Maplecroft ranked Madagascar as the 5th most vulnerable country in the World to climate change in its 2012 report[3]. This country has a high vulnerability due to its low adaptive capacity and exposure to extreme weather events such as cyclones, droughts, floods, and heatwaves. The projected increase of temperature and decrease of rainfall will exacerbate these climate impacts (Connan, Knutl and Eickmeier, 2014). Smallholder farmers – who constitute 83% of Madagascar’s population – are particularly vulnerable to the adverse effects of climate change because they have limited access to resources. They are highly dependent on natural resources, their access to technical support and credit are limited and their poverty rates are high (Hoff et al., 2014). Smallholder farmers in Madagascar face a number of challenges such as lack of education, limited access to credit or technical support, dependence on male relatives for access to land, and farming the most marginal land. In addition, they have limited access to markets.

There is an urgent need to identify and implement sufficient and efficient adaptation strategies that improve the resilience of households and smallholder farmers and their ability to cope with climate change impacts. Especially those that can also provide mitigation benefits.

Smallholder farmers in Madagascar have a variety of traditional strategies for coping with the impacts of extreme weather events such as cyclones, droughts, floods, and heatwaves. These strategies include crop diversification, intercropping, and other practices that improve overall resilience to climate change. Traditional coping strategies for agricultural production loss or food insecurity include consuming stored food, selling livestock, borrowing money from relatives or friends, selling small livestock or working as agricultural wage laborers on others’ farms (Tadros et al., 2008). However, these traditional coping mechanisms are not sufficient to cope with extreme weather events (Tadros et al. 2008) that will come with climate change. In addition, traditional coping strategies often degrade the environment and can lead to further degradation of natural resources.

Increasing the productivity and resilience of smallholder farming systems under climate change is a huge challenge that will require significant and sustained technical, financial and political support and action at both the local and national level.

This project aims to address these challenges by providing technical assistance for the development, monitoring, and evaluation of climate-smart landscapes strategies and measures, as well as renewable energy development, through training workshops in Madagascar during the first year of the project period.

### E.4.2. Financial, economic, social and institutional needs

Madagascar has one of the highest poverty rates in Africa and the world. Madagascar’s Human Development Index (HDI) is ranked as 154 out of 184 countries assessed in 2013 and GDP is 175 out of 184 countries. Average per capita income is only US$460 per year[4]. The poverty rate has been decreasing from 90% to 77% over the past decade, but still remains high at 87 percent in 2015, indicating that the burden of poverty that leaves people vulnerable to economic, social and environmental shocks is still very high. This is further exacerbated by the fact that women are more likely than men to be poor (UNDP, 2013). Women also bear a disproportionate share of responsibilities such as care for children and elderly, gathering water and fuelwood, and household agriculture, while further burdened with unpaid domestic work.

The population of the CAFZ and CAOF landscapes has the same predicament as described in section E.4.1 for smallholder farmers throughout the island. By targeting the most vulnerable households in the landscapes the project aims to lift them out of a situation of dependency on external aid, improve their access to markets and information and engage more successfully with markets, including accessing finance such as that provided by the Investment Fund.

Nevertheless, they are designed in a way to maximize sustainability, for example by anchoring them within the broader national development agenda and by ensuring that they are self-sustaining beyond the life of the Project.

The commercial agriculture activities are expected to be self-sustaining (see financial models in Annexes 3a and further discussion in section F.1) from the perspective of the beneficiary communities. The illustrative example indicates five-year internal rate of return of 14%, which is considered a good return for this type of investment. This is achieved by taking into account the fact that it takes time to build up production volumes, and that the time lag between production and sales and the lack of formal market mechanisms to support prices, both input and sale, can further reduce profitability.

With regards to both agricultural activities and the Investment Fund, without the support of the GCF it would not be possible to raise the required capital for these investments.

# H.2 Arrangements for Monitoring, Reporting and Evaluation

The co-executing entities will report regularly to their respective Accredited Entities following the terms of their respective subsidiary agreements. The Project will also report on its progress against the project milestones as outlined in Section H1 above.

The Project will establish a Monitoring, Reporting and Evaluation unit as part of the Project Management Unit. This unit will have the dual purpose of ensuring that the indicators are tracked and reported using appropriate and rigorous methodologies and protocols. It will also provide support to the Project Management Unit (PMU) in making project management decisions by providing data on progress against impact outcomes, output and activity will be collected.

The M&E plan will be designed to provide data to help the PMU monitor and manage the project effectively. The M&E plan will be developed at project start-up and included in the Project Management Plan (PMP). It will include a description of how the indicators will be tracked and reported, as well as the management approach to be used to keep the project on track. CI headquarters units and the EIB will provide support to the monitoring and evaluation process.

All data collected under the M&E plan will be stored in an electronic database (M&E database) that is accessible by all relevant stakeholders. The M&E database will be designed to allow for easy access, analysis and reporting of the data. All data and records will be stored in a secure and retrievable manner. All documents and records will be kept for at least two years after the project end date.

A detailed Monitoring and Evaluation plan will be developed at project inception in accordance with the AIMs to be signed by the Project Co-Executing Entities, CI and EIB. The M&E plan will include a description of how the indicators will be tracked and reported as well as the management approach to be used to keep the project on track.

## Terms of Reference

There will be three evaluations as part of the M&E plan: one in Year 3; another in Year 6, and a final evaluation in Year 10. The terms of reference for these evaluations will be developed by the CI/GIC unit in collaboration with the EIB. These evaluations will include review of progress against impact outcomes, outputs and activities as well as an assessment of the effectiveness of project management practices.

The M&E plan will also include a framework to support the analysis of data collected under the M&E database. This framework will be developed by the CI/GIC unit in collaboration with the EIB. The framework will allow for easy access to the data and will provide guidance on how to use this data effectively. The framework will be designed to ensure that all stakeholders can view the data used in the analysis.

An integrated monitoring tool for this project will be used to demonstrate the impact of the interventions in the target landscapes. CI/GIC will develop a dashboard that will allow users to drill down into the data collected under the M&E database and use it to track progress towards the project goals, impacts and outcomes, facilitate effective and adaptive management, and provide more detailed information on the landscape level.

The dashboard will be designed as an interactive tool with three main sections: 1) a map that shows the location of each landscape; 2) a unique tool, a scorecard that captures underlying trends observed at the landscape level; an interactive dashboard that "drills down" into the data collected under the M&E database and provides more detailed information on the landscape level. The framework will be built around three criteria (natural capital, production and human well-being) and the indicators to be used will be based on local circumstances and tailored to the needs of the project.
