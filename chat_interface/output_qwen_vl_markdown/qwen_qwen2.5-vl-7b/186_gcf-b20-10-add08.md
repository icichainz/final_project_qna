# Climate Strategy

AFD was one of the first international donors to integrate the fight against climate change into its practices. This approach, initiated more than a decade ago, is based on the principle that the fight against climate change must be integrated in all aspects of development policies of countries, in terms of opportunities and threats, as well as the involvement of ecosystems and natural resources in the development process.

Since then and in line with its national positioning and its involvement in ecological and climatic issues, AFD has developed a strategy to support the fight against climate change through climate finance. The AFD Group has thoroughly adjusted its intervention strategy and commitment to climate change, which is now fully integrated into all of its operations, designed to provide climate co-benefits.

AFD's Climate Strategy is based on a strong model of its identity, with three main aspects: (i) systematically measuring the carbon footprint of the operations financed (carbon balance), (ii) committing to a quantitative and qualitative commitment in terms of climate change, (iii) an ambitious quantitative commitment to financing 50% of projects with climate co-benefits. This strategy is also based on the principle that it must be adapted according to the level of development of the countries concerned. This strategy has profoundly transformed AFD's approach to climate issues, both in terms of operations financed and also shaped its partnership strategy.

This strategy was developed in close collaboration with the AFD Group's various stakeholders at all national and international levels. It is actively involved in most major initiatives undertaken with its peers and civil society actors. AFD was particularly involved in issues of accounting for climate change, the development of a carbon balance methodology, economic modeling, and issues of strategic integration of climate dimensions into financial analysis.

The new international momentum, transcribed in the SDGs and the Paris Agreement, requires AFD to support fundamental and rapid changes in the development models of countries and territories.

Moreover, through the setting up of its Climate Plan in July 2017, the French government wished to reinforce the role of AFD as a major player in climate change financing at the international level. This provides AFD with a framework that extends its mandate by allowing it to support the implementation of the Paris Agreement and to continue to implement the Paris Agreement across its entire portfolio. AFD must therefore continue the transformation of methods and instruments, launched within the framework of its previous climate strategies.

Three strategic challenges have been identified concerning the role and place of AFD in climate change financing: (i) ensuring that all activities are consistent with the Paris Agreement, in support of low-carbon and climate-resilient development and related public policies; (ii) increasing the volume of climate finance provided by AFD through leverage effects; and strengthening AFD's role as a platform for France's international financial commitment to climate and its positioning as a reference on climate and development issues.

The new AFD Climate and Development Strategy for 2017-2022 thus proposes four major commitments: (i) ensure activities are 100% Paris Agreement compatible, (ii) increase the volume of climate finance provided by AFD through leverage effects, (iii) support co-build solutions and bring influence to bear on standards.

### A.2. Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

Delivering the large investment flows needed to address climate change can only be achieved by leveraging the resources of the private sector. The Green Climate Fund (GCF) is committed to supporting the private sector in developing countries, can play a key role in financing this transition. However, market barriers continue to hold back investment at scale, including insufficient access to finance, low internal capacity and a limited pipeline of bankable projects.

The TSGF Program aims to engage LFPs to scale up private sector climate finance. The Program builds on over a decade of experience in implementing the AFU SURENEF initiative (Sustainable Use of Natural Resources and Energy Finance) in 30 countries.

The goal of the Program is to create a market for investments in climate technologies in 17 countries by removing the financial barriers that are often faced by LFPs to enable borrowing, mainly, from the private sector. The Program will facilitate project funding (through credit lines and guarantee mechanisms) in the target countries and will develop the capacity of LFPs and project developers to scale up climate finance.

The project will deliver GHG reductions estimated at 46 million tCO2e over the 20-year lifetime of the mitigation investments, with an additional 1.3 million tCO2e avoided by the adaptation activities (the total number of direct beneficiaries is 1.3 million). Co-benefits include green jobs and business growth (1,100 SMEs). The total project cost is EUR 892 million, of which EUR 330 million of GCF financing and EUR 552 million of co-financing from AFD. The GCF share will be used to finance the mitigation activities in the target countries. The adaptation activities in these areas will leverage additional private sector financing of approximately EUR 1099 million (EUR 315 million from LFPs and EUR 784 million as equity/loans) on both mitigation and adaptation.

### Financial Markets Overview (if applicable)

Despite countries' commitment to fight against climate change in their NDC and a number of initiatives supported by GCF, the lack of climate finance is still a major barrier. This is mainly due to the presence of a number market barriers that hold back investment at scale. # C.1 Strategic Context

To address climate change, the international community is converging towards a common agenda that recognizes the need to build a model with a reduced carbon footprint in order to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals and the objectives of the Paris Agreement.

Historically, the Paris Agreement marks the recognition by countries in the global North and South of the need to change current development models. These long-term objectives have been defined in the Article 2: keep the increase in the global average temperature to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and pursue efforts to limit the warming to 1.5°C, recognizing that this would significantly reduce the risks and impacts of climate change; increase the ability to adapt to the adverse impacts of climate change and foster climate resilience and sustainable development.

The achievement of these objectives concerns all Parties and, even in a context of differentiated responsibilities, a sharp reduction of emissions will be required in many AFD intervention countries. At the same time, many countries in which AFD operates are the most vulnerable to the effects of climate change.

In addition to the Agreement, one of the key results of COP21 is that all countries have published their nationally determined contributions (NDCs) and communicated them to the UNFCCC. These NDCs represent a commitment to climate change. While these were indeed very crucial initial national references in terms of emissions targets, the targets defined by each country are not yet sufficient for the achievement of the 2°C target (reduction deficit of 15-17 Gt of CO2 equivalent per year in 2030), which would lead to a warming of 3.8–3.9°C.

Local financial institutions have a key role to play in scaling up climate finance for local private investments. However, despite the dynamism and profitability of these sectors, investors still face numerous obstacles: lack of awareness on the environmental and social impacts of their activities; lack of information about the availability of public or corporate funding, etc. Contributing to removing these technical and financial barriers would allow these institutions to finance transformational change towards more climate-resilient development.

Therefore, AFD has for many years been engaged in an active partnership with many LFPs, notably through the SUNREF initiative launched to promote climate change mitigation and adaptation investments in developing countries. AFD has launched the SUNREF initiative in 2006. To date, 50 SUNREF project have been selected by AFD in 31 countries, for a total commitment of over EUR 2 billion of loans allocated by AFD, including EUR 1.8bn already disbursed.

SUNREF has been a successful “green finance” label: it is an innovative integrated approach to environmental finance through a combination of financial and technical support. It provides a range of tools and services for this, which are increasingly being used in other contexts (e.g., energy efficiency, agroforestry). SUNREF aims at providing financial support to structure green investments, assistance in the implementation of an environmental and social management system (ESMS), policy, and valorisation of the results achieved (e.g. energy savings, afforestation, CO2 emissions avoided, etc.).

Building on this experience, AFD wishes to take the SUNREF initiative further by increasing and diversifying its scope worldwide. More specifically, the aim of the Programme is to redirect financial flows towards more diversified investments in developing countries, with a particular focus on:

### Vulnerability Baseline and Objective of Change
According to the ND-GAIN index ranking, the target countries are in the first quartile in terms of resilience, i.e., among the most vulnerable countries (Mauritius, Burundi, Burkina Faso, Cameroon, Côte d'Ivoire, Kenya, Madagascar, Nigeria, Tanzania, Togo, and Uganda – ND-GAIN, 2016). Most of the target countries are therefore severely vulnerable to climate change, which means these are potential opportunities for the private sector in terms of adaptation investments. ### Key Barriers and Challenges
Depending on the local context, a number of obstacles can hold back the implementation of climate investments. These are:
- **Policy and regulatory barriers:** lack of legal structures and systems to push the market and open up the sector; lack of climate and environmental regulation and legal binding targets which is often a hindrance to private sector investment in the energy sector.
- **Financial barriers:** high perceived risk of climate-related investments, limited or inappropriate financial instruments for long-term projects, lack of access to finance, and insufficient capacity of financial institutions to support their long-term benefits; lack of attractiveness of climate finance resources; lack of financing structures and systems to support private sector investment in the energy sector.
- **Technical barriers:** limited access to technology; insufficient experience of LFPs and private sector companies in originating commercially viable climate-related projects, lack of technical knowledge among LFPs and private sector companies on how to identify and develop adaptation and mitigation projects, and limited awareness among LFPs and private sector companies about the benefits of climate-resilient infrastructure investments.

# Background information on ARIZ

ARIZ is AFD’s guarantee mechanism providing MSMEs with access to finance. Created in 2007, ARIZ offers partial risk coverage to financial institutions (FI) guaranteeing their MSME loan portfolios. It thus supports the development of loan facilities for MSMEs and contributes to the diversification of financing sources available to these enterprises.

The financing gap MSMEs are confronted with is covered by a shared loss mechanism between AFD and the FI: in case of default at payment by the creditor, the FI receives a first payment (advance payment) representing 25% of its exposure. If the loan remains unpaid after this advance payment period, the FI receives an additional payment during the collection period. If the loan is still not repaid at the end of the collection period, a final payment (balance payment) is made to the financial institution, taking into account the collaterals which could be realized.

ARIZ offers eligible FIs either a single deal guarantee scheme or a portfolio guarantee scheme in EUR, USD, or the respective local currency.
- The ARIZ Individual Guarantee is granted on a loan-by-loan basis. Based on the credit file produced by the FI, the AFD will accept sub-participation of up to 50% with a maximum amount not exceeding € 2 M EUR over a tenure of 1-2½ years.

The ARIZ Portfolio guarantee is a partial guarantee (up to 50%) on a pre-defined future loan portfolio of the FI. Over a period of 2 years and up to a pre-defined portfolio guarantee amount, all loans granted by the bank are automatically guaranteed in full up to a set equivalent credit limit: 1-7 years loan maturity, 10-300 K EUR loan amount, as well as other criteria (e.g. sector, geographical location, etc.).

The guarantees will be granted in local currency or, if requested by the LFP, in EUR or USD. This implies that if a guarantee is claimed, the payment to the LFP will be made in the local currency as well. Therefore, in case of a guarantee provided in local currency, the counter-guarantee will cover the equivalent amount in EUR or USD.

AFD has established an ARIZ partnership with over 100 financial institutions. The mechanism is available in 39 countries and territories (including all types of operations) across Africa, Asia, Latin America, but not in the Caribbean (AFD has local agencies in Haiti and the Dominican Republic), Southeast Asia, and the Mediterranean Region.

Since 2007, AFD has issued more than 85M EUR of guarantees. In total, 7,500 loans have been guaranteed with an average loan amount of €13K.

Thanks to SUNREF, the Medine Society benefited from financial support as well as technical assistance to realise these green buildings. The project has also been recognised by the Ministry of Education for its contributions to protecting the environment as well as the replicability of the measures available to other school buildings.

### E.4.2 Financial, economic, social and institutional needs

For local financial institutions which are evolving in a very competitive environment, their need is to meet the demand, to distinguish their offer compared to their peers, and to improve their image/social recognition; access to new markets related to the green economy and climate change.

However, local financial institutions generally lack expertise to assess and finance climate investments and do not always have a realistic view of the risks associated. Climate-related projects are still viewed as complex, risky, and having high transaction costs. When they are, the requested collaterals are significant and represent a heavy burden for private companies (especially SMEs).

In addition, climate investments need mid-to-long-term loans that LPFs may have difficulties to propose.

For many companies, the needs are to develop new production methods strengthening the resilience of their company to extreme shocks and increasing their competitiveness. More and more companies realize the need to manage environment-related risks in order to avoid being caught on a surprise environmental protection law list, and to reduce their risks of being non-compliant with environmental protection rules thanks to a better anticipation.

However, project investors do not always have sufficient skills to offer attractive and bankable projects. Most of the time, audits are required for the identification of these skills.

To overcome these barriers, the GCF contribution is needed to set up an TA programme for LPFs and companies and to develop concessional funding/financial incentives to trigger their investment. GCF contribution will allow addressing the above-mentioned needs by providing technical assistance to LPFs and companies in order to help them obtain favorable terms on the basis of certain criteria (maturity of green financing market, type of investment, target client base) in order to reduce transaction costs. The GCF contribution is also expected to be an increased access to more affordable lending by commercial banks in the target countries for climate related projects.

The purpose of the Programme is also to answer indirectly to other needs of investors such as (i) the access to low-cost new technologies, (ii) the use of renewable energies, which are often the only way for isolated households to access energy, and (iii) the use of more environmentally friendly and less harmful solutions to adapt to climate change.

## F.3. Environmental, Social Assessment, Including Gender Considerations

An Environmental and Social Management Framework has been developed for the Programme, based on AFD and GCF’s safeguard standards and approach to E&S risk management. The approach to E&S risk management for the projects will be implemented through the use of a project-specific Environmental and Social Management System (ESMS) to ensure E&S risks are properly mitigated on subprojects.

The ES due diligence process will be based on the project life cycle, following the steps below:

- **Identification:** The LFP portfolio and the expected Project activities are screened against AFD’s Exclusion List and GCF’s list of excluded activities. Based on this screening, the LFP is assigned to one of its existing management system and portfolio for E&S and classifies the Project as F1A (High-risk portfolio), F1B (Medium-risk portfolio), or F1C (Low-risk portfolio). This category defines the E&S due diligence to be implemented.

- **Instruction:** In-depth analysis of the LFP ESMS is conducted by an AFD E&S Expert through a review of ES policies, procedures, and practices. The ESMS is evaluated against GCF’s safeguard standards in terms: integration of E&S aspects to be addressed by the feasibility study, and analysis of specific sectoral and/or country context. If necessary, additional ES due diligence activities will be required. An ESD Action Plan (ESAP) is defined for LFPs in need of strengthening their E&S, ESMS operational aspects, including the ESMS audit.

- **Commitment:** E&S due diligence finalized and integrated into the credit facility agreement.

- **Supervision:** where applicable, AFD E&S Expert follows up the technical assistance activities dedicated to the ES. This includes: review of project documents (e.g., feasibility study, environmental impact assessment), and a documentation review. For all Projects, the AFD E&S Expert monitors ESMS operational aspects through at least two visits per year, annual monitoring report and, if necessary, on-site visits of subprojects and review of subproject documentation.

Gender-related aspects are considered through a specific gender analysis of Projects and Subprojects, aiming at ensuring that women’s rights and interests are fully taken into account. This analysis is temporarily conducted on Projects and gender is integrated into E&S due diligence for Projects and Subprojects. The possibility and opportunity of defining gender equality objectives in Projects is considered during project appraisal, in line with AFD’s Gender Policy. An ESAP will be defined if necessary to ensure that gender equality objectives are included in order to cover E&S risk management and gender-related topics and to strengthen LFPs’ capacity on such topics.

# H2. Arrangements for Monitoring, Reporting and Evaluation

Inside the arrangements laid out in an AMLA, please provide project/programme specific institutional setting and implementation arrangements for monitoring and reporting and evaluation. Please indicate how the interim/mid-term and final evaluations will be organized, including the timing. Please provide methodologies for monitoring and reporting on key outcomes of this project/programme.

## Project level

During the disbursement period, AFD will receive reports from LFPs' implementation to track progress and identify potential issues as well as improvement opportunities. In this document, LFPs will report on a semi-annual or annual basis (whichever is more frequent) on the following topics: project implementation status, including key information (climate strategy, training, growth of green portfolio etc.), as defined in the AFD-LCF credit facility agreement.

AFD local agencies will ensure the day-to-day monitoring of the implementation of projects, meeting with LFPs at least once a year. The meetings will be held either by a senior representative of the local agency or a delegate they will review projects' performance and assess potential risks. The local agency staff will be able to check the accuracy and robustness of the project results thanks to their proximity, strong knowledge of the situation on the ground, and strong relationships with the LFPs.

Project Managers (based in AFD's Paris headquarters) or accredited consultants will visit projects and meet LFPs on average once a year. Following this mission, a project implementation report will be shared with all the project stakeholders (central and local governments, companies, NGOs etc.). The report will be reviewed by the team for overseeing project implementation and for approving funding disbursements. On a yearly basis, all projects will be assessed against the AFD's evaluation framework (project management, financial management, environmental performance, disbursement, procurement, outcomes, etc.). Red flag projects that meet any kind of difficulties are subject to a specific monitoring process before returning to a regular implementation plan.

Central and local agencies will continuously oversee the correct implementation of projects. In particular, AFD will ensure that the required conditions have been fulfilled before proceeding with a disbursement. Accounting and financial audits could also be conducted by the Group AFD or an independent third party.

At the end of the disbursement period, AFD will prepare a project completion report summarizing the main events of the implementation phase, the results achieved, and the lessons learned. This report will also provide recommendations for future projects.

During the repayment period, a biannual monitoring of the use of funds will be carried out by the local agencies and at corporate level by the middle office.

In addition, about a year after drafting of the project completion report, the Evaluation department of AFD may consider conducting an evaluation of projects or appoint a local consultant to do so. In most cases, AFD prefers to select a local consulting firm based in the country where the project is located. This allows the Agency to take advantage of direct knowledge and experience of the situation on the ground.

The consultant will be tasked with evaluating the impact of the project (economic, environmental etc.) and writing a report that will be shared with all relevant stakeholders (central and local governments, companies, NGOs etc.) and civil society organizations. The evaluation methodology will follow OECD guidelines.

It is worth noting that the evaluation function within AFD refers to the same evaluation principles and criteria developed by the OECD Development Assistance Committee (DAC) and its evaluation protocols are similar. AFD adheres to the principles of independence, objectivity, transparency, comparability, relevance, and accountability in all evaluations (of our partners) and coordination (with other donors). # ANNEX B: AFD Carbon Footprint

## I.1. The AFD Carbon Footprint Tool's general principles

### A. The AFD Carbon Footprint Tool's guiding principle

The AFD Carbon Footprint Tool is a method for estimating an activity’s operational data to estimate its GHG emissions. A carbon footprint calculation is created by making an inventory of a project's activities, using physical data inputs, e.g., kilowatt-hours (kWh) of electricity and/or tonnes of diesel consumed in the project, litres of fuel used per vehicle, etc. The emissions are calculated for the number of people transported, etc. The quantities are entered into a spreadsheet that directly computes each item's emission in carbon dioxide equivalents (CO2e) via a scientifically determined 'emission factor'. This emission factor is based on the activity the sheet measures. The sheet converts all data values by this emission factor; the spreadsheet instantly converts such physical value into its CO2e using kilograms and/or metric tonnes as a unit of measure.

Some examples of emission factors follow:

- 1 kWh of electricity consumed in China equals 0.74 kilograms (kg) of CO2e emissions.
- Producing one metric tonne of cement emits 862 kg CO2e.
- A short haul 500-kilometre (km) airline flight in business class generates 330 kg CO2e.

When using the Tool calculator it is important to keep in mind the relatively high or low levels of certainty for each emission factor; the calculated result remains an approximation. The AFD Carbon Footprint Tool provides flexibility and ease of use more than accuracy so that information representing orders of magnitude can be used pre-project analysis and support decision-making.

### B. Gases measured

The AFD Carbon Footprint Tool's emissions inventory counts the six main greenhouse gases identified in the Kyoto Protocol:

- **Carbon Dioxide (CO2)**, which results primarily from combusting fossil fuels and from producing aluminum, steel, cement and glass.
- **Methane (CH4)**, which results from burning and/or decomposing biomass (organic material) and from livestock manure, rice cultivation and natural gas leaks.
- **Nitrous Oxide (N2O)**, which results from incinerating solid waste, spreading fertilizers, and/or various transportation means.
- **Hydrofluorocarbons**, which occur as a by-product of industrial processes making insulation, refrigeration and air conditioning.
- **Perfluorocarbons** are also a by-product of aluminum production.
- **Sulphur hexafluoride (SF6)**, which is used for insulation and current interruption in electricity transmission and distribution equipment and electronic systems.


---
**Page 88**
---

- In addition, the Tool counts GHG emissions from air travel, such as water vapor trails in the stratosphere, various forms of condensed water, nitrogen oxides (NOx), and methane, which combined create Ozone (O3): these gases are included in the emission factors.
- **C. CO2 equivalent: a common measurement unit for GHG impacts**
Each type of greenhouse gas has a different potency and atmospheric global warming potential. For instance, one metric tonne of carbon dioxide is equal to 1 metric tonne of CO2. It has a global warming potential 29,900 times greater than carbon dioxide; one-half kilo of SF6, has the same global warming impact as 11 tonnes of carbon dioxide.
- To reconcile the differences between the warming potency of various gases and have a consistent measure for comparing impacts, a specific measurement unit - the **tonne CO2 equivalent (tCO2e)** is used. It expresses the equivalence of one metric tonne of GHG to the number of metric tonnes of CO2 needed to generate the same warming effect over one-hundred years. (For more information, see the 2006 Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Fourth Assessment Report, page 38: http://www.ipcc.ch/pdf/assessment-report/ar4/syr/ar4_syr.pdf)
- **D. Sources of emissions**
AFO's methodology and the ARD Carbon Footprint Tool use the same sources of emissions and non-emissions producing processes for the Carbon Footprint of Organisations. Emissions are separated into two distinct categories according to the project's phase – construction vs. operation – and further sub-categories, as follows:
- **Project "Construction Phase" emissions sources:**
  - Construction materials: production of cement, steel, metals, etc.
  - Construction energy consumption: fuel and electricity used during construction
- **Project "Operating Phase" emissions sources:**
  - Fuel and electricity consumption: use of fossil fuels
  - Electricity/heat consumption
  - On-site operations: includes non-energy producing processes, especially decarbonation from cement clinker production, methane released from mining and dam reservoirs, methanization of organic waste and wastewater, nitrous oxide released by spreading slurry or manure on land.
  - Purchase of goods and services: includes the production of products consumed due to the project's activity, especially metals, plastics, glass, paper and cardboard, and chemical and agricultural inputs
  - Freight: moving commodities, inputs and/or finished products by road, rail, air or ocean transport
  - Waste and waste treatment
  - Land use: changing land used in land, resulting in emissions from biomass and soil utilisation, the use of utilities and infrastructure and/or factories or other buildings. Includes the mix of their use of transportation, electricity, fuels, products, etc., and their waste

The methodology used to measure the GHG emissions of AFD-financed projects accounts for direct and indirect emissions, both up- and downstream from a project, as per Scopes 1,2 and 3.

The scope of a project's indirect emissions can be quite large, and the difficulty of obtaining suitable data can hamper including them in an AFD Carbon Footprint Tool calculation. The Tool helps resolve these issues by:

* All emission sources that can be quantified with a reasonable effort are included in the calculation’s scope.
* Indirect sources that are too difficult to quantify are not included in the calculation, but can be the subject of complementary qualitative analysis.
* The factsheets in the last section of this guide indicate which direct and indirect emissions sources are deemed crucial to include + and which are not + for each primary project type

## Why measure direct and indirect GHG emissions?

Measuring indirect emissions can pose problems: the risk of double counting, our 'ownership' or control over them, and regulatory frameworks that only cover direct emissions, such as the European Union Emission Trading System.

Notwithstanding these issues, AFD's choice to measure carbon footprints using the largest scope is in line with its goal to clarify the link between development and the climate, and follows a widespread trend:

* In the same way that a project’s development impacts are measured via indirect effects, e.g., the number of children in school or extent of road or air traffic, etc., understanding a project's emissions must include its indirect emissions, e.g., vehicle or airplane final energy use, etc.
* A project's vulnerability to higher costs for fuel and/or tonne of CO2 is tied to direct emissions and to the upstream and downstream value chain, e.g., a carbon-intensive supplier and/or changes in demand due to such price increases.

### C. Certainty and accuracy

The AFD Carbon Footprint Tool’s purpose lies in providing an order-of-magnitude accounting for the GHG emissions that a future project will create or abate. An approximate accounting is sufficient, the inherent uncertainties in some emission factors—as indicated in the AFD Carbon Footprint Tool—and the uncertainty of the project's actual emissions are not expected to be significant enough to alter the calculation made ex ante. However, for most projects, usually the main emissions sources are limited in number so any quantitative uncertainty will not affect their relative ranking and should not impede taking a decision. The AFD Carbon Footprint Tool is designed with this in mind. The Tool’s calculation accounts for only the main GHG emissions sources; they are indicated later in this document.

A carbon footprint measurement—even if approximate—helps AFD analyze projects and guides investment and financing decisions; an order-of-magnitude accounting suffices for that.

### D. Conservativeness

AFD, aiming to be “conservative,” uses a “conservative” approach in measuring the carbon footprints of the projects it finances. The assumptions and values used to calculate the carbon footprint must tend towards overestimating emissions and underestimating reductions, especially if data inputs are uncertain.

### E. Project lifetime

The calculation of GHG emissions resulting from a project covers the project's entire lifetime, which the AFD Carbon Footprint Tool user determines. The project lifetime includes both construction and operating phases:

- **Construction phase:** If building the project will generate negligible emissions, its construction phase is not included in the analysis; if the project generates significant provisions, the Tool uses a one-year duration by default.
- **Operating phase:** For all other situations, standardized lifetimes for each type of project are suggested below. The Tool user can change them on a case-by-case basis as needed. The standard lifetimes are set as:
  - 20 years for buildings
  - 30 years for transportation infrastructure
  - 20 years for other projects
