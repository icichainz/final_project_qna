# A.20. Executing Entity information

## A.21. Executive summary (max. 750 words, approximately 1.5 pages)

As like all island developing states, the four Programme countries (Comoros, Madagascar, Mauritius, and Seychelles) are extremely vulnerable to climate change. Their populations, agriculture, lands and infrastructure are highly exposed to the adverse impacts of climate change in coastal areas where sea-level rise and increased frequency and severity of extreme weather is most damaging**1**. At the Programme level, the countries face a range of challenges including: increasing temperatures; higher sea levels; rising ocean acidification. Climate models, even under the most optimistic scenarios, project extreme changes to temperature, precipitation patterns, water availability, public health, infrastructure, agriculture, natural ecosystems and people's livelihoods at risk. Increased air and sea surface temperatures, changes to seasonal rainfall and increased numbers of extreme weather events including both droughts and heavy rains are expected to intensify.

While the combined effects of projected climate change mean that many people are at risk, the populations in the Programme countries are highly dependent on natural resources and therefore ecosystem services. However, the natural ecosystems that provide these services are already under severe threat from human activities such as deforestation, over-exploitation of fisheries and hunting, and conversion to agriculture. These threats have led to a loss of biodiversity importance but with extremely threatened natural ecosystems. As a consequence, the resilience and ability of ecosystems to provide essential ecosystem services necessary for people to adapt to climate change are severely compromised.

The Programme will address these challenges by supporting conservation strategies at all of the Programme countries (see section D). EBA encourages management and restoration of ecosystems to provide the essential services that people need to adapt to climate change. The Programme will also support capacity building in the four Programme countries despite the urgent need and opportunity to scale up EBA action. While there has been a lot of progress on EBA, it is clear that more needs to be done to address the challenges. This includes attention on harnessing the capacity of civil society to address these challenges. Despite their potential to play an important role in addressing EBA, CSAs are typically under-utilized, under-valued and under-financed by development actors.

In this context, the Programme will provide specific funding for EBA through the Critical Ecosystem Partnership Fund (CEPF). CEPF is a global partnership that provides grants to civil society organizations as a mechanism to enable CSOs to support conservation of critical ecosystems within biodiversity hotspots. CEPF is a joint initiative of AFD: Conservation International (an international NGO), the European Union, the Global Environment Facility (GEF) and the World Bank Group (WBG).

## B.1 Project Title (max. 1000 words, approximately 2 pages)

The GCF funding for Programmes and targets the Indian Ocean: Madagascar, Mauritius, and Seychelles, a region where CEPF has current investments. The region, recognized as a biodiversity hotspot, is among the most biologically diverse yet threatened ecoregions in the World[1]. It is also one of the most vulnerable to climate change (see below). The goal of the Programme is to reduce the vulnerability of populations in island states by securing long-term funding for conservation and sustainable development. Three of these countries are Small Island Developing States (SIDS): Madagascar is a Least Developed Country (LDC) that has been identified as one of the most vulnerable to climate change due to its coastal areas and offshore islands share many of the same environmental and socio-economic features as SIDS.

## Climate Change rationale

The specific vulnerability of SIDS and LDCs are recognized by the climate convention (see UNFCCC[2]). Warming temperatures, increased rainfall (and temperatures), sea-level rise and the increased frequency and severity of extreme weather events have, and will continue to have, significant and profound effects on island populations. These include: loss of biodiversity; changes in land use; increased vulnerability of coastal communities; population living on land less than 5m above sea level; the threat of sea-level rise and destruction from storm surges and cyclones; and the potential for saltwater intrusion into freshwater supplies[3]. The combined effects of climate risks, combined with their particular socio-economic situations, make SIDS and LDCs some of the most vulnerable countries in the world to climate change. Large parts of the populations of SIDS and LDCs also rely on natural resources for their livelihood.

All the countries included in the Programme are facing broadly similar challenges related to climate change. The IPCC Fifth Assessment Report (AR5-2014) indicates with high confidence that current and future climate-related impacts will be felt across all sectors, including agriculture, water supply, health, coastal zones, sea level rise, cyclones, increasing air and sea surface temperatures, and changing rainfall patterns[4]. The goal of this programme is to reduce the vulnerability of island populations by securing the critical ecosystem services they need to resist to climate change. This goal will be achieved by harnessing the capabilities of CSOs to accomplish EBA to climate change in the Programme countries and then replicating this experience to other parts of the global grants portfolio. The proposed project builds on the success of the GCF’s first phase, which has been a focus on strengthening government programmes to address climate change impacts, there has been less emphasis on building capacity for CSOs to support EBA actions.

CSOs can play an effective role in addressing ESA. CSOs are typically under-utilized, under-valued and under-financed by development actors.

The project will support EBA-actions that protect, restore or promote the sustainable use of critical ecosystem services to vulnerable people, beginning with demonstration subprojects in four island countries in the tropics. Each GCF will then be able to expand to other parts of its global grants portfolio. As shown in Figure 2, however, there is a clear pathway between EBA and increased human resilience for improving adaptive capacity to increasing resilience to stressors. This is especially the case in SIDS and LDCs.

EBA actions are nature-based solutions that can provide multiple benefits to individuals, communities, businesses and society as a whole benefit and are more resilient when these ecosystem services are protected, restored or promoted. EBA actions help people to survive, adapt and improve conditions in the face of stresses and shocks from climate change and other sources.

ESA is a nature-based solution that is gaining significant importance in the context of both climate change (e.g. adaptation to climate change and implementation of Nationally Determined Contributions under the Paris Agreement) and biodiversity conservation (e.g. integration into the CBD Strategic Plan 2011-2020 and Aichi targets). ESA makes important links between biodiversity conservation and sustainable economic development, which is a key element in addressing poverty and improving human well-being.

EBA is an emerging concept with an increasingly documented scientific basis, implementation of pilot projects globally, as well as the potential for scaling up to national level. The GCF has already supported several EBA actions in countries, where the potential for the approach is great. For example, four of the ten highest global priorities for adaptation of agriculture and biodiversity in the face of climate change are located in island biodiversity hotspots.

### Increased Resilience - better capacity to withstand drought and improve conditions in the face of stress and shocks

To achieve its overall goal of reducing the vulnerability of island populations by securing the critical ecosystem services they need to be resilient to climate change, the Programme will need to overcome several key barriers:

- **Lack of cost-long-term vision and strategies for EBA:** To be effective, EBA needs to focus on the most important ecosystems and ecosystem services for vulnerable people. The priority geographical areas for action are coastal zones, mangroves, coral reefs, and forests because their focus is usually on areas of the highest biodiversity value. The importance of natural ecosystems in providing critical ecosystem services should be reflected more strongly in national development plans and spatial planning tools. Currently the priority themes and areas for EBA-action are not defined for any of the Programme countries (this will be addressed through Discussion Groups at the end of the Programme).

- **Lack of financial resources for CSOs to engage:** Although CSOs have been identified as a necessary component in EBA, they have had strong reservations about covering these costs both within the Programme period but also beyond, to ensure sustainability and continuity of support to CSOs for further EBA activities. In addition to the lack of funding opportunities for addressing environmental issues, national governments remain the recipients of the majority of related funding. Meanwhile, the private sector is able to generate its own resources to engage in environmental protection. Civil society, despite its independent role as a key actor in environmental protection and sustainable development within the sector. In addition to a general lack of funding opportunities, EBA activities often include ecosystem restoration which can be expensive and time-consuming. The costs associated with this are not always covered by existing funding mechanisms.

- **Lack of capacity for CSOs:** There is a tendency to focus on protecting remaining natural ecosystems rather than engaging in the challenges of ecosystem restoration.

The Programme contributes to GCF’s aim of supporting paradigm shifts to move developing countries towards climate-resilient sustainable development. Therefore, procedures for addressing environmental and social concerns are included as part of the ESMF. ## II. Summary of the Secretariat's assessment

### 2.1 Project background and climate rationale

#### 2.1.1 Small island developing States (SIDS) globally are highly vulnerable to climate change impacts on their ecosystems, including terrestrial, freshwater, coastal, and marine and terrestrial ecosystems. Ecosystem-based adaptation (EBA) approaches have proven to be highly successful to cost-effectively reduce the vulnerability of people and ecosystems to climate change by leveraging natural resources for sustainable development (e.g., through ecosystem-based spatial land-use planning and natural protection), as well as providing substantial environmental and social benefits such as the protection of biodiversity, provision of ecosystem services, and enhancement of livelihoods. The planning documents of many SIDS there remain challenges to mainstreaming such approaches: for example, lack of capacity in EBA planning, limited access to technical expertise, limited public awareness, and grant financing, and the technical capacity and knowledge to design effective EBA interventions which are tailored to local conditions.

#### 2.1.2 The proposed programme targets four island countries in the Indian Ocean—Comoros, Madagascar, Mauritius and Seychelles—with a combined population of 29 million people. These countries share similar climatic characteristics, including high rainfall variability, frequent cyclones, and have a track record of international projects in the areas of ecosystem conservation and EBA initiatives. The four countries are also eligible for funding under the Green Climate Fund. The four countries are part of the Madagascar and Indian Ocean biodiversity hotspot, with livelihoods that depend on natural resources, particularly marine resources, whether dominated by agriculture and fishing (as in the case of Comoros and Mauritius) or tourism and other services sectors (Mauritius and Seychelles).

#### 2.1.3 The programme will address key areas of climate vulnerability in each of the countries. For example, reduced rainfall in the rainy season and increased temperatures are projected to affect the availability of water resources for agriculture, livestock farming and ecological flows, such as river flow and wetlands; while the increased intensity of cyclones will impact critical water supply infrastructure, agricultural supply chains and coastal erosion. Coastal erosion is also expected to increase due to rising sea levels and storm surges, with the impacts of rising water temperatures and cyclones, affecting in turn the livelihoods of the populations living on large islands such as Comoros and Mauritius. This is provided in section 3 of the feasibility study.

#### 2.1.4 The programme identifies several categories of EBA intervention that have proven effective in responding against the adverse effects of climate change and increasing the resilience of populations and ecosystems. These include:

(a) Protection and restoration of watershed forests that perform critical flood prevention, soil conservation and water retention functions;

(b) Piloting and replication of climate-resilient agroforestry models, using native plant species for windbreaks, ground cover and nutrient fixing;

(c) Preservation of traditional and indigenous knowledge and natural resource management practices;

(d) Diversification and strengthening of livelihood assets and strategies, including through sustainable fisheries management, nature-based tourism, value chains for natural products, and so on;

# Introduction

## The Proposed Programme

As all island developing states, the five Programme countries (Comoros, Madagascar, Mauritius and Seychelles) are particularly vulnerable to climate change. Their populations, agricultural lands and infrastructure are highly exposed to climate change because they tend to be concentrated in coastal zones where sea-level rise and increased frequency and severity of extreme weather is most damaging. Climate change will increase the intensity and frequency of tropical cyclones, rainfall changes, sea-level rise and increasing ocean acidification. Climate models, even under the most optimistic scenarios, project that existing changes are going to become more pronounced in the future, potentially leading to a doubling or tripling of the current climate change risk.

Increased air and sea surface temperatures, changes to seasonal rainfall and increased numbers of extreme weather events including both droughts and floods are expected. Cyclones are already a significant threat to life and assets and are expected to intensify.

While the combined effects of projected climate change make it clear that many people are at risk, the populations and economies of the Programme countries are highly dependent on natural resources and therefore ecosystem services. However, the natural ecosystems that provide these services are already under severe threat from human activities in all the Programme countries, which are also biodiversity hotspots with high levels of endemism and species richness, and with extremely diverse natural ecosystems.

As a consequence, the resilience and ability of ecosystems to provide essential ecosystem services will be severely compromised by climate change. This will increase existing climate change vulnerability. The most significant impacts are expected to include: declining provision of freshwater due to degraded watersheds; increased flooding due to conversion of ecosystems that provide flood protection; increased coastal erosion leading to damage to coastal ecosystems such as mangroves and coral reefs; declines in the productivity of fisheries due to loss of nursery habitat, and decreased agricultural production due to heat and drought.

Ecosystem-based Adaptation (EbA) measures have been identified as high priorities in the climate change strategies of all the Programme countries. EDA encourages conservation or improved management of natural ecosystems that provide essential ecosystem services for people who need to adapt to climate variability. However, beyond a few pilot projects, financing for EbA is currently insufficient in the Programme countries despite the urgent need and opportunity to scale up EBA action. While there has been increasing recognition by donors of the importance of addressing climate change impacts, there has been less attention on harnessing the capacity of civil society to address these challenges. Despite their potential to play an effective role in addressing EbA, CSOs are typically under-funded or not sufficiently involved.

In this context, the Programme will provide specific funding for EBA through the Critical Ecosystem Partnership Fund (CEPF) to mobilize Civil Society Organizations (CSOs). CEPF was established in 2000 as a mechanism to enable CSOs to support conservation of critical ecosystems within biodiversity hotspots. CEPF is currently funded by the Global Environment Facility, the United Nations Development Programme, the Global Environment Facility, the Government of Japan and the World Bank. 2. Gender Assessment

With its focus on Small Island Developing States and Least Developed Countries, the Programme includes some of the most vulnerable countries in the world to climate change as well as some of the poorest. All the countries in the biodiversity hotspot targeted by the Programme (Comoros, Madagascar, Mauritius and Seychelles) have already reported significant impacts of climate change with increasing frequency and intensity of extreme weather events such as cyclones, floods, heatwaves and droughts all being attributed to climate change. Climate change projections for the Programme countries indicate that climate change impacts will continue to get worse over the next one century even under the most optimistic scenario (RCP 2.6). In addition to these natural hazards, other anthropogenic pressures on ecosystems are reducing the availability and quality of important ecosystem services, further reducing the resilience of people, often the poorest and most vulnerable in society.

Gender inequality remains a significant problem in the Programme countries, increasing the risks and burdens of climate change for women. In Mauritius and Seychelles, per capita national incomes are higher than in Comoros or Madagascar but gender disparities remain. In Comoros and Madagascar, women are often unequal participants in decision-making, labor markets, legal processes and they face gender stereotypes due to socio-cultural norms. Women also have limited access to land, credit and other resources as well as access to adaptation options when faced with risk events (e.g., poor crop yields, floods, droughts etc.).

Not only are women often disadvantaged in terms of human development indicators, many are also highly dependent on natural resources for their livelihoods. Women are often responsible within their households for water collection and food production, activities that are often first and most affected by climate change. In rural areas of all the Programme countries, the different roles and responsibilities of men and women in society, and different gender norms, mean that they have different access to resources and opportunities. In a context where there is limited land ownership, women tend to care for household gardens and subsistence agriculture, while men are more likely to engage in cash-generating activities. Women often therefore play an important role in sustainable livelihoods strategies but their contributions are often not recognized or rewarded.

Extreme poverty and food insecurity are extreme in many parts of rural Madagascar. Household structure plays an important role in the poverty in Madagascar. Extreme poverty incidence, though not absolute poverty, is higher among female-headed households (Sulla, 2014). Although there are no major differences between men’s and women’s earnings, women earn less than men on average. These facts do not imply that there are no gender-related disparities. Analysis of the labor market suggests that women's earnings are not as high as those of men when controlling for various other factors (Sulla, 2014).

In Madagascar, both government and civil society are confronting women’s rights issues more than ever before, but there is still a long way to go in terms of gender equality. There are many structural elements that hinder women's empowerment. Women's rights are enshrined in the Constitution of Madagascar and the government has a National Plan for Gender Promotion. However, gender disparities persist and were well characterized by the Africa Development Bank (ADB, 2015).

- **Social norms and values centered around men:** These are reflected in households, where traditional disposal rights have been given to men as decision-making roles to men. This division tends to also be reflected in workplaces.
  
- **Important gaps in gender-positive legislation and their application:** Throughout much of rural Madagascar, women have less access with traditional bylaws and customs. The legal system is hard for people, including women, to access in rural areas, which means that the many gender positive legal texts are effectively not applied.

- **High prevalence of underage marriages and adolescent children:** Related to the two points above, 15% of girls aged between 10–14 years old have already been married. In addition, 60% of girls already have children by the age of 18.

- **Violence against children and women:** Despite multiple initiatives over successive governments and by donors, violence against women and sexual exploitation of minors remains a problem that is amplified by poverty in some regions of the country.

## 2.3 Mauritius

As noted earlier, Mauritius is ranked relatively highly (80th of 189 countries assessed) for gender equality according to its GGI score (UNDP, 2018). The Constitution of Mauritius, approved in 1968, granted women the right to vote and equal access to, and full participation in, power structures and decision-making forums. After years of lobbying by women's organizations, two important developments occurred in the 1990s: a new law was passed on domestic violence and sexual harassment; and a new law on property rights, income and business ownership. Second, a new ministry was established with responsibility for women's rights, children and family issues. The current Ministry of Gender Equality, Child Development and Family Welfare has as its mission to design and implement policies and programmes that promote the empowerment of women in all spheres of life; to ensure their protection and enhancing their overall development and promoting the welfare of families. In 2008, the National Gender Policy Framework was established that calls upon all Ministries to include gender dimensions in all their respective policies.

In Mauritius, gender equality is observed in many spheres of society (UN JICA, 2016; UNDP, 2018). In education, the proportion of women is higher than for men in terms of tertiary and secondary school enrolment and literacy rates for those aged 15 to 24. Regarding health, child health (the under-5 survival ratio) and maternal mortality posed by HIV is similar for males and females. The life expectancy of women also exceeds that of men.

Women lag men in terms of employment. Women have lower labor force participation, a smaller share of jobs than men and lower rates of employment [see table 2]. Women have low ownership of businesses and fewer of them have access to financial credit. However, the proportion of women in senior management positions is high. A large number of women hold bank accounts (UNDP, 2018). One of the remaining areas of greatest inequality is in politics. At only 31.6%, the representation of women in parliament is significantly lower than that of men [UNJODP, 2018].

# Appendix 1. CEPP’s Gender Policy

CEPF’s mission is to engage civil society to protect biodiversity hotspots, the world’s most biologically diverse yet threatened ecosystems. ## Definitions

### Gender

Gender refers to the economic, social, political, and cultural attributes and opportunities associated with being women and men. The social definitions of what it means to be a woman or a man vary among cultures and over time. Gender is a sociocultural expression of particular characteristics and roles that are associated with certain groups of people based on reference group and sex and sexuality.

### Gender Analysis

Gender analysis is the process of examining and interpreting information on the respective roles and responsibilities among men and women in six domains of activity, including: practices and participation; access to resources; knowledge and beliefs; laws, policies and regulatory institutions.

### Gender Awareness

Gender awareness refers to explicit recognition of local gender differences, norms, and relations and their importance to outcomes in program/policy design, implementation and evaluation. This recognition drives the need for assessment of gender differences, norms, and relations in order to address gender equity in outcomes.

### Gender Equity

Gender equity is the process of being fair to women and men. To ensure fairness, measures must be taken to compensate for historical and social disadvantages that prevent women and men from operating on a level playing field.

### Gender Equality

Gender equality is the state or condition that affords women and men equal enjoyment of human rights, socially valued goods, opportunities, and resources.

### Gender Integration

Gender integration refers to strategies applied in program assessment, design, implementation, and evaluation to take gender norms into account and to compensate for gender-based inequalities.

### Gender Mainstreaming

Gender mainstreaming is the process of incorporating a gender perspective into policies, strategies, programs, project activities, and administrative functions, as well as into the institutional culture of an organization.

### Sex

Sex refers to the biological differences between males and females. Sex differences are concerned with males' and females' physiology.

## INTRODUCTION

Gender is a social construct that refers to relations between and among the sexes, based on their relative roles in society. These roles are influenced by cultural norms, values, expectations, and opportunities associated with being male or female. Gender varies across cultures, is dynamic and open to change over time, and because of this, gender roles should not be assumed, but analyzed.

Gender, however, is a significant characteristic that influences a person's interactions with the environment and ability to participate in and benefit from conservation initiatives.

As such, gender can influence who uses, has access to and owns natural resources. Therefore, considering gender can help determine who participates in and benefits from a conservation project. These considerations can ultimately affect a project’s long-term success.

For these reasons, CEFP developed this gender toolkit to ensure that CEFP's investments are implemented with gender-awareness in mind. This means that CEFP's Secretariat staff, regional implementation teams, and grantees will be encouraged to consider the different needs of women and men in CEFP-related activities at all scales, and that gender issues and considerations are actively incorporated throughout the grant-making process.

Commonly used in reference to human resources and equal participation of women and men in all areas of work, projects or programs.

### Gender Blind
Does not take gender into account. Based on the often-incorrect assumption that women and men are the same in all aspects, including their needs and abilities.

### Gender Division of Labor
Allocation of different jobs or types of work to women and men. For example, among human farmers, males and females target different types of foods and crops for production, marketing, and consumption.

The knowledge that differences between women and men are socially constructed and impact our behaviors, attitudes, beliefs and values, which affects the distribution of rights and access to goods, opportunities and resources.

The process of being fair to women and men. To ensure fairness, it addresses structural barriers and social disadvantages that prevent women and men from operating on a level playing field. An example of gender equity: providing child care at a day-care center, mentoring, therapy, allowing women to participate in decision-making regarding the taking care of their children.

### Gender Gap
The differences between women and men, especially as reflected in social roles, political, intellectual, cultural, or economic attainments or attitudes.

### Gender Integration
Refers to strategies applied in program assessment, design, implementation and evaluation to take gender norms into account and to compensate for gender inequalities.

### Gender Mainstreaming
The process of incorporating a gender perspective into policies, strategies, programs, project activities, monitoring and evaluation, budgeting, and organizational structures. It is the continuous integration of a gender perspective in all areas of an organization to promote equality between women and men, and to combat discrimination.

# ANNEX B: IDEAS TO CIRCUMVENT GENDER BARRIERS

| Barrier/Challenge/Risk | Possible Solution |
|---|---|
| Lack of time/competing priorities | * Find out what time of day/session women and men tend to be available and schedule activities then. Provide childcare during activities. |
| Inaccessibility/travel to activities is onerous | * Move activities closer to communities where you want to engage people. Provide transportation to activities. Undergo a door-to-door approach for overnight activities and work with households to identify appropriate ways to allow for their participation. |
| Feeling uncomfortable about participating publicly in activities | * Conduct separate activities with women and men (with same facilitators) so that participants feel comfortable. Provide opportunities for women who may have less background/experience on the subject so that everyone can be prepared and knowledgeable. Recognize that women bring different opinions and contributions from quieter groups/individuals. Ensure that every effort is made to value the contributions of all voices and hope to see a diversity of community members. Ensure that the discussion is conducted in the local language. Work with local leaders and male project participants to explain the importance of women's participation and identify culturally appropriate ways to allow for their participation. |
| Limited access to information | * Provide materials in multiple languages (or with images) that everyone can understand. Tailor messages for different groups through the most appropriate channels (e.g., radio, TV, SMS, informal community meeting, etc.) depending on how each group prefers to receive information. |
| Project design does not address gender/social equity | * Provide basic gender training and follow-up trainings, particularly for staff members who interact with the target population. Allocate an amount in the project budget for gender activities and/or training. Group women's organizations, indigenous and youth groups, governmental entities, or conservation/development NGOs. |
| Project may increase work load (paid or unpaid) of a certain group | * Ensure that those with the increased workload are benefitting from the project.
