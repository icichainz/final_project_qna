### A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

1. Given its geographic location and mountainous terrain, Bhutan is particularly vulnerable to changes in climate which will have a significant impact on the country’s agricultural sector. The most immediate impacts are expected as changes in temperatures which affect the altitude at which crops can grow and evaporation from soils. Water availability for irrigation will also be affected by increased frequency of droughts and floods. In addition, Bhutan is experiencing high rainfall, flash floods and landslides have damaged existing irrigation schemes and disrupted market access for smallholder farmers. The Royal Government of Bhutan (RGoB) has a long-term vision to achieve food security as the Royal Government of Bhutan’s (RGoB)s goal of food self-sufficiency and inclusive green socio-economic development.

2. These climate-wide impacts on agricultural infrastructure, production and supply side pose risk of 58% of the country's households, predominantly older farmers whose livelihood options are compromised. The increasing costs of recovery limit public resources, jeopardizing hard-won development gains within this least developed country. With only 71% of the population having access to electricity, the RGoB is committed to meeting the SDG targets.

3. Consequently, the RGoB seeks support to enhance the resilience of smallholder farms to rainfall variability, as well as increases in temperature and projected increases in monsoonal rainfall. Enhancing the resilience of the population engaged in agriculture will be critical for ensuring that the country’s development pathway is functional to meeting the RGoB's aspirations on climate resilient, low carbon development.

4. Given the specific climate change challenges confronting smallholder farmers in Bhutan it a priority for the RGoB to support the adaptation and mitigation of climate change impacts on smallholder farms due to climate change, especially variation in rainfall and frequent occurrence of extreme events, through three complementary Outputs:

- Promote resilient agricultural practices in the face of changing climate patterns
- Integrate climate change risks into water and land management practices that affect smallholders
- Reduce vulnerability and impact of climate change induced landslides during extreme events that disrupt market access

# C.1 Strategic Context

## Geographical context and climate change related vulnerabilities

### The geographical context of Bhutan is particularly vulnerable to climate change.

The UNFCCC identifies countries with mountainous ecosystems as among the most vulnerable in Article 4.8, as well as their 2nd, 3rd, 4th and 5th Assessment Reports, which point to a number of vulnerabilities that mountainous countries face, including their size and limited resources for adaptation.

### Bhutan is predominantly made up of fragile mountain ecosystems with large areas of ice and snow.

Only 7.9% of total land area in Bhutan is arable or semi-arable; agricultural land is generally located along steep geomorphic terrain. More than 31% of agricultural land is on slopes greater than 50% resulting in soil losses of up to 2,000 tonnes per hectare annually. The majority of the population lives in mountainous areas and are vulnerable to climate-related processes such as pronounced runoff during the monsoon season, particularly after long dry periods.

### Data from weather stations in Bhutan show clear increases (positive trends) in mean and maximum temperatures, as well documented by the Global Historical Climatology Network (GHCN) and other global datasets. These positive trends which are indicative to have started earlier (since 1970) according to global data.

Whilst average annual rainfall has seen a slight increase of around 2% over the last century, there is a clear decrease in the length of the dry season and several indices measuring extremes of daily rainfall (see Annex II: Feasibility Study).

### Climate risk-related challenges are expected to continue and worsen with climate change.

Bhutan's Second National Communication (SNC) states that mean annual temperature across Bhutan is projected to increase by 0.8-1.2°C before 2039 and by 2.0-2.4°C before 2069. Rainfall is expected to increase slightly overall (+0% for the 2030-2059 period) but with a large degree of interannual variability, with an average annual increase of 4.8% during the monsoon period (opposite to currently observed changes). For the 2060-2089 period, an overall increase of precipitation (+21%) is expected with generally more intense monsoon seasons. Downscaled projections from local climate models indicate that extreme rainfall events will become more frequent and lead to critical thresholds more frequently in the future (see Annex II.A Report on the Analysis of Historical Climate Change and Climate Risk).

### These observations and projections highlight the increased risk of occurrence and magnitude of flash floods and landslides, which on steep slopes cause damages and limit the functioning of open irrigation channels and roads. In the near term, climate change is expected to increase the frequency of droughts in Bhutan.

The projected changes in temperature and precipitation will have a significant impact on agriculture, farmers by limiting water available for planting and land preparation. Decreases in observed rainfall, increasing temperatures and increased evaporation rates are likely to reduce crop yields and decrease the amount of water available on crop yield and the amount of land used to produce crops. These climate impacts reduce food security, where under current conditions 30% of households experience food insecurity at least once a year.

The nutritional status for Bhutan measured in terms of stunting prevalence, while reduced from 56% in 1988, remains high at 33.7% (2014) and is the highest among South Asian countries.

### Livelihood difficulties in agriculture caused by climate changes are already resulting in growing trends of rural-urban migration of farmers, particularly man in search of off-farm employment opportunities – a trend which further exacerbates the phenomenon of food insecurity.

The poverty rate has been decreasing over time. However, while the overall poverty rate in Bhutan has improved, poverty continues to be a rural phenomenon and is concentrated among smallholder households. The 2014 National Household Living Standards Survey (NHLS) shows that 35% of rural households are poor compared with only 9% urban households. While the poverty rate has decreased from 67% in 1987 to 14% over the last 17 years*, while the overall poverty rate in Bhutan has improved, poverty continues to be a rural phenomenon and is concentrated among smallholder households. The 2014 National Household Living Standards Survey (NHLS) shows that 35% of rural households are poor compared with only 9% urban households.

### The risks of projected climate change on the sector and on the country's development goals are high.

Given that the average size of each smallholder farm is less than 2.5ha, household investment in land development is low. Faced with the challenges of climate change, there will be a need to increase investments in land development and diversify opportunities in urban areas. Of the total cultivable land of 94,903ha, 26% of the agricultural land is left fallow (24,975ha)*.

# Detailed Project / Programme Description

## Climate Vulnerability Baseline

### Temperature Changes and Hazards

#### 1. Temperature changes in Bhutan are rising and expected to continue.

Observations show that annual average temperatures have been increasing between 1998 and 2016. This is reflected in increases in mean and maximum temperature, as well as the number of days with extreme high temperatures (see Annex II: Feasibility Study). Downscaled future projections from a validated set of 6 NASA NEX-GDDP models, consistent with projections in the IPCC AR5, indicate that Bhutan will experience an increase in annual average temperature by more than 2°C by mid-21st century and exceed 3°C by the late 21st century. This is compared to the 20th century, with the highest daily maximum temperatures potentially increasing by 4–6°C. IPCC's 5th Assessment Report states that Bhutan will experience a significant increase in mean annual temperature of up to 7°C and an increase in extreme temperature concentrations in amongst others Bhutan. Mean annual temperature in Bhutan is expected to increase by 3.8-5.7°C by the end of this century.

#### 2. Rainfall changes: Annual rainfall over Bhutan is highly variable, with nearly all 17 class stations demonstrating an average downward trend in both winter and monsoon rainfall between 1998 and 2016 (see Annex II: Feasibility Study). Total rainwater availability has decreased by about 5% since the mid-1990s (see Annex III: Feasibility Study, 1970–2014). This reduction in rainfall is further reflected in negative trends (reductions) in snow areas between 2003 and 2016 (Appendix A: Snow Cover Trends). These trends are critical in terms of water resources.

### Slope Stabilization through Scaling Up of Sustainable Land Management Technologies

202. The steep slopes of Bhutan are an important characteristic of the land that contributes to its vulnerability to climate change and degradation. These approaches have been supported by a participatory monitoring and evaluation (M&E) system that facilitates continuous engagement and participation of the communities and other stakeholders and promotes ownership and engagement of the rural farming communities.

203. The project developed a participatory SLMP action planning at the village and Gewog level in 3 pilot Dzongkhags (Chhume, Wangdue Phodrang and Trongsa). The preparatory actions contain a wide range of SLMP interventions aimed at reducing land degradation, improving soil fertility and agricultural production and enhancing vegetation cover and alternative livelihoods. These include: establishment of community-based conservation groups; demonstration plots in the pilot phase were rolled out in total 133 villages in the pilot Dzongkhags. SLMP pilots under SLMP showed clear impact for example, in Chhume Gewog, where the project has been successful in reducing soil erosion and land degradation, enhancing soil fertility and workability of the land and improving crop yield. A longer-term engagement with communities is required to ensure that these gains are sustained over time. The project also included awareness raising and implementation support and participatory monitoring, proved successful in raising awareness of rural farmers on sustainable land management practices and their benefits. These interventions have had direct impacts to be felt by households in the medium- to long-term with more direct short-term impacts through support for rapid adoption of new technologies and practices. The project has also produced a number of publications, including the Participatory Action Planning Manual and Tool Kit (NESC, 2003), Working the Land (NESC, 2011), Bhutan’s Soil and Water Conservation Catalogue (NESC, 2011), and the Bhutan Catalogue of Soil and Water Conservation Approaches and Technologies (BHUCAT) (NESC, 2011).

# Gender policy

The project is expected to benefit 60,652 women which is 51% of the total beneficiaries. The proposal contains a comprehensive assessment and analysis of gender issues in Bhutan, including the impact of climate change on women's livelihoods and the vulnerability of climate change on women and men. The analysis provides insights into additional burdens inflicted on women by climate change. It also proposes a gender action plan and further complements the existing guidelines of the GCF Gender Policy Framework.

The development of the funding proposal included a specific study on gender issues that has allowed the AE to identify specific concerns of women, thereby integrating actions to be addressed in the project. The assessment reveals that the level of involvement of women in agriculture varies across crops and the nature of activities between women and men, with women being more involved in post-harvest activities such as processing and marketing of cardamom. Generally, women's role is limited to agricultural activities near the household in Bhutan. Women are also responsible for domestic chores like cooking, cleaning, and fetching water. These responsibilities are significant and add to the women's work burden and time constraints, limiting them from engaging in other productive activities. Climate change has affected agricultural productivity and production patterns, leading to a decline in crop yields and income generation. This has led to reman on the farms, leading to the increased trend of feminization of the agriculture sector in Bhutan. Women are also responsible for managing water resources at household level, which is the responsibility of which lies with both women and men. ##### 1.1 Impact potential

1. Bhutan is a small country with mountainous terrain, located in South Asia. Due to its geographic positioning in the Eastern Himalayan region, it is subject to high rainfall variability under monsoon influence and experiences a high degree of elevation change. The rugged mountainous range exhibits much colder average temperatures zones, which restrict agricultural production.
2. Bhutan has a population of about 750,000 people (as of 2019), living in the hills and valleys. The agriculture sector is important for the rural population; about 70 per cent of the population depends on it as their primary livelihood (including livestock, horticulture, and forestry subsectors) for its livelihood.
3. The crop subsector is dominated by subsistence-level smallholder producers.

4. The funding proposal package presents observations based meteorological datasets for less than a decade (1986 to 1996). Despite having such limited observational data, a thorough analytical review of the past climatology of Bhutan has been presented.
5. In doing so, the proposal package provides an overview of the climate change projections and generally acceptable analytical tools and methods. It is found that the average surface temperature has been elevated by 0.2°C over the last three decades (1986 to 2016). This warming trend is more pronounced in the eastern region and a more prominent change in the western mid-mountain and low-altitude regions.
6. The proposal package also provides evidence of increasing frequency and intensity of the occurrence of high rainfall events. The pre-monsoon dry season has become exceedingly drier due to diminishing seasonal rainfall availability over time. These phenomena have been linked with climate change, which is expected to continue in the future.
7. Agricultural droughts, with increasing potential evaporative transpiration (ET) during low-rainfall seasons and high temperatures, are also expected to increase. This insight shows that the Bhutanese agriculture sector is indeed highly vulnerable to climate change.

## Gender Division of Labor in Agriculture

- Taking into account the different roles that women play at all levels of planning and decision-making from local governance to national level (e.g., entrepreneurial/business skills as expressed by women).
- Ensure project supports are directed to women and men's practical and strategic needs and priorities that would bring transformational change in the gender relations and shift more power to women.
- Ensure access to information on markets, pricing policy and climate through innovative information dissemination channels.
- To reduce negative impacts on women (e.g., workload, health), through project intervention on promoting women's empowerment.
- Conduct qualitative assessments on the gender-specific benefits from the project interventions and incorporate in the mid-term report and terminal evaluation.
- Ensure that all project outputs are designed to effectively reflect gender equality in climate change adaptation evidenced through the project are shared effectively amongst stakeholders and national authorities.
- Identification of gaps in gender equality through the use of sex-disaggregated surveys enabling monitoring and analysis of project benefits to women.
- Addressing gender inequality by ensuring that outputs effectively reflect gender-specific differences. Strategies used in the project are then tailored, taking into account such differences; including having a specialist position / position of advice within the project to implement gender related activities.

In addition, the monitoring and evaluation framework for assessing the achievement of project objectives in relation to gender equality will include men and women who had access to the project outputs.
