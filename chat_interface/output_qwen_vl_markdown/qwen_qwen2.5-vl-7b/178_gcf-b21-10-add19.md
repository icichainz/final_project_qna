### A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

Agriculture, livestock and forestry activity are the foundations of the economy in Central American countries. This sector is especially vulnerable to climate change because it is highly dependent on climatic conditions, the geographical location and socioeconomic and technological characteristics. Climate change scenarios suggest that production could drop significantly by 2050 (10% for maize, 30% for beans, and 40% for coffee). Small agricultural producers would be affected as family businesses at subsistence levels, but they would also impact food security, poverty and the degree of dependence on imports.

Moreover, environmental degradation and the destruction of biodiversity and pests spreading are processes that are already in full way and will very probably become more marked as climate changes progress.

One of the primary constraints that limit the growth of any of these small agricultural production units or rural MSMEs is the limited access to credit. This is a major constraint for most of them, especially those located in remote areas which has left the agricultural sector with limited financial options for business development. In most of Central American countries, except for Costa Rica and Panama, the percentage of microfinance institutions (MFIs) that provide services to MSMEs is low: El Salvador (10%), Nicaragua (5%), Honduras (2%), Guatemala (3%) and Dominican Republic (4%). The percentage in much lower in the case of MSMEs, and even lower for adaptation purposes.

This Project aims at increasing access to credit for Microfinance Institutions (MFIs) and Medium-sized Enterprises (MSMEs) in Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, Panama and the Dominican Republic by removing barriers to access financial and non-financial services for adopting and implementing climate change adaptation measures. The initiative will promote innovation and provide solutions of adaptation to climate change through the following specific objectives:

1. To reduce the obstacles to credit access for MSMEs. Access to lending will be promoted through financial and non-financial services, including microfinance institutions (MFIs) and Medium-sized Enterprises (MSMEs), which are connected through the networks of Intermediary Financial Institutions (IFIs) accredited by CABEI across the region. Through this financing mechanism, MSMEs will have access to credit for climate change adaptation measures as well as the consolidation of production systems adapted to climate change.

2. To strengthen the beneficiaries' capacities through training and technical assistance services to develop best adaptation practices in order to increase their organizational capabilities and introduction of new technologies for adaptation to climate change (Component 2).

3. To promote adaptation measures through an incentive scheme that increases MSMEs resilience. This incentive will award MSMEs with a credit line, which can be used as collateral when applying for loans from MFIs, while providing technical assistance in the form of training and capacity building, as well as access to financing for adaptation measures, as well as their successful implementation (Component 3).

This initiative aims to reach more than 17,000 direct beneficiaries which correspond to more than 50,000 indirect beneficiaries. ## B.1. Description of Financial Elements of the Project/Programme

The financial instruments proposed in the Programme will help reduce the obstacles to investment in adaptation actions in sectors where there is insufficient access to credit and a significant lack of knowledge on the impacts on climate change as well as the potential solutions for increasing resilience of the productive systems. Barriers to banking and business services, which are essential for creating an enabling environment that catalyses resilient investments in micro-small and medium-sized enterprises needs to be created.

The programme aims to promote access to lending through financial and non-financial services to install and spread the adaptation technologies, and in parallel to establish incentives under the form of awards to promote best adaptation practices and good credit behaviour among beneficiaries of programmes. The Programme will help MSMEs to adopt good quality adaptation projects as well as credit behaviour.

This initiative will reduce barriers to credit for 5,000 MSMEs to implement adaptation measures and will increase awareness on climate change and the need to adapt in order to secure access to finance and other forms of investment. Both MSMEs and CBFIs (Cooperative, Micro-Finance and Non-Bank institutions) will receive incentives to promote best practices in terms of credit management and financial inclusion. The Programme will help MSMEs to manage credits and IFIs to implement a financial product that responds adequately to MSMEs' needs to reduce their perception of MSMEs as a high risk across the region.

## 1 Risk sharing facility (Component 2)

For this component, GCF has allocated US$ 12.5 million to the GCF for the establishment of a Risk Sharing Facility in the form of a trust agreement, in which CABI will match GCF's loan amount allocated to this Facility. This total of US$ 25 million is to be used by CABI to provide credit lines to MSMEs and IFIs. The risk sharing facility that each institution becomes responsible for their portion of loans to IFIs (50%). However, CABI will still be bound by AMA obligations and thus shall protect GCF's interests amount.

CABEI will be the lender of record and manage all the outflows and inflows of loans to IFIs. CABEI will refer principal and interest payments from IFIs to GCF. The revolving nature of the credit lines to the IFIs will only apply to the principal amounts repaid by the IFIs. CABEI will also perform all AE duties.

In this scheme, CABEI and GCF take the credit risk of the IFIs. The IFIs will bear a full recourse liability to CABEI/GCF even if MSMEs default on their loans from the IFIs.

The merits of this structure compared to the situation of CABEI bearing the full credit risk of the IFIs:
- Lower cost for CABEI
- Minimum concessionality: GCF can minimize GCF's concessionality by aligning financial terms to underlying loans.
With regards to concessionality of the credits to be granted to the MSMEs, CABEI guarantees that GCF's concessionality will be passed on to final beneficiaries through the IFIs.

Concerning private sector leverage, CABEI will seek to leverage funds from IFIs and from MSMEs.

#### Climate change impacts
Central America is one of the areas in the world that is most exposed to the consequences of climate change, which is negatively affecting its biodiversity and viability, and will have an increasingly strong influence on its economic growth since weather-related factors have a decisive impact on many of its productive activities. According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Fifth Assessment Report, climate projections suggest increases in temperature and variations in precipitation for Central America by 2010.

## Climate change and the agricultural sector in Central America

### Component 3: Incentive scheme to promote ecosystem-based adaptation measures implemented by MSMEs (Adapt-Award)

The measure proposed by this component consists in the refund of 20% of the loan principal granted by the IFIs in the case of women-led MSMEs. This means that in the case that loans are more than USD $5,000, the MSME will be granted a maximum of USD $10,000 (this measure is only applicable to loans with a term of 36 months or less). The criteria for this incentive scheme are listed below. This incentive is to be awarded only after the adaptation measures have been successfully implemented and monitored. Adapt-Awards constitute grants for their recipients.

The proposed ratios are based on CABEI's experience with previous programmes, with the incorporation of a distinction between women-led and men-led MSMEs in order to provide incentives for increasing the access to credit and further increasing the quality of the implementation of adaptation measures:

- The refund of 15.0%–20.0% of the total loan will allow the MSME to further extend the reach of its investment. Examples include: (a) exchange with other MSMEs in adaptation measures, assistance to courses or conferences on adaptation; (b) exchange with other MSMEs in adaptation measures, assistance to courses or conferences on increasing resilience, to mitigation activities (e.g., renewable energy) and similar activities that contribute to increasing resilience or mitigating climate change.

- The incentive of 5.0%–10.0% of the total loan will allow the IFI to promote changes within IFIs structure and operations in order to make the financing more attractive for financial institutions these incentives are expected to increase the level of adoption of the Programme based on CABEI's knowledge of the MSMEs' needs, as well as their interest in adopting sustainable practices that further extend the reach of the Programme (e.g. strengthening of environmental and social management systems).

On the one hand, this incentive would motivate the IFI to reduce the level of risk of MSMEs and promote credit mechanisms focused on adaptation to climate change. On the other hand, this incentive acts as a boost towards increased spread for the IFI.

Agriculture in Central America is extremely sensitive to weather and climate-related factors because of these countries' geographical location and their socioeconomic and technological characteristics. It is the production sector that has sustained the greatest losses because of extreme weather events in recent decades.

Initial estimates based on climate change scenarios suggest that grain yields could drop significantly during this century. On average, maize yields are projected to decline 30%, 45% and for maize, beans and rice, respectively, by the end of the century. These potential losses would have a direct impact on producers, most of whom are small farmers who depend heavily on agriculture as their main source of income and food security.

The available evidence for Central America indicates that environmental degradation and the destruction of biodiversity are processes that are already in full way and that will very probably become more marked as climate change progresses. The area under forest cover has decreased by 13% since 2005 (FAO, 2012). Forests have been reduced by 13% during this century because of changes in land use (without climate change). Climate change is projected to boost this loss to around 40% by the end of the century.

Other processes that generate large impacts on the agricultural sector are those related to pests, plant diseases provided by the pine beetle ("Gorgoni") or the coffee rust ("Yuya"), which have already caused irreparable damage to woods and coffee crops. The effects of climate change will be more severe in the future because they will lead to longer drought periods (in the case of pine beetles) and with excessive humidity due to intense rainfall (coffee rust).

Agriculture, livestock and forestry activity still are the foundations of the economy in Central American countries. However, their use is incorrect and deficient: 51.2% of the surface area of Central America is over-used, and 21.1% is under-used. As a result, only 27% of the surface area is correctly used.

The agricultural sector contribution to the GDP of countries within the region, measured by the national accounts, represents between 50% and 60%. It varies from less than 10% in Honduras and El Salvador and less than 10% of GDP in Costa Rica and Panama while in the Dominican Republic it represents 8.9% of total GDP. Family Agriculture produces more than half of all food consumed by Central American populations, but its contribution to the GDP is much more significant, as an example, in countries such as Guatemala, Honduras and Nicaragua whose sector contributes 30% of the GDP.

The Central American countries have approximately 2.1 million small family agricultural production units or Micro, Small and Medium-sized Enterprises (MSMEs) that are extremely vulnerable to climate change impacts because they are highly vulnerable in the face of climate change and the production area is characterized by low productivity and little income generation capacity. ### D.1 Value Added for GCF Involvement

GCF involvement is critical to assure the IFIs have the necessary credit enhancement tools to undertake risks associated with capital and credit restrictions, which would otherwise not be assumed under normal credit operations. This funding will address these issues by providing a risk mitigation mechanism.

CASEI will ensure that the GCF concessionality is passed on to IFIs and beneficiaries. Thus, access to credits will be enhanced by offering better conditions to final beneficiaries.

Additionally, funding will allow the Programme to be strengthened by a Technical Assistance component to provide capacity building required of project proponents as well as to ensure quality studies are conducted along adequate project preparation phases, including the identification of potential risks and opportunities for the project.

Furthermore, GCF funding will support an incentive scheme to promote adaptation measures among MSMEs and catalyzing the process of breaking barriers to adaptation finance within the IFIs.

The amount required to undertake the leverage components, Financial Mechanism, Technical Assistance and Adapt Award, are essential to mobilize private investment in three key results areas of the GCF: most vulnerable people and communities; climate change adaptation; and sustainable development and resilience.

Finally, the Programme takes on a definitive role in enhancing Direct Access given the nature that private actors from Central America and the Dominican Republic will be directly responsible for proposing projects to which they require funding through the CASEI mechanism. ### E.4.1. Vulnerability of country and beneficiary groups (Adaptation only)

The Feasibility Study presents a detailed description of the scale and intensity of vulnerability of the countries and of the beneficiary groups where the Programme will be implemented.

A brief description of the main drivers of climate change, projections, impacts and vulnerabilities at the regional level is presented. Climate change is heightening Central America's social and economic vulnerability and it will have an increasingly strong impact on the region in the future. The following factors have a decisive impact on many of its production activities, such as agriculture and hydropower generation.

Significant trends in precipitation and temperature have been observed in Central America with high confidence of the estimated changes being due to climate change. These trends are expected to continue into the 21st century.

Climate projections suggest increases in temperature and variations in precipitation for Central America by 2100. Changes in streamflow and water availability have been observed and projected to continue in the future, affecting already stressed water resources. The frequency of extreme weather events is also increasing. In addition, sea level rise (SLR) and human activities on coastal and marine ecosystems pose threats to fish stocks, corals, mangroves, seagrass beds, and other coastal habitats.

Changes in weather and climatic patterns are negatively affecting human health in Central America, by increasing morbidity and mortality from heat waves, floods, droughts, vector-borne diseases (malaria, dengue fever), cholera, and other diarrheal diseases, hantavirus and rotavirus, chronic kidney diseases, and psychosocial disorders. The pollution associated with pregnancy-related outcomes and diabetes, among others.

Vulnerabilities vary with geography, age, gender, race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status, and are rising in large cities. Central America is one of the most vulnerable regions to climate change, given the region's population growth and high vulnerability in health, water, sanitation and waste collection systems, nutrition, pollution, and food production in poor rural areas.

The evidence suggests that climate change is already having significant impacts in Latin America and the Caribbean and that, in all probability, its impacts will be even greater in the future. The effects in the region are unevenly distributed across countries and within countries, with some groups being more vulnerable than others. The overall impact is negative. For example, there is evidence of major impacts on agricultural activities, water resources, biodiversity, sea levels, forests, tourism, the population health and the regions cities.

### F.1 Economic and Financial Analysis

The analysis of the projected financial cash-flow of the intervention shows the projected financial income and expenses of CABEI and IFIs over a period of 20 years with the structure of a Risk Sharing Facility, where CABEI and the GCF share the risk of IPs in the operation. The analysis considers the different outputs of the project expenses as well as the revenue generated from the sale of the products produced by the farmers. It also takes into account the insurance cost and operations costs of administering the Programme implementation. The financial cash-flow simulates how the revolving fund is expected to be used during the period of implementation.

Based on the analysis of historical data of CABEI with previous programmes, and the results of the projected cash-flow, it is assumed that the funding for the project will be able to revolve approximately 1.5 times by the end of year 9. Until then, the revolving fund will be used to finance the production expenses of the farmers in the region. This way, the first 5 years US$26 million USD will be allocated and from year 6 to year 9 additional 12.5 million USD from the revolving process will be allotted. Thus after 10 years, a total of US$357.5 million is expected to be allocated to IPs for the production expenses in the region. The analysis assumes that the interest rate on loans is at an average rate for the loans allocated until year 8. From year 9 onwards, the analysis assumes a decreasing maturity of one year until year 12 and then a maturity of two years from year 13 to year 20. At this point, the revolving fund will be repaid in 6 years and the last loan will be repaid by the end of year 15. Approximately US$8.75 million are estimated in the analysis as interest payments for the revolving fund during its period of operation from which half of the money is assumed as a potential loss by the GCF and half by CABEI (USD $6,375 million each).

The analysis considers both the net operation margin of CABEI and IFIs, without the financing expenses; and the net financial margins for the revolving financing expenses for both institutions. For CABEI the net operation margin is positive from year 7 onwards and the net financial margin from year 10 onwards. The cumulative net financial margin is positive from year 12 onwards.

Under the assumptions made, the net operation margins and net financial margins are always positive, which is essential to support the economic case of this proposal.

### Economic and financial justification or the concessionality that GCF provides

Climate change in the region is already negatively impacting the economy and food security of the region, and this impact is expected to increase. The region has a high dependence on agriculture for its livelihoods. For example, it produces about 50% of the total agricultural production of the region and produces over 75.0% of the Central American food. According to FAO figures (FAO, 2016) in developing countries, agriculture absorbs 25.0% of the impact of climate change. These losses would have a direct impact on producers, most of whom operate as family businesses at subsistence levels.

Through the Programme, 5,000 MSMEs will have access to credit and technical assistance to implement and adopt strategies that will help them mitigate climate change impacts such as reducing production costs, reduction in damages and losses due to climate-related events, which in turn, has a great impact in the sustainability of the economy of the most vulnerable families of the region. Moreover, it allows increasing food security and preventing poverty increase. The region is also highly dependent on agro-biodiversity for its livelihoods. The conservation and protection of agrobiodiversity is key not only to the region but has also an impact on the world. The expected benefit is the stabilization of the environment and the reduction in climate change impacts.
