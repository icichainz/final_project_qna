## Provide an executive summary of the projectprogramme including:
- Climate change problem
- Proposed interventions
- Climate change co-benefits
- Climate impacts/benefits

The Climate Change problem facing Africa is substantial, with over 570 million people lacking access to electricity. Renewable energy (RE) can provide a solution to this problem and the potential for RE development in Africa could be undermining Africa's growth and stability on a continent. There exists a tremendous opportunity to make a tangible difference by scaling up renewable energy deployment across the continent.

### Proposed intervention
Leverage the LEAP Framework (a USD 900+ million) framework with the overarching objective to unlock local currency debt and overcome market barriers to support the growth of RE in Africa. In particular, the Framework, via a US$150 million grant, will provide concessional financing for the development of decentralized renewable energy (DRE) space through guarantees and subsidized debt, unlocking local currency debt finance and scaling up investment in DRE. The Framework will also provide technical assistance to governments to develop enabling policies, structure strong financial transactions, and support governments to create enabling policies for private investment. The Framework is being developed as a stand-alone initiative under the Green Climate Fund (GCF) Strategic Programme. This proposal focuses on decentralized and distributed renewable energy generation in six countries: Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Mozambique, Madagascar, and Guinea, based on the market potential for renewables and project pipelines of DRE initiatives being developed by ADB.

### Climate change mitigation
Scaling up renewable energy is a necessity to meet growing electricity demand while reducing greenhouse gas emissions. The current mix of energy sources in Africa is dominated by fossil fuels for power distribution; many African countries still face considerable challenges in achieving universal access to sustainable, affordable and reliable energy services. Renewable energy can provide an alternative to the use of fossil fuel-based solutions that serve the existing industrial, urban and rural customer base. Rural communities often rely on fossil-fuel based solutions such as kerosene for lighting and cooking; this is a major source of indoor air pollution which has been shown to cause damage to humans, the climate and pollute the environment. According to IEA's 2020 Africa Energy Outlook report, in SSA, energy access is still low with only 45% of the population having access to electricity. In addition, 80% of energy consumption in SSA, primarily for cooking. While African countries are facing growing demands for energy services, they also face increasing pressure from climate change and its impacts. The use of fossil-fuel based energy sources is a major contributor to climate change by promoting and supporting the transition from high-carbon electricity solutions to cleaner and more sustainable alternatives. It also contributes to clean energy solutions for rural populations who currently do not have access to electricity.

### Climate change adaptation co-benefits
Increasing the access of a population to clean and reliable energy (mini-grid and off-grid) can provide significant climate change adaptation co-benefits by increasing household capacity and resilience through economic, social and environmental co-benefits. The electricity systems and appliances that are being deployed under this Framework will have a direct impact on improving the quality of life for people's socio-economic resilience impacted such electrification. Access to knowledge and information via improved energy access can also improve health outcomes by increasing access to healthcare services (networks, government aid agencies etc) and recover faster from the impacts of disasters. Additionally, women and children will be able to spend more time on productive activities rather than spending hours each day on health from energy access, mainly through reduced vulnerability to pollution associated with charcoal and firewood collection, improved security and learning for schools.

### Climate impacts/benefits
By unlocking local currency debt and increasing the availability of financing, the LEAP Framework will increase access to clean and reliable energy (mini-grid and off-grid) in rural communities. This will have a direct impact on climate mitigation and co-benefits in the selected countries. Direct mitigation benefits include an additional 38MW of renewable energy capacity being deployed across the six countries with associated carbon dioxide reductions over the lifetime of the systems. The Framework aims to provide 1.18 million households and businesses with green, reliable energy solutions and productive use appliances.

While many African countries have targeted ambitious electricity access goals and a transition to renewable energy sources, the off-grid sector has been slow to respond. With Africa having abundant solar resources, the potential for decentralized renewable energy solutions such as mini-grids and solar home systems (SHS) is enormous. The pay-as-you-go model, using mobile payments, has introduced an innovative financing mechanism that has disrupted the ways payments are collected in the off-grid sector.

### Solar Home Systems (SHS)
Solar Home Systems provide rural, low-density populations with affordable and safe solar electricity. They usually span from basic packages, offering lighting and phone charging, to larger packages including solar powered appliances such as water pumps, fans, and televisions. The pay-as-you-go model allows SHS companies to reduce the costs and risks associated with payment collection. SHS are equipped with a rechargeable battery that stores electricity generated by photovoltaic cells. These systems can also present an electricity solution for sparsely populated areas and communities with low power loading. They provide a clean, reliable source of energy for lighting, charging mobile phones, and powering small appliances.

### Commercial and Industrial Solar
While commercial and industrial solar for businesses (C&I or captive power) is a nascent market in Africa, Bloomberg New Energy Finance projects that the cost of solar photovoltaic (PV) systems will be competitive with diesel generators by 2035. The high upfront costs of both diesel generators and the grid in focus countries are giving way for solar systems for businesses to become more attractive. Solar can also provide a reliable source of power for electricity access or as backup power in case of power cuts. Solar C&I helps to displace diesel generators in Sub-Saharan Africa, where diesel is expensive and difficult to transport.

### Market Dynamics
Despite market growth, cost reduction in technologies and improved policies, there remain challenges related to access to private capital and a lack of conducive enabling environment that are hindering the deployment at scale of C&I.

### Structure of the proposed Framework:

#### Component 1: De-risking

The feasibility report confirms both the advantages of local currency debt and the demand for commercial and local commercial banks to finance DRE projects. De-risking instruments will unlock commercial capital and may reduce overall project capital costs. GCF’s support will primarily take the form of an envelope for concessional credit enhancement (accelerated disbursement, interest rate reduction, etc.) and a guarantee pool.

The proposed de-risking instruments are based on pan-paice and pro-rata risk-sharing terms with the exception of pricing with ADB investments, including PSCs and debt in exchange for equity. The GCF will provide concessional credit enhancement (accelerated disbursement) to banks and provide the level of co-making and lending required to unlock private sector investors and developers into the sustainable DRE projects. This is achieved by providing a range of de-risking instruments, including structured financial intermediation structures to mitigate the risk of payment defaults of consumers. While the proposed instruments aim to provide a credit enhancement mechanism for banks, they are also designed to be flexible enough to accommodate other types of investors.

For example, SHS and C&I solutions are based on future cashflows of receivables, whilst mini-grids may rely on subsidies or guarantees. The de-risking instruments will be structured in such a way as to mitigate the financial risks experienced by projects and underlying country risk. In general, investors (i.e., GCF and ADB) sub-branches and first loss providers will provide the necessary capital for the project. This supports the objective of attracting commercial investors, who are prepared to finance the lower-risk senior tranche.

The LEAF (Local Currency Access Facility) Framework is a proposal to increase private sector involvement in financing solar energy projects, particularly in developing countries where access to local currency funding can be limited. The framework aims to leverage the strengths of local financial institutions by providing them with the tools and resources needed to finance large-scale solar projects.

### Objectives

The primary objectives of the LEAF Framework are:

- **Strengthening Local Financial Institutions:** By providing these institutions with the necessary expertise, capacity building, and access to standardized agreements, the framework aims to enhance their ability to offer financing solutions for solar energy projects.
- **Promoting Private Sector Participation:** The framework seeks to increase private sector involvement in solar energy projects by offering them a structured approach to accessing local currency funding.

### Key Components

The LEAF Framework includes several key components:

1. **Capacity Building and Training:** The framework provides training and capacity building for local financial institutions, focusing on the development of standardized agreements that can be used across different projects.
2. **Standardized Agreements:** These agreements are designed to streamline the process of financing solar energy projects by providing a clear set of terms and conditions.
3. **Local Currency Access Facilities (LCF):** The framework establishes LCFs, which provide local financial institutions with access to funding in their own currencies.

### Implementation

The LEAF Framework is implemented through a series of steps:

1. **Capacity Building:** Local financial institutions are trained on the use of standardized agreements and best practices for financing solar energy projects.
2. **Standardized Agreements Development:** The framework develops and disseminates standardized agreements that can be used by local financial institutions to finance solar energy projects.
3. **Local Currency Access Facilities Establishment:** LCFs are established, providing local financial institutions with access to funding in their own currencies.

### Long-Term Ownership and Exit Strategy

The LEAF Framework includes a long-term ownership and exit strategy for the projects it finances. This strategy is designed to ensure that the projects remain viable over the long term and can be exited by investors when appropriate.

### Financial Sustainability

The financial sustainability of the LEAF Framework is ensured through a combination of factors, including:

- **Long-Term Ownership:** Projects are owned by local financial institutions for an extended period.
- **Exit Strategy:** Projects are exited in a manner that ensures their continued operation and maintenance.
- **Financial Instruments:** The framework uses various financial instruments to ensure the long-term viability of projects.

### Conclusion

The LEAF Framework is designed to increase private sector involvement in financing solar energy projects, particularly in developing countries. By providing local financial institutions with the necessary tools and resources, the framework aims to promote sustainable development through the deployment of solar energy technologies.

## Due Diligence

Each sub-project under the Framework will be processed individually by the Bank. Any funding commitment to an underlying transaction will be subject to ADB's credit evaluation, due diligence and approval processes as described in the IDP. The Board of Directors has delegated the responsibility for conducting due diligence to Senior Management to be presented to the Board’s of Directors for approval, including various interdepartmental committees.

ADB will apply its diligence policies including Know-Your-Customer (KYC), Anti-Money Laundering (AML) and Combating the Financing of Terrorism (CFT) procedures and operational safeguards, and other evaluations to ensure that the underlying transaction is not used for illegal or illicit purposes. The ADB’s due diligence aims at mitigates, manage and monitor potential loss from integrity risks and to ensure that funds are used for their intended purpose. This includes ensuring that the underlying transaction complies with applicable laws and regulations; identification of sanctioned persons and entities, and identification of Politically Exposed Persons (PEPs) and other high-risk individuals; and ensuring compliance with ADB’s policies on anti-money laundering and combating money laundering policies, the ADB ensures that its financing operations and investments are not used for illegal or tax evasion purposes.

## Financial Management

Financial management will follow the ADB's Guidelines for Financial Management and Financial Analysis of Projects, which describes the Banks' policies and procedures with regard to financial management and financial analysis. The Bank’s financial management system is a comprehensive integrated information system, which covers budget planning and implementation, procurement, financial statement preparation and reporting, and internal audit. The Bank will conduct an annual assessment of the financial management systems of its implementing agencies as part of the final appraisal. The purpose of this assessment is to evaluate the executing agencies' accounting systems and internal control procedures in order to ensure that they are adequate for the implementation of ADB projects.

## Supervision and Portfolio Management

All funding commitments will be monitored for compliance and eligibility by the Bank. # Independent Technical Advisory Panel's assessment of FP168

## Proposal name: Leveraging Energy Access Finance (LEAF) Framework

### Accredited entity: African Development Bank (AfDB)

### Project/programme size: Large

## Assessment of the independent Technical Advisory Panel

### 1.1 Impact potential Scale: High

Access to energy is a critical challenge in much of Africa, and access to clean energy even more so. While considerable progress has been made over the last decade, lack of energy access continues to be a major constraint on economic development, poverty reduction, and health, contributing to continuing environmental degradation. In the commercial and industrial sector, where grid-power is available, it is often unreliable and expensive.

Specifically, the programme will address these challenges in six African countries: Ethiopia, Ghana, Guinea, Kenya, Nigeria and Togo, through investments in distributed renewable energy (DRE) projects such as solar panels, solar home systems (SHS) and renewables for commercial and industrial (C&I) sites. It is targeted at households and small businesses with off-grid energy who have created the possibility for DRE installations to compete on a favourable basis with grid electricity.

The programme is expected to directly deliver close to USD 1 billion in total financing, financing 386 MW of much-needed new capacity, leading to about 29.9 MCO2eq of emissions reductions and 500,000 new connections. The TAP has also reviewed the assumptions that financial institutions will continue to invest in the new DRE business lines created by the programme, and that these investments will be sufficient to ensure a steady increase in expected generation capacity added.

Following discussions with the independent TAP, the AfDB revised cost assumptions about the average capital requirements for all three forms of DRE proposed to be supported under the programme. The revised assumptions are based on the assumption of an increase in expected generation capacity added. The TAP welcomed this engagement from the accredited entity (AE) and raised it as a potential limitation level of the programme, and thereby also allowed the TAP to increase the impact rating.

### Operations and maintenance

The TAP has concerns about the assumptions being made regarding operations and maintenance (O&M) funding, which is presumed to be given, with no further involvement from the financial institutions. The TAP believes that this assumption may not necessarily ensure the upkeep of their operations, which is required to be able to continue to service the loans they have received; experience from the target countries indicates that this may not always be the case.

The TAP therefore requests that, as part of the operations manual of the programme, and to support the knowledge transfer to local financial institutions and final beneficiaries, a set


---

---

# of principles for sustainable O&M for DRE operations is developed as part of a knowledge and technical assistance (KTA) package to be utilized, for example, by local financial institutions during their credit assessment process.

## Overall, notwithstanding the observations on O&M, the independent TAP considers the mitigation measures to be adequate.

### 1.2 Paradigm shift potential Scale: High

The paradigm shift of the programme derives from two key sources:
- The intervention through local financial institutions, which is expected to deliver lasting changes in the market and the business environment for DRE projects.
- The substantial scaling up of DRE investment activity in the target countries which will in itself foster a change in the market. This will be achieved by changing the economic reality are lowered and a thriving DRE eco-system can evolve.

In combination, these two elements should be a powerful driver for lasting change in the target markets. It is expected that this will have positive impacts on clients and clients of the local financial institutions who are investing in DRE. At present, in a range of markets in Africa, DRE is not an attractive proposition to investors due to high risk perceptions and limited local financial investment plans for the continent are lacking the required funds to truly make a difference.

This programme therefore has the potential to deliver a substantial lift to the DRE market in these countries. It will do so by providing a new source of funding, which is either relying on development finance and foreign investors, who are unlikely to be as familiar with the local markets and business environment as local financial institutions can be.

This is therefore a well-targeted programme, combining deftly the strength of the AE in understanding the local markets with the concessional financing instruments of GCF. The focus on the provision of technical assistance will ensure that the programme has a positive wider systemic impacts that will generate paradigm shifts, such as the reduction of these local financial institutions’ risk perceptions and the creation of new business opportunities, and allowing them to build new lines of DRE business made possible by this programme.

# Introduction

The Leveraging Energy Access Finance (LEAF) Framework offers an important opportunity to apply a gender lens and assess the impact of energy access for women. The positive impact of the energy sector on women is experienced in their multiple roles as customers, entrepreneurs, and agents of change.

## Impact of access to energy on women

The countries targeted by LEAF—Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, Guinea, Ethiopia and Tunisia—count approximately 90 million women and girls who currently have no access to clean and reliable energy. Many of these women live in rural areas. Promoting access to decentralized affordable energy technologies is essential for improving their quality of life and economic empowerment are essential for the overall improvement of people's living conditions. The social, economic, and environmental benefits of energy access for women include:

- Increased opportunities for income generating activities: Access to energy increases opportunities for women to start small businesses or support new income generating activities. For example, women can use solar lights to work late into the night, saving time by replacing manual labor and reducing the drudgery of fetching fuel wood and water, tasks that usually fall to women, increases their engagement in income-generating activities. Women can also use energy for cooking, lighting, and heating in the organization of everyday chores. Finally, women are more likely to become wage earners when they have access to energy, which improves their economic security.
- Financial independence: additional income generated as a result from access to clean energy or employment within the DRE sector, allows women to contribute to household expenses and improve family living standards.
- Tackling gender stereotypes: businesswomen benefit from increased status at home and in society.
- Strengthening household economic resilience: Income generated as a result of access enables women to purchase goods that they would otherwise have spent on expensive fuel, such as kerosene increases household economic resilience.
- Creating safer communities: access to lighting creates safer communities; households avoid the risk of theft or burglary at night. Women can also use energy for cooking in some cases, even death; and community members experience enhanced safety as they are able to see better at night.
- Creating a ripple effect: putting income and energy in women's hands can have powerful economic and social benefits for their families and communities.
- Improved education: access to lighting enables girls to study longer into the evening.
- Improved health: access to clean energy improved women’s health through reduction of indoor air pollution.
- Reduced violence: the energy sector contributes to efforts to combat Gender-Based Violence. Evidence demonstrates that women in electrified households report significantly lower acceptance of domestic violence.

# Energy and gender inequality

The countries selected by LEAF have different national electrification rates, however they share similarities in terms of access to and control over energy, as well as persistent gender gaps.

Women in the selected countries for LEAF, especially those living in rural areas, are disadvantaged with respect to energy as compared to their male counterparts. Overall, women tend to be responsible for household decision-making on energy use and related decisions, despite having different preferences. Agricultural yields of female farmers often are adversely affected by the inability to mobilize sufficient labour and energy efficient equipment when needed.

On average, female-headed businesses have a reduced ability to invest in productivity enhancing energy assets. In addition, women energy entrepreneurs have been shown to have more limited access to credit than men, as well as fewer connections with business networks, and consequently smaller businesses. Conditions of energy poverty foster a distinctly gendered experience with regards to energy needs, access and use that is not present at higher levels of energy access.

Overall lagging levels in access to clean energy, is critical to advancing development objectives. Gender inequalities and underlying issues are further addressed in the country-level assessments. This information feeds into the gender action plan for LEAF to support gender equality and addressing some of the remaining gender gaps that can be addressed by LEAF.


## Important gender aspects for LEAF

- **Energy access preferences:** Both women and men desire improved access to energy. There are minor differences related to their respective preferences for particular types of lighting technologies.
- **Entrepreneurship:** Access to renewable energy provides a range of options and economic opportunities for both women and men. In the small business sector, there are multiple examples where women have been able to start businesses with different preferences than men; the types of businesses women tend to operate drive these preferences.
- **Key buyers:** Women have significant weight in household decisions related to the purchase and use of energy technologies (35% of energy access customers is women), which underlines their importance as a target group for marketing and educational messages.
- **Health:** Increased awareness of the indoor air pollution health risks caused by fuel-based lighting is stimulating demand for clean alternatives in many countries. Women are more affected by this phenomenon as they typically spend more time at home.

### Gender and Energy

Despite Nigeria’s abundance of fossil and renewable energy resources, Nigerians still experience acute energy poverty; they either lack access to modern energy sources or have to cope with inadequate supply and poor quality. About 80% of Nigerians use solid fuel for cooking and heating in their homes, which is a major cause of indoor air pollution.

In Nigeria, electricity access stands at 54%, with 87% of urban areas and 23% of rural areas with access<sup>1</sup>.

As a member of ICPENAS, Nigeria adopted a concerted approach to the implementation of the Sustainable Energy for All (SEforALL) Action Agenda. This included the development of the Action Agenda alongside the Renewable Energy and Energy Efficiency Action Plans, and their formulation into National Energy Policy. The government aims to increase electricity access to 75% (urban up to 90%), and rural up to 60%) by 2020 and to 90% by 2030.

The installed electricity generation capacity is expected to increase from 7560 MW in 2015 to 14,000 MW by 2030 and energy efficiency will increase by at least 20% by 2020 and 50% by 2030.

Women and girls are disproportionately and more severely affected by the lack of access to energy. Nigeria’s 2013 National Energy Policy includes as one of its primary objectives, the promotion of gender equality in energy access and use. Women's needs are defined as primarily related to their care economy tasks in the household (Energy Commission of Nigeria, 2013).

The use of traditional biomass for cooking at more than 69% has an impact on women, who are mostly involved in collecting woods and domestic work. Increasing degradation of these natural resources causes them to spend more time and physical effort finding and bringing home firewood needs.

There are also serious health impacts associated with burning traditional biomass fuels. Open fires in the home produce unventilated smoke as well as a high concentration of carbon monoxide and other pollutants, which women and children, who are most often inside the house, are exposed to. This exposure increases the risk of diseases, burns to children and injuries to women from carrying wood.

Limited access to energy has consequences to the agricultural sector and living conditions of households, especially those headed by women. For instance, rice production and processing in Nigeria is a labor-intensive activity that requires a lot of time and energy for women. Typical issues limiting economies of large-scale rice production include: lack of access to improved seeds, fertilizers, irrigation systems, and machinery (e.g., threshing and food processing). Most women especially in the western part of the country are into rice farming, but they have major issues regarding processing since the machinery for processing is not always available.

3. Conclusion

The gender assessment highlights the main gender gaps in the respective countries targeted by the LEAF programme, as well as important aspects and the impact related to increasing access to energy and women. While gender gaps are present in many areas, the LEAF programme will focus on addressing the disparities in the decentralized renewable energy sector.

There are some differences between the countries covered by LEAF, in terms of the mix of energy sources and level of women's participation. However, many of the core challenges linked to access to clean energy for women remain common across all countries. Based on analysis and interventions of the programme, we have identified two key areas where we can apply a gender lens and develop specific activities to reduce some of the remaining gender gaps in the context of LEAF: 1) increasing women's economic empowerment through increased financial inclusion and entrepreneurship by 1) increasing women as consumers of energy access solutions, as well as 2) increasing their participation in the workforce and as leaders of energy access businesses.

### Enhancing access to finance and fostering job creation for women

Female participation in the labour force in the six countries lies between 40-50%, with the exception of Tunisia which is lower. The energy sector has still some steps to take, with only 30% in renewable energy and 28% in female representation in the energy sector overall.

According to the IRENA and the gender analysis, four categories of barriers exist with regards to employment of women in the DRE sector: 1) perceptions of gender roles and the nature of work in the sector; 2) participation of women in STEM (science, technology, engineering, maths); 3) lack of access to finance and credit for women entrepreneurs; and 4) barriers related to renewable energy; and 4) prevalent hiring practices, as well as an unequal access to employment entry points, such as apprenticeships.

By requiring DRE companies to demonstrate their commitment and approach to increasing their share of female employees, and providing support to DRE companies to develop gender inclusive recruitment and HR policies, and career plans, LEAF aims to enhance job creation for women and effectively address those barriers to reducing existing gender gaps.

Furthermore, LEAF aims to increase access to finance and payment plans for women to purchase energy solutions. The Financial Inclusion Taskforce encourages DRE companies to provide financing schemes benefiting female customers.

In TA support provided to financial institutions, gender will be included in the training to enhance their ability to create gender-responsive appraisal tools or standardized gender-responsiveness assessment criteria.

The Program aims to facilitate access to credit for women for SIES, green mini-grid and captive power solutions, and is estimated to create 15,600 jobs for women during the scale-up of DRE companies.

## Implementation of the gender action plan

The gender activities planned in the gender action plan will coordinated, implemented and monitored by a gender expert recruited by the AE through consulting services arranged by the TA team. The gender expert will be responsible for: (i) developing country-level gender assessments; (ii) developing and refining national gender action plans and activities; (iii) responsible for the implementation of gender related activities under the programme; (iv) supporting DRR and disaster risk reduction activities in line with the national gender indicators and targets of the national gender action plan approved by the AE; (v) monitoring and evaluating the progress made on the gender action plan, including the development and updating the annual LEAF gender reports. Most of the gender activities will be implemented by consulting firms or other partners as part of a larger assignment.
