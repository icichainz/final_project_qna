African agriculture also presents a huge opportunity given its underdeveloped nature, as development in the sector can be aligned with climate resilience objectives without the costs of undermining existing developments and retraining.

Based on our experience in the sector, Acumen believes that to accelerate the achievement of climate resilience, in addition to supporting specific climate resilience initiatives, the broader enabling systems and services also need to be supported. Acumen believes this could be achieved through investing in aggregator platforms that act as a channel through which multiple services can be delivered to the farmer. Not only are these businesses more impactful due to multiple service delivery but are also financially more resilient compared to single-product focused enterprises.

- **Aggregator Platforms:** Agribusinesses that provide bundled solutions to two or more key needs of the farmer (i.e., inputs, knowledge, finance, buyers, etc.). Essentially, these platforms aim to improve smallholder farmers' access to and integration with the market.

- **Innovative Financial Services:** Innovative payment, credit and insurance solution providers to farmers at scale

We believe Aggregator, Digital Platform and Innovative Financial Services business models are key to effectively addressing the structural socio-economic challenges that are known to inhibit smallholder farmers' adaptive responses to climate change.

### B.3. Financial Markets Overview (if applicable)

How market price or expected commercial rate of return was (non-concessional) determined?

Social impact is built into both the operations and the investment structure of ARAF. The ARAF Fund Manager will only achieve a 25% profit from ARAF’s profits if the Financial and Impact Hurdle Rates have been achieved. The Financial Hurdle Rate for ARAF is 3% and Impact Hurdle Rate is 5 million lives impacted. The ARAF Fund Manager will receive 25% of ARAF's profits to the extent that the Financial and the Impact Hurdle Rate have been achieved. If ARAF achieves only one or both of these Hurdles, then 100% of profit will be adjusted downward in proportion to the percentage of the Target, then 50% share in profits will be adjusted downward in proportion to the percentage of the Impact Target.

Given the relatively early state of the impact investing sector, and more specifically venture capital and impact investments in agribusinesses in Africa, there are limited relevant and comparable data points from other funds that could provide support for this target rate. ## The Promise of Platform Businesses

Acumen, from its decade long investing experience of experimenting with a range of business models, has concluded in its new strategy that Platform businesses are one of the most powerful channels for impacting smallholder farmers. Platform businesses are defined as those that provide access to multiple services and resources to smallholder farmers, which can be delivered to the farmer. In addition, these businesses are also financially more resilient compared to single product companies. Acumen has identified two types of platform business models: Agribusiness Platforms and Digital Platforms.

### Agribusiness Platforms

Acumen has invested in a number of agribusiness platforms that have been successful in scaling up services across Uganda that sources organic cotton from 70,000 smallholder farmers and in the process provides access to improved cotton seeds, fertilizer, credit, and market linkages. These platforms are also financially more resilient compared to being a seed company only selling seeds or a fertilizer company only selling fertilizer to farmers. Non-platform businesses pivoting into platform business models can be an effective way of addressing some of the key challenges that affect their economic viability. For example, a seed company will find it difficult to push a new seed, without farmers having access to active extension support (on how to use that seed) and affordable credit. Further, building up its own distribution network is also expensive for a small seed company. A platform business model can be the perfect solution. On the other hand, with multiple offerings that complement each other, some of the adoption and distribution risks are mitigated by the platform business model.

We believe Aggregator, Digital Platform and Innovative Financial Services business models are key to effectively addressing the structural socioeconomic challenges that are known to inhibit smallholder farmers' adaptive responses to climate change.

### 1. "One-Stop" Visibility and Access
Smallholder farmers often interact with a splintered network of intermediaries who together provide access to agronomic information, extension services, credit, inputs, and markets, often at a high cost or in an inefficient manner. Improving channels, Services, Aggregators, and market linkages can help smallholders get the right destinations for all or most of their needs as a bundled package, improving both the quality and stability of farmer incomes and therefore strengthening their ability to adapt to climate change.

### 2. Stable and Predictable Cash Flows
Stable and predictable cash flows helps farmers manage daily expenses better, a finding that has resonated across many different contexts. By providing access to credit at an affordable rate, having productive income helps farmers plan and start building a base of savings and assets that they can use to reduce the impact of any kind of shock. Today, many smallholder farmers' inability to access credit and markets results in uncertainty about their future cash flows and thus their ability to adapt to climate change.

### 3. Access to Inputs and Services
More consistent and often higher incomes as a result of linkage to aggregators and ICT enterprises allow smallholder farmers to invest more in inputs, such as seeds, fertilizers, and other climate change adaptation solutions that would otherwise have been either unaffordable or too risky to pursue.

### 4. Scaling Up Innovations
So much value remains untapped by smallholder farmers because they do not have access to the right information, skills, tools and equipment, and inputs. Many organizations have tried providing these services as piecemeal solutions but have found that it is difficult for smallholders to adopt new innovations and hestitate to pay for isolated interventions. Often rightly so, because we have also learned that these technologies are most effective when they are combined with one or more of the other component offerings of aggregators and digital platform enterprises.

### Conclusion
In acumen, investors in companies that offer smallholder farmers the dual and inextricably linked value propositions of becoming both financially and environmentally sustainable. These solutions not only account for the private sector's role in addressing climate change but also help to overcome many of the socioeconomic barriers to climate change adaptation. In the face of climate change and unpredictable weather patterns, smallholder farmers need access to affordable inputs that will help them increase their yields and build soil capacity. In addition to predominantly inhabiting arid regions, smallholder farmers’ vulnerability to climate change is exacerbated by low levels of education, poor infrastructure, limited access to credit and extension services, and with key challenges:

- When the harvesters are ready to sell their harvest, the millerman is usually the off-taker and will offer prices lower by 20–40% compared to market prices. These price margins are skimmed by the millerman in the name of service fees, which reduces farmers’ income and reduces their ability to save and invest in new and improved climate-resilient inputs and practices.
- Inputs provided by these millermen are often adulterated leading to poor yields for farmers. That said, climate-related shocks such as droughts and floods can cause a significant drop in the quality of crops harvested and limit access to them.

Farmers pay high rates on their inputs in credit. It’s typically the local influential millerman that provides inputs on credit to farmers and is also the off-taker for the farmer since farmers do not have direct access to buyers. Credit costs are often as much as 20% per month, which is unsustainable for most smallholder farmers. Such high rates leave very little upside for the farmer, if any or risks putting them in a debt trap in case market prices at this time of year are lower than what they paid for inputs.

There is virtually no extension or training support available to farmers in large part of Africa. There are limited extension services and those that exist are often not well-funded and poorly managed. This exposes farmers to magnified risks specifically given the changing weather patterns. Farmers are unable to put practices into place that will help them mitigate against climate shocks.

In a post study of 22 sub-Saharan African countries, crop yields increased by 40 percent in the first year after agricultural extension was offered. Given the lack of quality extension, climate smart extension is virtually non-existent. To be effective, extension services need to be provided at scale and need to be coordinated to incorporate climate resilience lenses while cost-effective delivery channels need to be enabled.

# ACUMEN INSIGHTS ON SMALLHOLDER FARMING

1. Climate change is the single biggest challenge being faced by smallholder farmers and adaptation to climate change is key to long term sustainable development of sector.
2. Lack of **gaps** and **ability** between smallholder farmers and value chain actors is a major factor.
3. Adoption of innovative inputs by farmers is slow even if the value proposition is compelling.
4. Smallholder farmers have a strong preference for one-stop solutions.
5. Farmer decisions are deeply influenced by uncertainty associated with farm income, largely linked to climate.

# H.1. Logic Framework

## H.1.1. Paradigm Shift Objectives and Impacts at the Fund Level™

### Paradigm shift objectives

Acamun strongly believes in the need to create a Paradigm Shift through our interventions that is much greater than the already significant impact we have on the individual enterprises we invest in. Our goal is not only to increase their profitability, but also to enable them to create green and sustainable Systems Change by testing and proving new commercial business models that are more resilient to climate change impacts and better aligned with their core and innovative needs.

This means that our invested enterprises need to listen closely to the real needs of low income smallholders, as the success of their core business models depends on it. This will enable them to better serve their customers/suppliers (safeguarded by our Lean Data metrics; see Section E1).

ARAF will be the world's first climate change fund that is focused on agribusiness SMEs that serve BOP populations in Africa. With partnership from the GCF, this Fund will demonstrate that smallholder farmers can be part of a solution to climate change and not just a problem. Catalyzing millions of dollars in private capital in climate resilient agriculture in Africa, Africa presents an opportunity for the world to see how a combination of innovative financial climate aligned developments can put the sector on the path of sustainable development while aligning with the need for unwinding existing developments and costs of unlearning.

### Increased climate-resilient sustainable development

Acamun believes that to truly create a paradigm shift towards a business ecosystem that enables climate resilience, we will not only have to change our investment approach rather than piecemeal solutions. Therefore, in addition to investing in agribusinesses that directly enable climate resilience, we will also invest in the enabling infrastructure and scale up of the resulting climate-resilient systems as strongly believe that these are key to the development of an enabling ecosystem for sustainable agriculture in the target countries (see Key Performance Indicators). This will be achieved through a multi-stakeholder approach focused through the addition of an individual on the ARAF Investment Committee who shall be responsible for ensuring that all investments made by the Fund are aligned with our own investment decisions that are not reasonably expected to contribute to the climate adaptation goals of the fund (see Section C7).

In addition, through ARAF, Acamun will also look to invest in partnership with the public sector and global organizations with experience in this space and would leverage their expertise to build mutually beneficial partnerships. This will further allow Acamun to leverage its learnings and experience in climate resilience to influence how the public sector and corporates approach challenges in agriculture.
