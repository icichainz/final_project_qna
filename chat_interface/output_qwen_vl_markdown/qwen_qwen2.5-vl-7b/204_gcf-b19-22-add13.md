### A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

The World Bank (the Bank) has agreed with the Government of Vietnam (Govt.) to adopt a holistic approach to scaling up energy efficiency in industrial enterprises, which will unlock the huge potential for energy savings and greenhouse gas (GHG) emission reductions. The proposed Scaling Up Energy Efficiency for Industrial Enterprises in Vietnam Project (Project) aims at achieving an estimated 10% reduction in primary energy consumption, i.e., 25% GHG emission reduction compared to the Business As Usual scenario with international support, under the Paris Agreement.

Overall, the Project comprises an integrated package of credit risk mitigation, technical assistance and capacity building activities to various stakeholders from public entities to local financial institutions and industrial enterprises, complemented by a loan component. The Project is expected to address the following challenges: limited access to finance and capacity of stakeholders, high project risk perceptions, and the existence of an insufficient policy and regulatory framework that governs EE in the industrial sector.

The Project will be implemented through two inter-related components:

- **Component 1:** US$78 million GCF Risk Sharing Facility (GCF-RSF)
- **Component 2:** US$10 million World Bank and GCF Technical Assistance (WB/GCF-TA)

The Project is complemented by:
- **IBRD Loan component:** US$34.5 million World Bank Energy Efficiency Credit Line - Vietnam Energy Efficiency for Industrial Enterprises in the Banking Sector Project (WB-VEEIE)

The joint implementation of the two components and the GCF supporting project will promote a market-driven approach to industrial EE, with the aim of creating an enabling environment for local financial institutions and industrial enterprises developing a new EE product line. With financial and technical support from the World Bank and the GCF, the Project and the supporting IBRD Loan will mobilize approximately US$47.3 million of EE investments, supporting over 100 industrial enterprises in Vietnam to scale up their energy efficiency investments through financing and capacity building over the lifetime of the Investment.

Ultimately, the Project will contribute to a paradigm shift in the nascent EE market, by providing know-how and experience and by creating demand for EE products and services, thereby creating an enabling environment for social financial institutions and industrial enterprises to scale up investments in energy efficiency.

The GCF-RSF will provide PFIs with partial credit risk guarantees to cover loans extended to IEAs for EE investments. It is expected that these loans will be provided on concessional terms and at a lower interest rate than the market rate. In addition, the guarantee offered by the IRRD can be used in combination with other financial instruments such as grants or equity financing provided through the IRRD loan. All investments mobilised under Component 1 are additional to the investments made under the World Bank's VEEIE TA and WB-VEEIE (IRRD co-financing) projects. The GCF-RSF guarantee will be available on loans or tranches of loans (in case of parallel financing). Any default on loan repayment to PFIs will be covered by the GCF-RSF, expected to be 50 percent of the loss on average during the project life. The Program Implementing Entity (PIE) managing the RSF will have flexibility in setting the coverage level for each tranche of loans and can choose between a fixed coverage level or a variable coverage level. A fixed guarantee lending could but provide an optimal level of leverage to encourage PFIs to lend while at the same time providing certainty on the risk profile of the facility. A variable coverage level, which would be based on a coverage level to be considered acceptable by the market, although the average ratio in the first 2-1/2 years of the project may need to be higher to increase the attractiveness of the guarantee product to PFIs. After the ramp-up period, and assuming that the facility is performing well, the coverage ratio could be reduced to a level between 30 percent and 50 percent or less to mobilise more commercial financing. The RSF will cover losses pro rata (not as a first loss) with the PFIs and IRRD in proportion of their respective commitments.

The GCF-RSF grant will cover administrative start-up costs and operating expenses for the first two years, and provide initial seed capital for possible sub-guarantee payouts during the early stages of the operation. After an initial ramp-up period, the amount of grant funding provided by IRRD to the RSF would be sufficient to cover GCF-RSF operating expenses, IBRD/GCF guarantee fees, and guarantee payouts for expected losses from the covered loan portfolio. ## B.3. Financial Markets Overview (if applicable)

**Sector overview:** Vietnam's financial sector is a bank-based system. According to the SBV, by the end of 2015 the banks and other deposit-taking institutions accounted for about 8 times of GDP and accounted for more than 90 percent of financial sector's total assets.

By the end of 2016, the banking sector consisted of 79 banking entities including a Fully State owned commercial banks, 33 foreign banks, 45 joint venture banks, and 10 rural credit cooperatives. The share of foreign banks in terms of assets is about five percent of the entire banking system. Much of the rapid credit growth in recent years has occurred through the banking sector.

The SBV has been implementing a series of measures to ensure that the NPL ratio remains under control, with the targeted 15-18 percent in the beginning of the year. However, continuing weaknesses include undercapitalization and a subdued market for non-performing loans (NPLs). The NPL ratio at the end of 2016 was around 3.4% of total assets, that is lowered through recent debt restructuring. The NPL ratio across the sector was around 2.8% at the end of 2015.

Capital markets in Vietnam remain small and underdeveloped but growing. As reported by the MOF: by the end of 2016, the combined market capitalization of the stock exchange and the bond market reached VND34 trillion (approximately USD12 billion). Vietnam's formal market is highly retail-based in nature with more than 98 percent of accounts registered with the Hanoi Stock Exchange.

This measure has dual impact, inducing the banks to increase their capital adequacy ratios and reducing the interest rate spread between deposit rates and loan rates.

# C1. Bilateral Contact

## Country Context:
Vietnam is one of the most energy-intensive countries in East Asia. Its energy intensity of GDP is steadily increasing, and its energy elasticity of GDP is estimated at 2, compared to less than 1 for most countries. As a result, the final energy consumption tripled over the past decade. Industrial growth has been one of the key drivers of Vietnam's economic development since the early 1990s.

## Green Climate Fund (GCF) Engagement:
Vietnam is expected to be among the top five recipients by 2030. According to World Bank and 2030, Vietnam's existing GHG emissions are projected to increase by 50 percent per capita emissions, and energy intensity of GDP by 20 percent.

The government recognized the importance of green growth and passed the Vietnam Green Growth Strategy for the period 2011-2020. The strategy aims at reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from all sectors through increased investments in technologies that reduce GHG emissions, increasing energy efficiency, promoting renewable energy sources, improving management of natural resources and improved competitiveness of the economy, which will be achieved through increased investments in technology, infrastructure, human capital development, and institutional reforms.

The government has set a target to reduce its carbon intensity by 40 percent compared to business-as-usual case. ## Taxation:

Taxation that could be relevant to industrial energy efficiency includes:

- **Enterprise income tax:** Taxable income is defined as total revenue less deductible expenses (depreciation, cost of production, etc.). The taxable income can be carried forward up to 5 years.
- **Value Added Tax (VAT):** Applies to most goods, uses credit method. Exemptions include agricultural production, salt, some imported equipment, credit, business services, education.
- **Imports and exports:** Subject to import/export taxes. Some exemptions for aid, goods in transit, education, research, for export processing, and certain machinery & equipment (Rates vary from 0% to 60%, with most in the 1%, 3%, 5%, 10%, 15% brackets).
- **Export incentives:** Export tax is zero. Cashback: Wood - 2.0%; Metal - 4.0%; Textile - 5.0%; Wool - 5.0%; Cashmere - 4%.

There are tax incentives and other forms of support to be offered by law to manufacturers and producers of EE products and technologies. Article 41 of the Law on Energy Efficiency and Conservation prescribes that "Organizations and individuals who manufacture, produce, import, export or use energy-efficient products and technologies and energy-saving technologies are entitled to incentives and supports" which include a) incentives on import and export duties and enterprises' income tax; b) preferential interest rates for loans from the state budget and other financial institutions such as the development bank, the fund for science and technology development support, the national fund for technological renovation and the environment facility and supports from the national programs on hi-tech development and application of new technologies in industry. ### D1. Value Added for GCF Involvement

The GCF-RSF, to be capitalized by the GCF, is essential for successful scale-up of energy efficiency lending in Vietnam.

The provision of partial credit risk guarantees to PFIs will encourage them to extend EE loans from their own resources beyond the current limits of their balance sheets. The use of a combination of equity and debt financing for EE lending is still considered a high-risk product line by financial institutions in Vietnam. While the TA and capacity building activities supported under the GCF-RSF are expected to reduce the risk profile, PFIs will continue to require additional leverage to cover the necessary financial risk mitigation to alleviate borrower credit concerns and expand funding for EE.

Based on the initial seed capital of US$3 million grant and US$75 million guarantee, the total sub-portfolio issuance by PFIs is estimated at US$108.4 million. This includes US$92.6 million in covered loans and associated sub-portfolio amortize. Adding the same amount of uncovered PFI lending and 20 percent of equity from EE and other sources will result in a total loan portfolio size of US$135.7 million. The expected leverage effect of GCF-RSF financing therefore will be 3.2 times. This is additional to the US$156.8 million of investment mobilized through the WB-VEEIE, as the GCF-RSF does not offer guarantee World Bank-funded EE loans.

## Figure D: Leveraging Effect of IBRD/GCF Guarantee

The IBRD/GCF guarantee represents a targeted solution to mobilize private financing. Sharing credit risk of EE loans allowed by PFIs and other financial institutions will enable them to extend more EE loans in the Vietnamese energy sector. The IBRD/GCF guarantee can be used to mitigate credit risk associated with implementing EE investments using commercial financing. The IBRD/GCF guarantee is therefore uniquely positioned to support the commercial transactions that are critical for scaling up EE lending, as it allows PFIs and other financial institutions to extend more loans than would otherwise be possible.

The conventional terms of IBRD/GCF guarantees allow PFIs and other participants in the market to become acquainted with the risks of EE financing at a lower cost. The benefit of concessionality will be passed on end-users, including PFIs, ES and EE service providers which form the EE market. Without GCF intervention, it would take considerably longer for existing PFIs to extend more loans than they currently do. In addition, the GCF-RSF can provide greater access to credit for selected borrowers and more easily than many borrowers could buy. By expediting market development and learning by doing, the IBRD/GCF guarantee will unlock the considerable potential for energy saving measures and GHG emission reduction in Vietnam.

# D2. Exit Strategy

The Project has been designed to ensure that experience and exposure gained by PFIIs, IEs and EE service providers during the project period will be used for EEF lending activities in the post-project period. The Project reflects lessons learned from other countries and is aimed at minimizing risks to the IBRD/GCF guarantees.

## After Project Close

At Project close, it is expected that EEF lending will continue by PFIIs and other financial institutions. Several studies conducted during the project have provided valuable insights into how PFIIs can be supported in developing sound EE lending business models through hands-on assistance to identify and develop demonstration projects, building on experiences gained by other local banks. Dissemination of the early experiences of the PFIIs and IEs will continue as part of the Project’s exit strategy. The Project team has also been working closely with the PFIIs that the EEF will be investing in EE lending to specific industries and project types, based on their project portfolios and expertise. This close collaboration is expected to result in a better understanding by the PFIIs of how to finance financially sound EE projects, lower transaction costs for evaluation and appraisal, and thereby gain confidence to scale up as EEF lenders.

The Project team has also been working closely with the IE that will be investing in EE lending to specific industries and project types. This close collaboration is expected to result in a better understanding by the IE of how to finance financially sound EE projects, lower transaction costs for evaluation and appraisal, and thereby gain confidence to scale up as EEF lenders.

Based on experience from other countries, risk sharing facilities can be effective at increasing the capacity and interest of PFIIs in identifying profitable EE financing opportunities. The proposed approach for a risk sharing facility allows for substantial flexibility in terms of the type of projects that are eligible for funding, the amount of funds available, and the availability period. The project design is based on long-term sustainability with the globally tested expectation that PFIIs will continue to provide EE financing after such a risk-sharing mechanism is completed.

The proposed approach on risk sharing facility establishes several key operational principles. These include: (a) careful selection of PFIIs, IEs and EE projects against well-defined criteria; (b) inclusion of multiple PFIIs to encourage competitive financing; (c) requirement for dedicated project teams within the PFIIs with appropriate performance incentives; (d) provision of TA for EEF lending activities development and capacity building of both PFIIs and IEs; and (e) implementation of comprehensive marketing and business development efforts.

## E.6.4: Applicability of Risk Sharing Facilities

International experience demonstrated that dedicated credit lines are effective at increasing the capacity and interest of PFIs in mainstreaming the EE financing business line through a learning-by-doing process and at changing the behavior of PFIs. The GCF risk sharing facility is designed to provide a dedicated credit line as the best prospect for program sustainability. Evidence is accumulating that PFIs continue to provide EE financing after the credit line commitment is completed.

Based on experience in other countries, risk sharing facilities can also be effective at increasing the capacity and interest of PFIs in identifying profitable EE financing opportunities. The proposed approach for a risk sharing facility allows PFIs to identify projects with high potential for EE financing that are not yet bankable or that need to be re-volved during the availability period. The project design is based on long-term sustainability with the globally tested expectation that PFIs continue to provide EE financing after the Facility is closed.

The proposed approach will also allow PFIs to identify and finance projects in countries where there may be particular operational differences. When these financial interventions are well combined with proper technical assistance and capacity building for the initial project implementation, and when the overall operation is well prepared and supported, the operation will be able to open up a new market of EE financing in a market-driven sustainable manner.
