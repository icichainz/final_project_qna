A.21. Executive summary (max. 750 words, approximately 1.5 pages)

The people of Barbados are being severely impacted by climate change, constraining the sustainable development of the country’s economy and threatening its most vulnerable communities. These climate-related impacts are driven largely by the increased frequency and intensity of tropical storms and hurricanes – along with the associated flooding, landslides, and coastal erosion. The resulting loss of agricultural land, reduced soil moisture, and increased aridity. Urgent adaptation action is therefore needed to reduce the wide-reaching impacts on key economic sectors and secure a sustainable future for all Barbadians. In addition to these core adaptation actions, the project will also support the country’s efforts in transitioning towards renewable energy by 2030. These ambitious targets are intended to demonstrate the country's commitment to climate action, as well as serve as an example to the rest of the world of the feasibility of the transition when implemented at scale.

Given the existing fiscal constraints, Barbados' resilience strategy — encompassed in the Roads to Reefs Programme (R2R) — recognizes the need to partner with the private sector to mobilize finance for innovative climate investments that are, or can be made, bankable. Such an approach will enable the government to direct the resources of the private sector towards projects that have a high likelihood of generating positive financial returns.

There is a discernible gap in the market in Barbados and the Caribbean for a financial institution that can provide facilities for existing financial institutions and capital markets to mobilize funds, whether through conventional bank lending or alternative securities revenue stream revenue driven from climate change mitigation and adaptation action. There are also opportunities to create new sources of private capital mobilization.

To address this urgent need for climate finance in Barbados, the proposed project will establish a sustainable climate finance vehicle—the Blue Green Bank (BGB)—to stimulate innovation in the financial sector with the goal of mobilizing the securities and loan markets in order to unlock additional funding for climate-resilient initiatives. The BGB will be structured as an independent entity that can partner with existing financial institutions, including other financing players — such as commercial banks, credit unions, pension funds, and insurance companies — to provide them with a platform to access new sources of capital and to create new opportunities for these key financial institutions.

For such institutions to become involved in climate change action, the leadership should be provided by a third party putting the deal together. For which it must establish the appropriate capacity in technical and HR terms, as well as appropriate management and institutional arrangements.

The BGB is designed to become a significant institution for practical and strategic financing of climate change adaptation and mitigation projects that are aligned with Barbados' National Determined Contributions (NDCs) on resilience and emissions reduction targets. As a partnership which brings together public (Government of Barbados), private, and civil society sectors, the BGB will have the potential for green financing by ensuring that new projects align with Barbados’ National Determined Contributions (NDCs). The BGB will be structured as an independent entity that can partner with existing financial institutions but to act as a partner and support system for them in this underdeveloped market segment.

To this end, the bank will be wholesale in operation, providing support in the form of funds, guarantees or other forms of credit enhancement. It is envisaged that the BGB will provide such support through its relationship with commercial banks, credit unions, insurance companies etc to facilitate the funding of blue/green climate-responsive programmes and projects. The BGB will also serve as a conduit between local financial institutions, international investors and the Government of Barbados, allowing the BGB to quickly market opportunities, and provide feedback and data to inform policy decisions more effectively.

Once established, the BGB will engage with investors, including private investment funds, development banks, multilateral agencies, and other public sector entities. The BGB will maintain a debt finance to equity ratio of 7:1, aiming to mobilize at least US$5.8 billion of investment over its first five years.

Overall, the BGB will promote a paradigm shift towards investment in resilient livelihoods and low-carbon technologies with emphasis on low-income vulnerable households and the energy, housing, agriculture and transport sectors. The BGB will also provide support to existing financial institutions that are already active in support of a more resilient and sustainable Caribbean. Finally, by focusing on climate resilience and adaptation action in Barbados without the need for further injections of money by the Government and GCF. This will create new opportunities in the banking sector, with the potential to scale beyond Barbados into the broader Caribbean region over time.

### Country context and natural environment

**Country context**

Barbados is a small island state with a land area of only 432 km² and a population of 291,200 (2021) at a density of 681 persons per km², making it one of the world's most densely populated islands. Most of the population and infrastructure are located along the coastal zone, which given the low-lying topography of the island makes them highly vulnerable to natural disasters such as sea level rise and storm surges.

**Natural environment**

Barbados has rich marine biodiversity; although terrestrial diversity is limited as an island of the Atlantic's remote eastern Caribbean region, it supports some of the most diverse landmasses, characterized by clear-cutting associated with a colonial past and plantation agriculture. Several plant and animal species are endangered due to existing threats such as habitat loss and invasive species.

The National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan (NBSAP) for Barbados provides a full account of the flora and fauna of the country, including drivers of biodiversity loss which include habitat fragmentation, invasive species, pollution, and over-harvesting of species. The NBSAP identifies several critical ecosystem services that may be impacted or exacerbated by climate change.

### Economic impacts

In addition to biodiversity, the NBSAP identifies several critical ecosystem services which may be impacted or exacerbated by climate change. Of provisioning services, fisheries are most critical to livelihoods, tourism and food security in Barbados. The fishing industry is a major contributor to Barbados' economy with about 26,000 persons (9% of the total population) and indirectly contributes to about 40% of Barbados' earnings, making it an important sector for the country's economic development.

In addition to these external influences, the legacy of colonialism in Barbados has had an impact on the country’s social and economic structures—resulting in persistent disparities in wealth and income. ### Mitigation Needs

Barbados’ greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions are negligible on the global scale, totaling only 1.556 GtCO₂e in 2018—significantly less than 0.01% of global emissions. The majority of Barbados’ GHG emissions come from the energy sector, which accounts for approximately 74% of total emissions and is the largest contributor. The domestic transport sector accounts for 15% of the total emissions, while the industrial processes sector contributes to about 8%. The waste sector makes up less than 2%, with agriculture contributing only a small fraction.

Figure 4 shows that Barbados’ GHG emissions decreased by 26% between 2006 and 2020. This is primarily due to the decrease in domestic transport sector emissions. Further information on the country’s GHG emissions profiles are presented in Annex A:1.2 Barbados Country Climate Change Action Report.

Despite the country's relatively low contribution to global GHG pathway, Barbados first intended Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) was submitted in September 2015 and updated in June 2020 with a more ambitious 45% reduction degree objective of the Paris Agreement. With this NDC update, Barbados is now committed to achieving net-zero emissions from emissions growth, providing targets that are significantly more ambitious than its previous NDCs. This represents an unconditional commitment to a fossil-free electricity sector by 2030 and a significant contribution towards enhancing the resilience of its people and economy—as reflected in the Barbados Vision Energy Policy (BVEP).

To meet the ambitious goals set out in the NDC and the BNEP, significant additional investments are urgently needed for the implementation of the Barbados National Renewable Energy Plan (BNREP) that was finalized in 2022 to guide the implementation of the BNREP. The BNREP is a strategic plan and action plan for the energy transition, which includes the need for utility-scale solar (180MW DC/360 MW AC), along with non-solar (100 MW) renewable energy capacity by 2045.

The plan also identifies a number of pilot projects as a key area for budgetary support. These include grid-connected vehicle-to-grid (V2G) technology embedded electric vehicle chargers, and high-power DC vehicle chargers.

### Barbados' Resilience Strategy: The "Roots to Reefs Program" (R2RP)

To address the country's urgent adaptation needs, the Government of Barbados launched the Roots to Reefs Program (R2RP) in 2013. This initiative aims to protect private and communal coastal roots of Barbados from extreme weather events while encouraging the use of renewable energy sources, rainwater harvesting systems, and water conservation measures.

### Objectives of the R2RP

- **To increase the resilience of the country's infrastructure and utilities**.
- **To reduce critical utility, water, and sanitation, and road infrastructure climate-resilient and sustainable**.
- **To decrease land-based sources of marine pollution through more sustainable land use practices**.

### Key Components of the R2RP

1. **Protecting coastal roots**: This involves creating a buffer zone to protect private and communal coastal roots from extreme weather events while encouraging the use of renewable energy sources, rainwater harvesting systems, and water conservation measures.
2. **Increasing water collection and storage using non-groundwater sources for freshwater supply**.
3. **Increasing financial assistance for vulnerable households to minimize the financial burden associated with climate change adaptation**.
4. **Increasing resilience of marine ecosystems to build breeding grounds**.

### Main Objectives

- **To make coastal homes more resilient to extreme weather events and their impacts, such as possible loss of access to electricity and potable water distribution systems**.
- **To increase energy efficiency and water use efficiency and reduce emissions through the deployment of distributed renewable energy generation**.

## Water Sector Resilience for Sustainability in Barbados (WSSR B - Barbados)

The project aims to: i) build greater resilience to extreme storm events and drought conditions by improving water storage, promoting rainwater harvesting at the household level, and increasing access to clean drinking water; ii) improve the efficiency with which rainwater is used through improved management of urban runoff; iii) advance adaptation and mitigation initiatives in the water sector; iv) support the mobilization of local funds through a revolving disaster risk financing mechanism that reduces greenhouse gas emissions intensity (of water provision); v) develop an integrated approach to climate change education and lessons learnt platform; vi) support the review and development of national policies, strategies, and plans that supports climate-smart development and water sector resilience in Barbados.

The project will also support lessons learned for use in developing further adaptation and mitigation initiatives. It will raise public awareness about climate change and water security issues.

### Climate Change Readiness Programme (CCRP)

As a response to these climate change challenges, the GCF launched the Road to Resilience (R2R). The R2R provides the opportunity for countries to develop an integrated approach to addressing the negative impacts of climate change. The Barbados Government’s sustainable development model for resilience is based on the Climate Change Readiness Programme for Barbados. Problem Statement

The people of Barbados are being severely impacted by climate change, constraining the sustainable development of the country and threatening the lives and livelihoods of vulnerable communities. These climate impacts are driven largely by the increased frequency and intensity of tropical storms and hurricanes — along with the associated flooding, landslides, and coastal erosion. The island is also experiencing more frequent droughts, heatwaves, and increased moisture and increased humidity. Urgent adaptation action is, therefore, needed to reduce the wide-reaching impacts on key sectors such as agriculture, water resources, energy, and tourism.

The country recognizes the vital importance of global mitigation efforts to reduce the threat of future climate change exacerbating these impacts. To this end, despite contributing significantly less than 0.01% of global GHG emissions, Barbados has committed to reducing its greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions intensity by 45% below 2010 levels by 2030. These ambitious targets are intended to demonstrate the country’s commitment to climate action, as well as serve as an example to the rest of the world of the feasibility of the transition when innovative approaches are adopted.

Unfortunately, despite these commitments, progress has been slow — especially in adopting renewable energies and energy efficiency measures. This is partly due to limited access to affordable housing finance, which constrains homeownership of capital in the Barbados economy and the financial ecosystem (see Barriers below for more detail). Given the fiscal constraints, Barbados’ resilience strategy — encompassed in the Roots to Reefs Programme (R2RP) — has focused on leveraging concessional financing from international development partners. This approach is often debt-risked and can or be made bankable. Such an approach will enable the government to direct the limited concessional financial investments towards public-good resilience measures that do not have direct financial returns.

Local Status

If innovative and affordable climate finance is mobilised in Barbados through the establishment of the Blue Green Bank, then the transition to a fossil-free energy supply, as well as hurricane-resilient infrastructure and a secure food supply can be achieved. This will also enable the country to meet its ambitious GHG emissions targets.

Barriers

Householders currently constrain the financing of potentially bankable climate investments in Barbados — particularly when considering the potential involvement of low- to middle-income households. These barriers are detailed below:

Barrier 1: The government of Barbados is constrained in its capacity to take on additional debt to finance climate resilience.

It is estimated that Barbados needs to invest approximately USD $2 billion in climate-responsive initiatives to achieve resilience. Given the size of investment required relative to the country’s GDP (~40% of GDP), Barbados currently faces significant fiscal constraints, which are further exacerbated by being classified as a highly indebted country, constraining the government's ability to take on additional debt. This is particularly problematic for Barbados in light of its ambitious GHG emissions targets set for 2030, with the most significant contributor to increased debt levels recently having been the loss and damage arising from climate-related and other natural disasters alongside the necessary expense to protect the land and marine environment. The country’s ability to finance investments in climate resilience will be further constrained by the fact that any additional debt taken on in relation to climate resilience will worsen the country's debt position.

This structure is reinforced by an international bank de-risking process initially spurred by global anti-money laundering efforts. ### Context

Barbados was one of the highest per capita incomes in the Caribbean with a total GDP of US$4.84 billion and a per capita GDP of US$17,225. In recent years it has been able to transform itself from a low-income country, reliant largely on sugar production, to an upper-middle income country with an economy that relies on a range of primary and tertiary sectors including tourism, financial services (21%), government (18%), tourism (12%), manufacturing (9%), transport (10%), construction (5%) and others (14%).

Despite a relatively high GDP per capita, the Barbadian economy exhibits characteristics commensurate with many other small island economies. The country is highly dependent on external markets for its exports of sugar and rum, as well as a high reliance on imports and exports. Given its dependence on external markets and economies, Barbados is highly susceptible to market fluctuations, limiting the country’s potential for economic growth and international competition.

Challenge 3: Absence of local appetite for climate finance.

#### Vulnerability

Barbados, like SIDS, is highly exposed to the impacts of climate change. Several climate risks constitute significant threats to the country. Tropical storms and hurricanes also have considerable impacts on livelihoods and infrastructure in Barbados. The Caribbean region has a record of extreme weather events showing that major flooding events occur every 12 years on average. These phenomena can be extremely powerful, with devastating effects on the environment and human life.

It is also worth mentioning that rising sea surface temperatures will have adverse effects on coral reefs, causing them to bleach. For Barbados, where foreign exchange earnings are heavily dependent on tourism services and products, a decline in the number of tourists visiting the island due to bleached or dead coral reefs will have a detrimental impact on the natural defences against storm surge during a hurricane.

The country is also facing serious water issues due to a combination of droughts, floods and rainfall variability extremes. The lack of adequate infrastructure for water storage and distribution in the country means that rainfall will increase salinity, thus affecting the country’s water resources and intruding into aquifers. Changes in precipitation patterns can also have an adverse effect on the quality of surface water used for agriculture and water resources. The existing water infrastructure is also at risk of destruction from extreme climate events, which creates a lot of uncertainty around water supply. Water scarcity has historically been a major issue in Barbados, with the country experiencing severe droughts every 10 years or so.

## Economic and social development

Barbados has transformed itself from a low-income economy reliant almost solely on sugar production to having one of the highest per capita incomes in the Caribbean. With a GDP per capita PPP of $13,487 as of 2021, Barbados has managed to diversify its economy to become services-driven and pay more onshore jobs than offshore ones. The tourism sector is the largest contributor to GDP at 15% and has had negative economic effects of the Covid-19 pandemic, with a 14% contraction of GDP in Q2 2020. However, the Barbados Tourism Authority (BTA) reported that visitor arrivals for the first six months of 2021 were up by 85%, and the number of overnight stays grew registered at 113% in the first quarter of 2022. The tourism sector has been an important driver of economic growth.

The country's economy is marked by a huge amount of public debt. Typically, a nation saddled with debt means that it will have less to invest in its own future. However, efforts by the GoB to manage this debt means that Barbados has managed to reduce its debt-to-GDP ratio from 130% in 2018 and 145% in 2019 to 131.9% in 2020. It has a debt-to-GDP target of 60% by the 2035/36 fiscal year (Economic Survey of Latin America and the Caribbean, 2021).

The HDI, which measures the country's average achievements in three basic aspects of human development: health, knowledge and standard of living, is relatively high: 0.790 - ranked 79th globally and 9th in the LAC region (Economic Survey of Latin America and the Caribbean, 2021). The HDI has increased from a base value (0.733), creating a GHD of 1.024 – the highest in the LAC region and among the highest in the world.

Multi-dimensional poverty is low, with 2.5% of the population being multidimensionally poor – the equivalent of 790 people living below multi-dimensional Poverty Index (MPI), 2022. There are a narrow job market and a lack of job opportunities with an unemployment rate of 13%.

### Alternative sources of financing

As mentioned above, Barbados has a considerable amount of public debt, which significantly reduces its flexibility to invest in climate change mitigation and adaptation issues. Furthermore, given that its income status – which is skewed by the small size of the economy and extensive inequality in the distribution of wealth – it is not considered an attractive investment destination for international development finance lenders, such as Multilateral Development Banks (MDBs). It is also worth mentioning that the local capital market has limited capacity for climate investment with very few local private financial institutions. The rest of them are being foreign-owned and have a preference to invest in high-risk projects and are reluctant to invest in or lend money to small customers - especially in untraditional, risky or low-profit finance areas such as microfinance.

As a result, available finance is directed to high-income households and focused on collateralised loans, with limited opportunity for most vulnerable groups. Loans outside of the primary offerings from banks are generally not available at affordable rates. The country has therefore been unable to access climate finance. There is therefore a clear and substantial finance gap that urgently needs to be filled for Barbados to meet its ambitious climate objectives and build a sustainable, climate-resilient future for its people.

Financial viability

Design of the BGB

•	A market assessment was conducted for the Barbadian financial sector, which identified gaps in the current market that would be filled by BGB to ensure that the new bank complements the existing structure rather than competing with and potentially crowding out other banks.

•	An options analysis was conducted to identify the preferable financing mechanism under which the government could establish a new development bank. The following were considered: (i) a green bank; (ii) a public-private partnership (PPP); (iii) a credit guarantee facility; iv) a development fund; v) commercial banks. The assessment concluded that the best option was to establish a green bank, assessed against five criteria categories (mandate, funding, financing, governance and onshore) and while each mechanism aligned with some of the criteria, the green bank was the only option that suited all the criteria. This was further supported by the fact that the government had already established a fund to facilitate the financial disbursement of funds required for the implementation of blue-green projects in Barbados (the Blue-Green Fund). The BGB will be structured as an investment bank, with four pillars, including: i) wholesale/investment; ii) commercials and retail; iii) cooperative; and iv) credit union. Through this assessment, a wholesale bank was deemed best suited to the objectives of the BGB.

•	A gap analysis was conducted against the relevant regulatory frameworks – as set out by the Central Bank of Barbados – and international best practice (see Annex 3: Regulatory Frameworks). The analysis concluded that there is no review of the relevant regulatory frameworks – as set out by the Central Bank of Barbados – and international best practice. ### G.1. Environmental and social risk assessment (max 750 words, approximately 1.5 pages)

The Environmental and Social Management System (ESMS) guides BGB in integrating environmental and social risk assessments into its transaction processes. A key component of the ESMS is BGB's Environmental and Social Assessment (E&SA). The E&SA integrates BGB’s environmental and social considerations into its day-to-day business activities and transaction processes. This includes BGB’s E&S Compliance Policy, which sets out BGB’s commitment to comply with IFC’s E&S policies. BGB’s ES&A Policy is aligned with GCF’s E&S requirements. The scope of the ESMS extends outside of BGB’s core lending activities of BGB, including loans, guarantees and acquiring bonds.

The ESMS identifies four stages at which E&SA assessments are conducted throughout the transaction lifecycle: Transaction Screening, Transaction Structuring and Approval, and E&S Monitoring:

#### 1. Purpose of E&S Screening
The purpose of E&S Screening is to identify E&S red flags and key E&S risks of the transaction at an early stage in the transaction. There are two types of E&S red flags. The first are business activities that would trigger BGB’s Exclusion List and the second are business activities that, based on an initial assessment, may be considered high risk (e.g., Category A or B transactions) and should be referred to IFC for further consideration in accordance with IFC’s E&S Risk Categorization Framework). In both cases, BGB will abandon the transaction without further consideration. If the subproject is not categorized as F1 or Category A and if the risk associated with the subject project is considered low (e.g., Category D or E transactions) or moderate (e.g., Category F or G for non-financial intermediaries and F2 or F3 for financial intermediaries) and proceeds to the E&S Due Diligence stage, BGB will refer the transaction to IFC for further consideration of the E&S risks associated with the subprojects that the intermediary will finance.

#### 2. The E&S Risk Categorization Framework
The E&S Risk Categorization Framework (ESCF) is based on IFC’s E&S Risk Categorization. ESCF for F1 or Category A transactions is conducted by BGB internally. ESCF of F2 or Category B transactions can be conducted by BGB internally or by an external specialist. An external specialist will be engaged if the E&S risks associated with a transaction trigger any of the IFC Performance Standards.

## I. Assessment of the independent Technical Advisory Panel

### 1.1 Overview

1. Barbados is a small island developing state (SIDS) that is vulnerable to climate change impacts. These impacts are driven largely by the increased frequency and intensity of tropical storms and hurricanes—along with the associated impacts of sea level rise and storm surge—as well as droughts, heat waves, and extreme weather events.
2. The national development plans and nationally determined contribution (NDC) of the Government of Barbados aim to achieve 100 per cent renewable energy by 2030 and 50 per cent renewable energy by 2030. Mobilization of finance for these efforts will be key to a successful transition.
3. The assessment identified several local market barriers to mobilizing finance, notwithstanding Barbados' relatively robust economy. Access to finance for lower income communities is particularly constrained in the context of climate change adaptation and mitigation.
4. The proposed BGB will be a joint public-private sector entity to be developed by Pegasus Capital Advisors (Pegasus) as the accredited entity (AE) in close coordination with the Government of Barbados. The BGB will be established under the Investment Funds Act 2015 (IFA), along with three entities still to be established: Blue Green Bank, GGB RE Holdings LP, and Pegasus Capital Advisors Limited.


---
**Page 80**
---

6. The bank will also seek to be a market maker by partnering with existing finance institutions and government programmes to lead on the creation of climate finance opportunities and to broaden and accelerate current programmes. BGB will initially finance rooftop solar photovoltaic (PV) systems, which are expected to be one of the first projects that have already been developed but need financing, including Roots2Roots, HOPE and SHIFT (see funding proposal for a description of these programmes). A key feature of BGB (and all green banks) is that it will not act as a lender or investor itself. Instead, it will seek to partner with a partner. To this end, the bank's operations will be wholesale in nature, providing support in the form of guarantees and other financial instruments to enable access to finance through intermediaries such as existing lenders, whether banks, credit unions, or insurance companies, is facilitating the funding for climate responsive programmes anticipated by government policy climate.

7. GCF is being asked to provide USD 10 million in finance to BGB in a combination of equity (25%) and revolving loans/multi-year lines (75%). The Government of Barbados will provide an additional USD 10 million in equity on a pari passu basis with the GCF equity. This means that the Government of Barbados will be entitled to have its board take an active role in setting up BGB and will maintain a seat on the BGB board of directors.

8. Once established, BGB will engage with investors, including private investment firms, development finance institutions (DFIs), multilateral agencies, and other sources of capital to bank on the long term. Several potential investors have already been identified as being interested in participating in BGB’s activities. The funding proposal sets a target of raising between USD 30 million and USD 60 million total equity. The mid-range scenario would maintain a debt-to-equity ratio at equity ratios, aiming to mobilize at least USD 150 million of investment in climate projects over the next 15 years.

# Introduction

## Background and Context

The people of Barbados are vulnerable to major climate impacts, which have affected the country’s socio-economic and development trajectory thus slowing down economic growth in the past four years (World Bank, 2021). These climate impacts are driven largely by the increased frequency and intensity of tropical storms and hurricanes – along with the associated impacts of sea level rise and storm surge – as well as decreasing rainfall with longer dry spells, lower soil moisture and increasing temperatures. The country is committed to reducing its greenhouse gas emissions in order to protect its critical sectors and secure a sustainable future for all Barbadians. In addition to these core adaptation needs, the country recognises the vital importance of global mitigation efforts to reduce the threat of future climate change exacerbating existing vulnerabilities. To this end, and as part of its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) under the Paris Agreement, Barbados has committed to meeting ambitious mitigation targets, including a transition to 100% renewable energy by 2030.

These ambitious targets are designed to demonstrate the country’s commitment to climate action, as well as to serve as an example to the rest of the world of the feasibility of the transition when innovative approaches are adopted. Unfortunately, despite these commitments, progress has been slow – especially in adopting renewable energies and water conservation initiatives by middle- to low-income households. This relates primarily to the structure of ownership of water resources and the lack of access to finance for investment in climate-resilient infrastructure and flood control measures.

Barbados’ resilience strategy – encompassed in the Rights to Resilience Programme (R2P) – recognises the need to partner with the private sector to mobilise finance for innovative climate investments that are or can be, bankable. Such an approach allows the government to direct the limited concessional finance available towards public good resilience measures that do not reach low-income households.

There is a disconnect in the market in Barbados and the Caribbean for a financial institution that can provide opportunities for existing financial institutions and capital markets to mobilise funds, whether through conventional bank lending or alternative securitisation of revenue streams deriving from climate change mitigation and adaptation activities, thereby creating stronger and more diversified capital markets.

In addition to its climate resilience ambitions, Barbados has also made firm commitments with regards to gender equality and equity. The country is committed to ensuring that all citizens have access to information and to ensure that the country attains gender equality in all spheres of development. Over the years Barbados has made great strides in reducing gender inequality, particularly in education attainment. However, 60% of households are female-headed and women are overrepresented among the poor and vulnerable and low-income groups. As a result, these groups are disproportionately affected in accessing economic opportunities and resources. This is further exacerbated by climate change which reduces the capacity of women and low-income groups to own houses and render them vulnerable to climate disasters. When climate disasters strike poor women find it harder to recover compared to poorer men. An innovative and sustainable finance vehicle that targets the low-income groups specifically women is therefore imperative for the development of Barbados and for the region.

To address the urgent need for innovative, inclusive finance in Barbados, the proposed project will establish a sustainable climate finance vehicle – the Blue Green Bank (BGB) – to stimulate innovation in the financial sector with the goal of mobilizing the securities and loan markets to unlock additional funding for climate-resilience initiatives. The BGB will join a joint public-private sector effort to create an improved financing infrastructure that works closely with other financing players.

1.2 OBJECTIVES OF THE REPORT

The BGB fully adheres to the principle of country ownership, meaning that for each financed activity that receives funding from the BGB, a Gender Assessment (Annex 8.1) and Action Plan (Annex 8.2) will be put in place and align with the requirements set out in the GCF’s updated Gender Policy.

### 4.3 INFRASTRUCTURE AND THE BUILT ENVIRONMENT

Climate resilience is critical for the sustainable development of Barbados, especially in terms of infrastructure, including housing. The country is highly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change such as sea-level rise, increased frequency of natural disasters, and more intense weather events, all of which can cause significant damage to infrastructure, particularly those that are built in low-lying areas or on topographical features susceptible to erosion, thus increasing poverty and inequality.

Housing is an important aspect of climate resilience in Barbados. Adequate and safe housing is a fundamental human right and is essential for the health, well-being, and productivity of individuals and communities. Ensuring that housing is climate resilient is crucial for protecting people from the impacts of climate change, such as the increased risk of flooding and damage from hurricanes and storms.

Climate-resilient housing in Barbados can take several forms, such as elevating homes to higher ground, constructing homes with reinforced concrete and strong roofs, and designing homes with features that allow for natural ventilation and light. The government, in partnership with NGOs and private sector organizations, can also play a role in promoting and financing the construction of climate-resilient housing, through programs and incentives that encourage the adoption of best practices in building design and construction.

In addition to protecting residents from the impacts of climate change, climate-resilient housing can also provide long-term economic benefits. By reducing the risk of damage to homes, residents can save money on repairs and reconstruction after natural disasters, which can help sustain the economic productivity of individuals and communities and promote sustainable economic growth in Barbados.

Between 2020 and 2021, Barbados experienced infrastructure damage. Therefore, in responding to these climate hazards and in line with climate commitments the government of Barbados intends to provide funding for hurricane-resilient housing which is integrated into renewable energy and water efficient systems.

Due to its critical role in climate adaptation and mitigation, infrastructure and built environment are one of the G7’s 8 key result areas. The goals for this results area are advanced across four “transition” areas, namely: (i) build low carbon energy; (ii) protect natural assets; (iii) enhance water security; and (iv) improve land use. Forests and ecosystems, G7 aims to build new infrastructure and retrofit old traditional infrastructure to climate proof it.

This results area aligns with SDG 10 “Industry, innovation and infrastructure” and is also linked to SDG 11 “Sustainable cities and communities”, SDG 6 “Ocean water and sanitation” and SDG 7 “Affordable and clean energy”. Due to its cross-sectoral nature, it appears that the provision of climate-resilient infrastructure has huge potential in addressing climate change and achieving other SDGs. However, the provision of such infrastructure is not without challenges. The impacts are disproportionately negatively impacting women and the poor, far more than men and the affluent, mainstreaming gender in the provision of infrastructure is critical. Moreover, women’s empowerment through ownership of housing infrastructure is also flagged as a priority in the draft national Gender Policy (Government of Barbados – Bureau of Gender Affairs, 2021).

# Recommendations to Reduce Gender Gaps

This gender assessment acknowledges the success and efforts of the Government of Barbados in making headway in all its economic and development sectors. Some areas will continue to need improvement through the implementation of policies, initiatives, and monitoring. These areas include the gender pay gap, domestic and work security and violence against women (see Women 2022). The recommendations given in this report are within the scope or the data project.

It is, therefore, recommended that the BGB projects ensure that climate finance is accessible by all including the vulnerable and women-headed households who are most in need of homes - which in turn will reduce vulnerability to climate change. To avoid biases and ensure that the targeted beneficiaries apply and obtain the loans, the BGB will train their staff in gender mainstreaming, develop an internal gender policy and guidelines to support gender equality, as well as set up development finance lending packages.

The Blue Green Bank and its staff must have an appreciation of gender mainstreaming and what it entails. For this to happen the BGB needs to:

- Create a position for a gender specialist (if not in place already - recruit one) to assist in implementing the GAP and ensuring that the BGB and intermediaries have the necessary processes in place.
- Carry out sector-based gender assessments to identify the key barriers to securing finance and use these findings to guide how inclusive financing packages are designed and marketed.
- Develop an evidence-based gender policy for staff and intermediary partners.
- Develop and adopt the organization's code of conduct: a Sexual Exploitation Abuse and Harassment Policy for BGB and its partner programmes.
- Ensures that all its staff attend gender training/workshops so that there is an understanding and appreciation of the importance of reducing the gender gap in climate finance.
- Develop and adopt a gender screening tool that all partners supported by the BGB have to satisfy to obtain finances;
- Agree with the partners to put in place gender-responsive loan/investment products that target both men and women equally - and develop a funding guideline that is gender responsive.
- Carry out an awareness building program to give confidence to potential customers who may need to apply for climate finance.
- Request partners and borrowers to monitor the uptake of financing and collect sex-disaggregated data to measure if progress is being made to reduce the gender gap.
- Put in place customer surveys and complaints mechanisms that allow for learning and improvement, and package and distribute these lessons from the programme to be used as a basis for adjusting and improvements to services.
- Carry out a mid-term evaluation of the programme, using the Gender Action Plan.
