# A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

The proposed project is a financing initiative for West Africa, including the objectives and primary measurable benefits (see investment criteria in section B). The detailed description can be elaborated in section C.

Half of the population in the West Africa Region do not have access to electricity. Countries from this region face interrelated challenges of energy access, energy security and high cost of electricity. This is attributed to the high dependence on fossil fuels for power generation, which are expensive and subject to volatile prices. The lack of sufficient generating electricity demand, the inability to attract private capital in power generation at scale, and the inefficiencies along the value chain all impede progress.

The region's energy mix consists mainly of hydro and fuel oil (HFO). Hydropower and solar represent respectively 18% and 5%. Given the continuous decline in the cost of solar electricity generation*, the region would benefit from investing massively on solar technologies to reduce its dependence on expensive fossil fuels for power generation. The proposed programme will provide a blended finance approach to scale up solar energy investments with large financing needs over the sector's entire value chain, the solar sector increasingly relies on the private sector to deploy grid-connected and off-grid solar systems in rural areas of West Africa.

The total installed solar PV capacity in the countries targeted by the proposed programme was only around 70 MW at the end of 2017, which is far below the national plans and NDC targets of 1.192 MW solar energy capacity by 2030.


## The rationale for the proposed GCF programme

The BOAD Climate Finance Facility to Scale Up Solar Energy Investments in West Africa (BOAD) will provide a blended finance approach to scale up solar energy investments in West Africa Region and to access private sector financing for solar technologies through private capital leveraging. The proposed programme provides an opportunity to increase the scale of grid-connected and off-grid solar systems, and to create a market for investments in solar technologies and CSP in the region.

The BOAD will focus on the Least Developed Countries (LDCs) of this region, which are characterized by high poverty levels, low access to electricity, and limited financial inclusion. The programme will provide a blended finance approach to scale up solar energy investments with large financing needs over the sector's entire value chain.

## B.1. Description of Financial Elements of the Project / Programme

The proposed programme envelope aims to provide EUR 117 million of senior debt and standby loans (tenor extension instrument) to selected solar projects in Least Developed Countries (LDCs) from the Francophone West Africa region and EU countries.

The GCF contributions will account for 50% of the total funding of EUR 123m required to deliver the Programme. The GCF financing will include a loan to BOAD on co-lending to selected projects in solar PV and CSP technologies. BOAD will provide the remaining 50%.

The programme is designed to maximise total investment, using limited public funds to leverage greater private investment. The total commitment is expected to leverage a substantial amount of financing from project developers and private investors.

Overall lending in West Africa remains difficult for most banks due to particularly challenging conditions and internal vulnerabilities. Credit conditions are reflected in high interest rates from the commercial banks. The region is characterized by a lack of access to finance, which is a significant barrier to scaling up solar energy projects with characteristics of a solar investment. The local commercial banks are unable to extend loans with tenors of no more than 5-7 years due to the high cost of borrowing and the limited capacity for long-term lending in the context of external financing. The limited borrowing capacity and the lack of available and accessible commercial finance by local financial institutions are significant barriers to scaling up solar energy projects, particularly in West Africa. These financial barriers are a significant barrier to the mobilization and scaling up of climate finance.

Given that the current market conditions in the target region are not favorable and that commercial banks are risk adverse, it is necessary to address these financial barriers by providing affordable and long-term financing to scale up solar energy in the LDC countries. The following measures can be taken:

* To overcome the barrier of high capital cost, external finance support is necessary, combined with private financing and public funding. The access to concessional finance from the GCF and BOAD will crowd-in private sector investment. However, given that the interest rate on loans provided by the GCF and BOAD at an attractive interest rate, the blended cost of borrowing for BOAD will be reduced compared to the cost of borrowing of BOAD on capital market. Therefore, BOAD will be able to lend to projects at long tenors and more competitive rates than those currently available in the market.

* To overcome the barrier of the length of tenor which is a key limitation encountered by project developers, lending from BOAD can be provided through a Standby Loan Facility (SLF) under the GCF programme by providing up to EUR 12 million of Standby Loans to BOAD which will be on-lent to sub-projects for extension of the commercial loan. The repayment of the outstanding commercial loan will be made by BOAD at maturity of the commercial bank branch during their initial tenor. Commercial banks will provide senior debt, backed by the standby facility provided by BOAD. This will allow the project developer to extend the tenor and automatically provide a credit enhancement to the underlying sub-project by extending the tenor of the outstanding commercial bank loan at maturity to match the tenor of BOAD's loan to the sub-project.

# C. Strategic Context

The Paris Agreement, the Marrakech agreements (COP 22) and the SE4ALL initiative have all emphasized the urgency of scaling up renewable energy (RE) projects and programmes in the region by scaling-up Renewable Energy (RE) projects, particularly through Public-Private-Partnerships and private sector investment. The need for RE is also reflected in the global push for public-led green growth, as public and national financial institutions cannot bear the investment costs alone.

In West Africa, there is an urgent need to increase electricity supply and access for the population as almost half of it are without access to electricity. This can be achieved by increasing the share of RE in the energy mix through the promotion of clean energy as well as regional cooperation in order to meet their Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs). In particular, West Africa has a huge potential for solar energy and grid-connected renewable energies such as grid-connected solar technology which constitutes the best alternative to fossil fuels considering the region's potential.

The ECOWAS region is made up fifteen member countries that are located in the Western African region: Benin, Burkina Faso, Cape Verde, Côte d'Ivoire, Gambia, Ghana, Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Liberia, Mali, Mauritania, Niger, Nigeria, Senegal, Sierra Leone and Togo. These countries have both cultural and geopolitical ties and share common economic interest. The proposed project area focuses on six LDCs of West Africa region: namely, Benin, Burkina Faso, Guinea-Bissau, Mali, Niger and Togo.

The ECOWAS region has developed its RE strategy, which complements the ECOWAS White Paper (2006) to promote access to energy services by 2015. The strategy was adopted in 2013 as a framework for the development of an enabling environment for a competitive and sustainable energy sector.

The EREP (ECOWAS Renewable Energy Policy) strategy (2013) came from the realization that the demographic transition and climate change were significant threats to African economies and aims at promoting the use of renewable energies in order to meet the ECOWAS region's energy needs. The general objective is to achieve nearly 30% of installed RE capacity in the energy mix - totally 7,608MW - by 2030.

Based on the EREP baseline, the ECOWAS Centre for Renewable Energy and Energy Efficiency (ECREEE) has provided national governments with a set of policy recommendations to help them meet their national needs and challenges. In addition, national governments were strongly encouraged to take steps towards the alleviation of barriers for private investors through the simplification of the regulatory framework and additional fiscal incentives.

The goal of the proposed programme is to create a market for investments in solar technologies in the six LDCs of West Africa (Benin, Burkina Faso, Côte d'Ivoire, Mali, Niger and Togo). The programme will leverage public sector actors based in these countries to scale up private investment in solar technologies. The programme is fully aligned with regional and national efforts to drive low-carbon development in West Africa.

While the region has a huge solar potential capacity, market development has been slow because of the unfavourable business environment for investors. The combination of increasing RE investment, the barriers of the banks to doing business effectively and in a timely manner has meant that progress on scaling up private sector investments is likely to remain limited in these countries and private sector actors will not be willing to take the risk involved.

By removing the multiple barriers that are preventing the development of the solar market in West Africa, the programme will facilitate project funding in the target countries and will develop the capacity of local investors to deploy solar technologies. Many clean energy projects in West Africa are technically and commercially viable but have yet to become bankable investment opportunities. Traditionally, the public-private financing models being used do not necessarily guarantee timely or efficient investments. The procurement process is often slow and cumbersome, and the availability of project finance for renewable energy investors is not consistent and is largely influenced by global dynamics and perceptions beyond the control of project developers.

The potential private sector finance for RE projects in West Africa is significant. However, the challenge is to provide affordable and attractive financing frameworks that sustainably exploit commercial lending and investment opportunities in the private sector. RE technologies often have high upfront costs and low rates of return. The consequent high-risk nature of these projects makes them less accessible to financial institutions making RE finance a challenging task for investors.

### D1. Value Added for GCF Involvement

The GCF's involvement in the solar sector is critical for the following reasons:

- **Privatizing investment in the solar sector at scale through the appropriate risk-return profile**: As explained in previous sections, the solar potential is significant in West Africa but the installed capacity remains low because of limited access to finance. The GCF can help unlock private sector investments and provide long-term commitments to address climate change by investing massively in RE. The policy and regulatory frameworks are in principle supportive of a good business case for solar projects, but the lack of access to financing from commercial banks to doing business effectively and in a timely manner has meant that progress has been slower than expected.

- **In West Africa, financing for solar projects has traditionally been dominated by the Development Finance Institutions (DFIs) with limited private sector participation**: The DFIs have historically provided concessional funding at low interest rates. However, the associated cost reductions have created opportunities for the private sector. However, these technology solutions are still new, currently small in scale and operating in an unstable regulatory environment. Furthermore, solar projects usually require a long-term investment horizon that often exceeds the typical 5-year term of most commercial banks but require long-term credit facility. Commercial banks are not able to provide long-term credit for solar projects partly because they depend largely on short-term liquidity needs and are constrained by their capital adequacy ratios. The lack of access to financing from commercial banks constrains it presents to the project developers of solar projects will persist without availability of long-term credit facility.

# F.2 - Technical Evaluation

Overall, through this programme, GCF and BOAD financing will demonstrate and scale up commercially viable solar projects in the region while catalysing a paradigm shift within the electricity sector in the LOCs of West Africa.

## Solar PV

The solar PV sector is so dynamic that today's cost of generation may become tomorrow's benchmark. Prices have dwindled by 80% since 2010, and are expected to continue falling as technology matures and economies of scale build up. IRENA estimates that by 2030, total installed costs could fall between 50% and 60%. Battery lifetime and performance are also improving rapidly, with the cost per kWh dropping from USD 480 in 2017 to USD 90 in 2018. These differences can be attributed to different market maturity levels, differences in local labour, manufacturing costs and different support policy structures. Between 2010 and 2017, the global capacity weighted average total installed cost of new commissioned solar PV plants fell by an average annual rate of 35%. The cost of solar PV has fallen faster than any other technology in newer or underdeveloped markets are being developed at costs that are increasing at a par, and sometimes even cheaper than the overall market.

The global weightage average capacity factor of utility-scale PV systems increased by 26% between 2010 and 2017, from an average of 13.7% to 17.6%. This has been driven by three major factors: the trend towards greater deployment in sunny regions; the increasing use of trackers; and increased use of tracking and improvements in the performance of systems as losses have been reduced.

## Concentrating Solar Thermal Power (CSP)

Total installed costs for CSP plants that include thermal energy storage tend to be higher than those without, but storage capacity is a key differentiator. For example, for parabolic trough systems (the technology with the highest share of installed projects so far), total installed plant costs can range between USD 2 550 and USD 11 260/kWth. The cost per kWh ranges from four to six hours of storage; however, see this range increase to between USD 8 650 and USD 13 150/kWh.

Capacity factors have increased over time as a shift towards newer technologies, with larger thermal storage capacities, has occurred. In the case of parabolic trough systems, for example, capacity factors in 2017 were higher levels of irradiance were likely the main factor behind lower levelized costs. During 2016 the capacity weighted average LCOE of CSP plants was estimated to be USD 4.27/kWh (or fifth lower than in 2009) although IRENA data suggests that this figure could increase by up to four-fold during 2017/2018.

# Climate impact potential of the programme

## 1.2.2 Climate impact potential of the programme

### (a) Primary impact potential will be the reduction of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions through the displacement of fossil fuel electricity generation by the solar power facilities implemented under the programme.

#### (i) It has been estimated that the programme will deliver about 215 MW of new solar power generating capacity, which will deliver approximately 379 gigawatt hours (GWh) of clean electricity to the five targeted countries. This is equivalent to a reduction in carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2eq) per year (equivalent to 4.8 Mtonnes over 25 years), that would be emitted by fossil fuel power plants.

#### (ii) The GHG emissions reduction (ER) methodology utilized in this calculation is summarized as follows:

##### (a) The five electricity systems in five of the six targeted countries, namely Benin, Burkina Faso, Mali, the Niger and Togo, are connected to the West African Power Pool, while Guinea-Bissau's system is not. Therefore, each kilowatt hour (kWh) of electricity generated by a solar power project will displace 1 kWh of electricity from fossil fuel plants that would be deemed to displace a kWh of electricity on the baseline West African Power Pool network.

##### (b) The GHG emissions reduction as a result of the delivery of electricity from the five targeted countries has been calculated using the grid emission factor from the Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) project "Solar Energy for Africa" (SUN0314). Grid emission factors are used to calculate the carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2eq) emissions for specific power plants and fuel types (i.e., the CO2 emission factor) for the West African Power Pool, the interconnection between Benin and Togo, and the interconnection between Côte d'Ivoire, Ghana, Mali, the Niger, Nigeria, Senegal, and Togo. The grid emission factor used is 0.561 tCO2eq/MWh.

##### (c) For the solar project in Guinea-Bissau, the country's emission factor, estimated as 0.765 tCO2eq/MWh was used to calculate the GHG ER implication of the solar power projects implemented under the programme.

#### (iii) The total GHG emissions reduction of the entire programme was estimated as the sum of the ER from the solar projects in the five countries connected to the West African Power Pool and the ER from the solar project in Guinea-Bissau. Total ER in the six selected francophone countries was estimated to be 19,320 tCO2eq.

### (b) In addition to the impact of GHG emissions reduction, it is expected that the programme will deliver clean electricity to an estimated 2.9 million people. This number is calculated using the current population in each country and the total installed capacity of 339 GWh of clean electricity expected to be delivered by the solar power projects created by the programme.

### (c) Given these impacts, the independent Technical Advisory Panel (TAP) assessed the scale of the impact potential as "high".

1.2 Paradigm shift potential

### General overview

The projects covered by this programme will involve the development of grid-scale solar power projects. In terms of the technologies that will be implemented, these can be considered as mature and now widely deployed in many parts of the world. However, although similar technology has been deployed in the target region in the past, its development has not reached a level where it is currently being used to meet the primary resources in the region. Barriers to implementation of solar power technologies and delivery of projects are numerous and complex. These barriers can be grouped into three broad proposal and include technical, financial, regulatory and policy issues. A key goal of the proposed programme is to remove or reduce these barriers to the deployment of solar energy. A key component of the proposed intervention is the utilization of concessional financing from GCF to increase participation by local governments (national and sub-national), private sector investors (domestic and foreign) and local financial institutions in the implementation of grid-connected solar power facilities in the target countries. # 1. Introduction

Within the context of the six target countries, issues relating to gender equality remain an on-going challenge, even more so when it comes to energy access, poverty and affordability. While regulatory and policy frameworks are in place, their implementation is often inadequate or absent due to weak institutional capacity. In many cases, women have limited access to decision-making positions within the sector. Women and girls face significant barriers to accessing education and training opportunities, which limits their economic empowerment and reduces their ability to participate fully in society.

In rural areas, where most of the population lives in developing countries, women are disadvantaged compared to their male counterparts at both the technical and decision-making levels. This is particularly true for women living in isolated households or those who live with other family members but do not have access to electricity. Further, within the energy sector, a significant gender gap exists between men and women, with women occupying fewer positions of responsibility.

The six target countries selected by the proposed programme show low to very low rankings in terms of both the Gender Inequality Index (GII) and the Human Development Index (HDI), as seen in Table 2 below. 3. Objectives of the Programme

### 3.1 Gender and the energy sector

As access to affordable and clean energy is a prerequisite for economic empowerment and poverty reduction, gender inequalities limit economic opportunities for women and subsequently have significant negative impacts on their well-being. Women’s access to and control over resources often differ based on their roles and responsibilities in society. This results in gender-differentiated impacts of energy access and use. Women are more likely than men to be excluded from the decision-making process regarding the use of energy and benefits that derive from energy services. As explained above, women and girls tend to be disadvantaged compared with men when it comes to economic opportunities, financial inclusion, social norms, bias, discriminatory or blind policy frameworks, as well as political conflict and fragility. As a result, women are more likely than men to experience poverty, lack of access to education, limited educational and professional opportunities and have their own maternal and reproductive health rights curtailed.

The energy sector is one of the most important sectors for economic development in many countries. The programme aims to address the lack of inclusion of women in the energy value chain as only 2% of women are employed in the energy sector globally, according to the World Bank (2014). Ultimately seeks to develop a pipeline of investment-ready, women-owned energy businesses across the region.

Regarding women’s employment in the energy sector, in the largest countries and overall in West Africa, women suffer from gender-based discrimination. Women are underrepresented at all levels of the energy sector with respect to roles in the sales and service sector in roles covering retail, administration and HR, with lower salaries and fewer benefits than men. Women also face barriers when it comes to pursuing studies and entering the engineering and construction sectors. The fact that women are disadvantaged compared to men with regards to economic opportunities, financial inclusion, social norms, bias, discriminatory or blind policy frameworks, as well as political conflict and fragility.

Overall, males and females are not encouraged in equal measures to pursue studies, and eventually enter the energy sector. Specific constraints with regards to women’s employment in the energy sector include but are not limited to:

- Insufficiently resources initiatives to promote female education in STEM fields
- Little or no access to financial services for female entrepreneurs compared to male entrepreneurs
- Fewer resources dedicated to gender-sensitive adaptation of vocational, management and entrepreneurial training programmes
- Gender equal capital flows to female entrepreneurs in the energy sector are too small and too concentrated on microfinance
- Few incentives for the private sector to create a gender-balanced workforce (ECOWAS, 2015, p. 20)

At the consumer level, the challenges for gender equality are twofold: one is awareness about product availability and affordability; the other is access to energy services. The lack of awareness and information poses difficulties for the adoption of improved and cleaner energy in some settings. Gender equality at the community level is also a challenge as women often have limited decision-making power within their own agency. Specific constraints include:

- Many supply side agents still lack market intelligence about demand composition and gender-aware sales strategies
- Remaining inefficiencies in the application of end-use financing and targeted subsidies, along with continued high transaction costs for women
- Incomplete progress on achieving gender equality in household budgets, including energy power and agency

### 3.2 Gender and energy access

Improved access to modern, sustainable energy services can significantly reduce the time and labour burdens women are traditionally subject to, as well as improve their health conditions and opportunities for entrepreneurship. The ability of women to control and manage energy resources is a key factor in improving their economic empowerment and reducing poverty. Access to energy provides an opportunity for extended and/or flexible working hours, and therefore increases the time available

Children also benefit from these technologies by being able to participate more fully in other income-generating activities, while boys and girls are free of household chores and can attend school without delay.

## Challenges

There are, however, potential negative impacts that could occur during the development and construction of the project. These include but not limited to gender-based power dynamics and discrimination. This could be caused by the fact that access to benefits generated by the project is: compensation, employment, access to land and water etc., is subject to a male head of household. Women are often excluded from decision-making processes in rural areas; an important land use area, this could lead to conflict of interest over ownership and land use, governed by norms which typically favor men.

Indeed, existing gender disparities in energy poverty can be aggravated at the country, regional and local level, if energy access is expanded in a gender-blind manner. The benefits of on-grid expansion could apply disproportionately to men as they are more likely to have access to electricity than women.

The project must therefore pursue gender gains in its investment activities and look at opportunities for female entrepreneurship, improvements in the representation of women in the labour force, and energy access for the poorest households, particularly those headed by women-headed households – in the target countries.

Other negative impacts could include:

- Lack of engagement of women that would result in no genuine engagement in the project development process.
- Employment opportunities benefiting a selected few, excluding women to a large extent, thus increasing women’s dependence on men as household income providers.
- Increased vulnerability and exposure of women to gender-based violence and sexual harassment and contribution to local development plans.
- Increase in domestic violence and sexual assault against women around construction sites
