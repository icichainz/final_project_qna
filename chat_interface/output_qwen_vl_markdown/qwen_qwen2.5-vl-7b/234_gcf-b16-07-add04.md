### PROJECT EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (max 200 words)

The Project provides the remarkable opportunity to support a government of a least developed country that proves a strong commitment to introducing a paradigm shift in its climate adaptation policies. The Tanzanian Government intends to implement this project in the Simiyu Region, which is one of the most vulnerable regions in Tanzania.

The holistic approach contributes to the two main drivers for the population's resilience: Sustainable provision of water and food security. The Project will serve as a live model with lessons learnt from the Project being adapted at the national level at a later stage.

The Simiyu Region is a high profile example for the urgent need to face multi-sectorial challenges of climate change in Tanzania. Impacts include profound changes in water availability, temperature stresses to people, livestock and crops, changing rainfall patterns leading to more frequent and severe flooding as well as droughts, both longer and deeper intermission of dry seasons, but also of unpredictable intense rains and floods, posing a significant threat to livelihoods and food security.

The objective of the Project is to increase the climate resilience of rural and urban households, particularly small scale farmers and women, living in the Simiyu Region and to improve policies and regulation for cross-sectoral action towards climate change adaptation. The project will be implemented by the Ministry of Water Resources and Irrigation of Tanzania. More than 495,000 direct beneficiaries (out of them 257,000 women) and 2.5 m indirect beneficiaries are expected to increase their climate adaptive capacities through the Project.

Fields of action include:
- Improving the government's institutional and regulatory framework for cross sectoral and community based adaptation
- Improving climate resilience of water supply infrastructure, sanitation services and agricultural practices in the Simiyu Region
- Pursuing a community driven approach to ensure targeting of the most vulnerable
- Enhancing adaptive capacities of vulnerable urban and rural population, as well as implementing capacities of local and central government structures

## Description of Financial Elements of the Project

The estimated total costs of the Project amount to €143.4 m EUR. The Government of Tanzania earmarked 13.1 m EUR as its own contribution, which is a remarkably high amount for a LDC and which shows the exceptional government commitment to this project. In addition, the government has made available 20% of its annual contributions from local communities. During Project preparation, the government already made available EUR 680,000 for studies like the baseline study, vulnerability assessment, and climate change impact analysis. The remaining 26.1 m EUR grant funding from KfW provided by the German Government. The co-funding commitment by the German Government through KfW has been earmarked in the Government Negotiations between Tanzania and Germany.

The Government of Tanzania (GoT) seeks 102.7 m EUR of grant funding for this project. Maximum concessionality is proposed to be achieved with a loan from the GCF. The project addresses stressed stress of particularly poor and vulnerable population groups like subsistence farmers and women in a Least Developed and post-Highly Indigent Poor District and post-FAO food security, in a region strongly affected by climate change. The GCF funds are used to overcome barriers that currently prevent the implementation of adaptation measures and to support the development of an enabling environment for climate adaptation.

None of the Project's components generates sufficient revenue to pay back investment costs and/or amortization payments on loans. However, the project is expected to generate significant co-benefits, including allowing Tanzania to undertake the proposed adaptation measures without reducing funding for other priority development needs or increasing its risks of debt distress. Tanzania is taking part in the Policy Support Instrument (PSI) of the GCF, which aims at strengthening revenue mobilization, public financial management and debt management. Despite the fact that implementing this PSI will require additional resources, it is expected to have a positive impact on the country's economy.

C1. (Strategic Context)

Over the past 30 to 50 years, Tanzania has experienced more frequent precipitation extremes such as droughts and heavy rainfall. The following changes have been observed between 1960 and 2006 (all statistically significant):

- the average annual temperature has increased by 1°C, with the largest increase during January and February;
- rainfall has become more variable, showing a significant decrease in mean annual precipitation and in seasonal rainfall over dry seasons from October to March (March–May);
- the number of "5-day maximum rainfall" events in March to May has increased.

Forecasts foresee a stronger variability, but also an overall slight increase of the cumulative amount of rainfall in the Lake Victoria region. Temperature and extreme weather events such as droughts, heavy rains and flooding are expected to become more frequent and intense (see Figure 1). The results from the climate change impact studies for the present Project, impacts are expected to include profound changes in water availability, temperature, precipitation patterns, and frequency of extreme weather events. These will have significant effects on land use practices, incomes and food security; ecological disruption; and many other related impacts. Tanzania's economic performance is highly dependent upon rainfall (see Figure 2). The country has a high dependence on the agricultural sector for employment and income generation, with a strong correlation between agricultural productivity and the consumer price index and a high dependency on hydropower (refer to section E.4.2). The Government of Tanzania (GoT) estimates the annual economic costs of addressing climate change impacts to be about 1% of the country's GDP (see Figure 3). The GoT has also estimated that the total cost of adaptation measures for the agricultural sector in Tanzania (NDCs).

The negative impacts of climate change are perceived as a major obstacle for the country’s efforts to achieve economic growth. Its per capita poverty rate targets to reach middle income developing country status in the future as defined in the country's Five Year Development Plan (VDP II) and the Poverty Reduction Strategy (MUKUGA II). Tanzania has therefore prioritized climate change adaptation, and is one of 41 countries that have committed to the NDCs. It is also one of the countries that committed to Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) at the COP in Paris in 2015. Furthermore, as outlined in section E.3.1, Tanzania has developed a National Adaptation Plan (NAP), which includes the measures outlined in Section 5.1. Tanzania currently gains support from the German Government though GIZ on the National Adaptation Planning (NAP) process.

The Simiyu Region (see Annex A2) in the north-west of Tanzania is one of the country's most prominent examples of how negative effects of climate change pose a burden for development. According to the results of the Population and Housing Census from 2012, the population of Simiyu Region was 1,584,000 of which 824,000 were rural dwellers (see Figure 4). The region has an area of approximately 37,690 km². In 2012, there were around 1.7 million people. Whereas the growth rate is rather low compared to the national average of 2.5%, the population in Simiyu Region is growing at a rate of about 4% per year due to high migration rates and a low level of living conditions, especially in rural areas. The Simiyu Region's Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is estimated to be around US$100 million annually.

Agriculture dominates the livelihood and economic performance of the region. The sector contributes about 75 percent to the regional economy and employs about 80 percent of the active population. Water supply is a major challenge in Simiyu Region, with only 23% of households having access to improved water sources (see Figure 5). Industrial development comprises some scattered medium and small-scale industrial establishments like mills and processing plants for agricultural products such as tea, coffee, tobacco, and sugar. The construction of the Tunduma Nickel mining project in the village of Duhua has been identified but is currently on hold. It is currently unknown if and when this project will be resumed.

The region's poor road infrastructure continues to be a major constraint for economic development. Lack of road infrastructure to bridge these distances have hampered economic development and alleviation of poverty in the region.

#### Challenge 1: Vulnerable people, communities and regions suffer from most limited experiences in cross-sectoral planning and implementation of climate adaptation projects

While strategies and objectives for climate adaptation already exist in Tanzania, their implementation still lags behind. There are several reasons why this is the case including lack of capacity to increase climate resilience, but the country faces considerable institutional, financial and technical barriers. The reasons for this are:

- (a) the cross-sectoral nature of adaptation needs in Tanzania;
- (b) the limited experience with integrated and multi-sectoral environment adaptation needs of the vulnerable.
- (c) the lack of capacity to implement climate change adaptation projects.

Adaptation needs in Tanzania often call for interventions that touch several sectors. ## Improving Climate resilience of water supply infrastructure, agricultural practices and sanitation services

A set of measures will be implemented in the Simiyu Region to improve climate resilience of those who are most exposed to climate needs. As described below, a community-based participatory approach is pursued, where communities can decide on measures on the basis of climate adaptation benefits. While some beneficiaries may take part in more than one action, the following actions will be implemented as part of the Project:

### 2.1 Water Supply

The Project reliably supplies urban and rural households about 500,000 people of the regions of Burunge, Baradi and Simiyu with clean and safe drinking water. Water supply from Lake Victoria is the most sustainable and climate adapted solution as described in section C.1. A water intake and treatment plant is installed at Nyanza village on the shores of Lake Victoria to provide a reliable source of water for the Project. The water will be piped via an 80 km pipeline to a command reservoir.

### D2. Exit Strategy

The Project has a strong focus on ensuring sustainable operation, scaling up and replication. The main features of this "exit strategy" are: (1) knowledge building on the basis of lessons learnt from the implementation process of the Project; (2) establishment of institutional structures and tariff setting to ensure sustainability in operations and maintenance, and (3) development of replicable models for operation and operations processes. All three aspects are strengthened through comprehensive capacity development measures that are implemented throughout the project.

#### Knowledge building on the basis of lessons learnt

The Project establishes a collaborative and participatory process of institutional learning to enhance efficiency and effectiveness of water supply systems in Tanzania, including climate adaptation interventions. It enhances acquiring specific knowledge and adapts state of the art coordination and implementing mechanisms to the local context. It further develops products such as guidelines for urban water utilities, technical manuals for small-scale water projects, or the ICT platform on climate change. These products complement and are integrated into existing policies and sectoral strategies like the Water Sector Development Programme and the Agriculture Sector Development Programme. The knowledge gained will be documented in a project report that will be shared with regulatory systems and will be sustainably pursued long beyond the implementation horizon of the proposed Project.

#### Institutional structures and tariff setting

The water utilities in Tanzania are expected to fully cover their operational costs from the water supply system's revenues from year three of operation onwards. However, revenues only flow in once household connections and public services have been established. The project has developed a number of strategies showing how they intend to achieve the number of customers necessary to cover operational costs in the shortest possible time. The Project pre-finances household connections in urban centers to help urban utilities overcome this initial cost barrier. Urban utilities are expected to recover their investment from the sale of water and to generate income for the utilities and allows them to invest in smaller network extensions in order to further increase the number of customers. In rural areas, the project provides a pre-financing mechanism (e.g., through local authorities) that facilitates administration, accounting and legal services through service agreements. While the urban utilities have to bear a part of the costs for the initial connection, they are expected to recover their investment from CONOSOs. This is a strong incentive for urban utilities to increase customers, and for SIMMASOA to engage in supporting the formulation of CONOSOS in cooperation with the LGAU. Despite these efforts to connect customers as quickly as possible, it will take some time until revenues start flowing into the water supply systems due to the long commissioning, since water connections need some time to be established. To ensure that the urban utilities and SIMMASOA are able to cover their operational costs in a timely manner, the Government of Tanzania provides them with the necessary financial support within this phase. This will be closely monitored by the Project. The project also supports the capacity development measures for the Government of Tanzania to commit staff to the urban utilities and SIMMASOA sufficiently in line with Tanzanian standards and according to defined staffing concepts. This staff benefits from capacity development measures of the Project.

#### Operational sustainability

The Project's long-term sustainability strategy in rural areas builds on experiences from the Hai Water supply project in Kilimanjaro Region that has demonstrated significant high levels of operational efficiency, low commissioning of connections, and high levels of compliance. If projects are planned to be in line with local structures right from the beginning, Community-based climate adaptation planning, afforestation plans, life lines of official and casual communication, a long-term maintenance plan, and other measures can be integrated into the project design at an early stage. These are critical (please also refer to section D 2). Communities are supported in their planning processes through local structures, i.e., the Region's Water Supply and Sanitation Committee (WSSC) and the Village Water Supply and Sanitation Committee provides up to the level required to achieve adequate adaptive capacities. Local Civil Society Organisations are involved to enhance sustainability of the efforts.

The Project includes an optimized mix of using water from the shared resources of Lake Victoria for drinking purposes only, while promoting agricultural development on the basis of locally available water resources and protecting the environment. The second pipe will be laid out to cost least solutions for the rest of the population which would use water from Lake Victoria for cattle troughs and for large scale irrigation. ### BARUWASA (Bariadi Urban Water and Sanitation Authority) and Urban Water and Sanitation Authorities (UWSAs) of Lagangabili and Nyishongo towns

The Bariadi Urban Water and Sanitation Authority (BARUWASA) was officially established in 2005. It is responsible for providing water supply services to the urban areas of Bariadi town. In Tanzania, Urban Water and Sanitation Authorities (UWSAs) are legal entities that are responsible for providing water supply services in urban areas. The UWSA is divided into three categories: category "A" comprises of UWSAs with water service coverage of more than 75% of their area of service and which meet all operational and maintenance costs; category "B" comprises of UWSAs with water service coverage between 60-74%, but which meet at least 85% of the operational and maintenance costs except for plant electricity costs and salaries as shall be determined in the Memorandum of Understanding (MoU); and category "C" comprises of UWSAs with water service coverage less than 65%.

BARUWASA is a category "C" UWSA and is considered to suffer from a number of institutional weaknesses, including missing infrastructure like very limited available water and no functioning treatment plant. The lack of trained staff, poor customer relations, and the absence of a Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) with the Ministry of Water and Irrigation have contributed to high levels of unaccounted for water, low skill levels of staff and poor customer relations. BARUWASA has been recently staffed with a new and qualified Managing Director, but the utility sees itself continually confronted by challenges in terms of providing adequate services due to lack of resources, implementation of sustainable water and sanitation services. Additionally, the inadequate working environment makes it difficult for the utility to attract and retain skilled personnel. The lack of an enabling environment at the institutional (the legal and regulatory frameworks that govern the activities and the mandate of an organization) level is also a major challenge. This is due to the fact that there are no clear structures and processes in place to suit the suitability of work space and access to work equipment) and individual needs (skills, knowledge, competencies and abilities of staff personnel).
