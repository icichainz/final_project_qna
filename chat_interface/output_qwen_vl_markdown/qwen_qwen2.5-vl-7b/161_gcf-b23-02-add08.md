## A.2. Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

Please provide a brief description of the proposed programme/project, including the objectives and primary measurable benefits (see assessment criteria in section D). The detailed description can be elaborated in section C.

Economic growth in Ghana is highly dependent on the agricultural sector - contributing about 30% of GDP and 65% of employment. Agriculture is dominated by smallholder farming with over 90% of farmers being smallholders, primarily family farmers with approximately 90% of farm holdings of less than two hectares.

Ghana's agriculture sector is the third largest emitter of carbon, accounting for 13% of the total GHG emissions in Ghana. It is also highly vulnerable to climate change impacts. The period from the year 1961 to 2000 clearly shows a progressive rise in temperature and decrease in mean annual rainfall in all six agro-ecological zones of the country. Climate change will likely lead to more frequent extreme weather events, including droughts, floods, heatwaves, and storms (i) rising temperatures, (ii) declaring total rainfall and increased variability, (iii) rising sea levels and (iv) high incidence of weather extremes and disasters. Erosion baseline scenarios and climate vulnerability are analyzed in more detail in section D.

The Sudano-Guinean Savannah (Savannah) agro-ecological zone where this programme is largely focused is one of the ecological zones with the highest deforestation and emissions in Ghana. This is due to the high dependence on agriculture for livelihoods, coupled with increasing population pressure leading to increased land use and deforestation and remaining carbon sequestration from forest and land use to help meet its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) targets. The climate change vulnerability assessment shows that this zone has an average annual rainfall of 950 mm from 1976-2010. The climate variability is one of the major reasons for the high vulnerability in agricultural production.

The programme's objective is to empower those vulnerable women groups in this most vulnerable agro-ecological zone through Line of Credit (LoC) and through Technical Assistance (TA) to participate in low-emission climate resilient agriculture (CRA) practices. The programme will be implemented in Component 1, affordable loans to micro, small, medium-sized enterprises (MSMEs) and farmer based associations (FBAs) led by women who will adopt low-emissions and climate-resilient agricultural practices in Ghana; and in component 2, technical assistance for adoption of low-emission and climate resilient

## C1.1 Climate Change Policies and Strategies.

Ghana is committed to measures to reduce vulnerability of climate risks to human lives, ecosystems, production systems and assets as well as measures to ensure growth that is decoupled from high carbon emissions. The Government has also committed itself to the implementation of the National Master Plan against Climate Change for the five-year period 2015-2020, which is based on agriculture, infrastructure, communities, carbon sinks, ecosystems, health, water, gender, migration and energy sectors.

Ghana submitted its first Nationally Determined Contributions (NDC) to the UNFCCC in September 2016. The NDC is enbodied on: (i) Ghana’s National Climate Change Master Plan (2015-2020); (ii) its national medium-term development plans (Ghana Shared Growth Development Agenda II - GSGDAGII; the anticipated 40-year socio-economic development plan for Ghana, Ghana Vision 2030); and (iii) the National Climate Change Adaptation Strategy.

## C.1.2. GHG Emissions Contribution of the Agriculture Sector in Ghana

Ghana ranks 151 of 188 countries for per capita emissions, contributing only 0.07% of global emissions. However, the country with attaining middle-income country status and still pursuing increasing growth and socio-economic development. The agricultural sector is a major contributor to GHG emissions in Ghana. Agriculture for example currently contributes about 24% of GHG emissions which is expected to increase drastically if measures are not put in place. This is because the sector is projected to grow at an average rate of more than 5% linked to economic growth in Ghana. On average, Ghana's agriculture has grown at a rate of more than 5% within the past decade. Without any mitigation measures are not put in place at least there will be over 5% growth in GHG emissions from the agricultural sector.

The AFWA funding proposal will contribute to 5 of the 7 priority areas of the NDC. Those are: 1. sustainable land use, including food security; 2. equitable social development; 5. sustainable energy security; 6. sustainable forest management and conservation; and 7. climate change adaptation. The AFWA funding proposal is intended to help Ghana pursue its NDC targets for both mitigation and adaptation. Agriculture, forestry and other land uses contribute to carbon emissions in Ghana. ## D.1 Value Added for GCF Involvement

## Economic

Although agriculture has declined to around 20% in terms of its contribution to GDP in Ghana in the recent years, it remains a vital part of the economy in relation to crop production and employment. It is an important sector in the Ghanaian economy as it contributes significantly to the country’s foreign exchange earnings through cocoa and gold exports. Agriculture (MfA), agriculture is key to overall economic growth and development in Ghana and is expected to lead the growth of the economy over the next decade. The agricultural sector accounts for 30% of GDP, employs about half of the size of the agricultural sector. Ghana is a net importer of several key agricultural products such as rice, wheat, sugar and poultry (FAO, 2015). Small-scale producers, with average farm size of about 1.2 hectares, dominate Ghana’s agriculture production system. The majority of these smallholder farmers are located in the northern regions of Ghana.

Smallholder farmers account for about 80% of domestic production and the farming population is aging, as young people migrate to urban areas in search of better opportunities. This has led to a decline in productivity levels due to low levels of fertilizer use, reliance on rain-fed agriculture sub-optimal crop varieties, limited information and access to markets, and low climate-resilience. This project seeks to address these challenges by promoting financing to climate-sensitive activities that tend to be largely overlooked due to perceived risks.

Ghana graduated to lower middle-income country (MIC) status in 2010. The shift was achieved earlier than expected in part through a technical statistical adjustment in 2011. Although recent economic growth has been driven by services and manufacturing, the agricultural sector remains an important contributor to Ghana’s economy with cocoa and gold exports. Ghana's economy has been affected by the discovery of coastal oil from near the Western Region since 2011 when production commenced. The Petroleum Revenue Management Act (2011) has guaranteed that payments will be made to the government in exchange for the right to extract hydrocarbons. Payments are set at 30% of receipts to be set aside for savings; disposal of the remaining 70% is down to the Ministry of Finance, which is charged with ensuring that these funds are used for infrastructure and development projects. The discovery of oil has led to a significant increase in public expenditure on infrastructure and wider development impacts from oil production and revenues are perceived to have been slow to materialize. This project seeks to address this challenge by promoting financing to climate-sensitive activities, which tend to be largely overlooked due to perceived risks.

The country is still a high GDP country with a weakening currency have slowed alongside the more recent slump in oil prices, which has increased government borrowing from the domestic market. Falling growth rates have further been amplified due to a severe energy crisis that has led to power cuts and industrial slowdowns. The Ghanaian government’s economic growth was expected to slow down to an estimated 3.9% growth rate in 2015. This initiative strongly aligns with Ghana's sustainable development goals (SDGs) by promoting financing for agriculture from a manager 3% of credit to the private sector to at least 10% over the next 3 years particularly because agriculture has declined to around 20% in terms of its contribution to GDP.

# GENDER ANALYSIS AND SOCIAL INCLUSION IN GHANA

## I. GENERAL GENDER ANALYSIS

The Gender Africa Index 2019 ranked Ghana 20th out of 51 countries, reflecting the discrimination embedded in customary laws, social norms and practices and by inappropriate legal protection against gender discrimination in all dimensions of social institutions. Restrictive cultural practices and beliefs continue to be the biggest barriers to gender equality and women's empowerment in the country.

### I.1 Legal framework and institutional mechanisms for gender machinery in Ghana

The Government of Ghana has made great progress in improving its legislative frameworks for promoting gender equality, including through the 1992 Constitution and other national laws. The Government of Ghana has also ratified various international conventions and agreements that provide a legal basis for gender equality in areas such as women's access to finance, land and other productive resources, participation in decision-making processes, and protection from violence. However, the challenges in implementing the international laws and creating an enabling environment include: competing government priorities and political will; weak gender mainstreaming capacity at all levels of governance; lack of coordination among actors in the public sector; lack of effective monitoring and evaluation systems and practice within the sector; many weak and unaccountable institutions.

In Ghana, one of the major challenges to the effectiveness of sector policies and plans is that they are not strictly linked to budget allocations. The National Gender Policy (2015) is an example where the policy has been developed but there is no clear implementation plan. Another challenge is the absence of any multi-sector coordination mechanism to ensure that this process is implemented within all sectors. This process should be coordinated by the Ministry of Gender, Children and Social Protection (MoGCSP). The National Gender Policy was approved in all Ministries, but these gender desks do not have the necessary capacities, budget or resources to implement it effectively. Furthermore, the MoGCSP Department of Gender (responsible for coordinating and monitoring them) is unable to ensure the necessary follow-up because of its institutional weakness. As such, the contribution of this policy towards achieving gender equality has been limited and evaluated.

### I.2 Governance and participation of women in decision-making positions

Few women participate in politics, public life and formal decision-making and this has further diminished the voice and interests of women in public governance. Ghana is currently ranked 72nd on the most recent Global Gender Gap Index (2017) and 117th when it comes to Political Empowerment. Women's representation in Parliament has increased from just two seats out of 275 as of 2018, showing a very modest progress in the last 30 years (as of 1996 the proportion was only one seat). The National Gender Policy of Ghana adopted in December 1998 set a target of 40% representation of women at all levels of governance, but it has lacked the necessary legal 'force'. The Affirmative Action Bill was subsequently drafted to promote gender equality and increase women's political participation in society, but as it is yet to be passed into law. Strong political will is needed for this.

# I.3 Gender Statistics in Ghana

The availability and use of quality sex-disaggregated data and statistics is critical in tracking progress on gender equality, monitoring the situation of women and girls, and informing advocacy, policy planning and implementation. There has been a marginal improvement in school enrolment rates for girls at all levels. Gender parity has now been reached in primary education (92%), junior high schools and senior high school and TVET institutions. However, there are still gaps to be achieved. While there have been improvements in Ghana, the quality of education remains a problem. ## II.2. Gender analysis of agricultural finance in Ghana

Women play a major role in the agriculture value chain, from farming to food processing and marketing. However, women face obstacles to access funding for agribusinesses due to the limited capacity of women to produce high-quality proposals, limited access to information on how and where to access finance, lack of guarantee for climate change-related risks, mindsets about the agricultural sector being a man's domain, and gender-based violence.
