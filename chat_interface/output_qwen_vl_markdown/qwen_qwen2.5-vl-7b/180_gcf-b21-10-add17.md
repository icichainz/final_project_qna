# Climate Strategy

AFD was one of the first international donors to integrate the fight against climate change into its practices. This approach, initiated more than a decade ago, is based on the principle that the fight against climate change must be integrated in all aspects of development policies of countries, in terms of opportunities and threats, as well as the involvement of ecosystems and natural resources in the development process.

Since then and in line with its national positioning and its involvement in ecological and climatic issues, AFD has developed a strategy to support the fight against climate change through climate finance. The AFD Group has thoroughly adjusted its intervention strategy and commitment levels according to the level of development of countries concerned, as well as the projects designed to provide climate co-benefits.

AFD's Climate Strategy is based on a strong marker of its identity, with three main aspects: (i) systematically measuring the carbon footprint of the operations financed (carbon balance), (ii) committing to finance 50% of projects with a positive impact on climate change, (iii) an ambitious quantitative commitment to financing 50% of projects with climate co-benefits. This strategy is based on the principle that the fight against climate change must be integrated in all aspects of development policies of countries concerned and at the level of development of the countries concerned. This strategy has profoundly influenced AFD's approach, its operations, the operational framework, and has also shaped its partnership strategy.

This strategy was presented to the AFD Group Board visibility in 2015 and implemented at both national and international levels. It is actively involved in most major initiatives undertaken with its peers and civil society actors. AFD was particularly involved in issues of economic modeling, issues related to climate change, issues of strategic integration of financial models, and issues of strategic integration of climate dimensions into economic modeling.

The new international momentum, transcribed in the SDGs and the Paris Agreement, requires AFD to support fundamental and rapid changes in the development models of countries concerned. Moreover, through the setting up of its Climate Plan in July 2017, the French government wished to reinforce the commitment made by the AFD Group to fight against climate change at the international level. This provides AFD with a framework that extends its mandate by allowing it to support the implementation of the Paris Agreement and to continue to implement the Paris Agreement across its entire portfolio. AFD must therefore continue the transformation of methods and instruments, launched within the framework of its previous climate strategies.

Three strategic challenges have been identified concerning the role and place of AFD in climate change: (i) ensuring that all activities are consistent with the commitments made by the Paris Agreement, in support of low-carbon and climate-resilient development and related public policies; (ii) increasing the volume of financing for projects with a positive impact on leverage effects; and strengthening AFD's role as a platform for France's international financial commitment to climate and its positioning as a reference on climate and development among International Financial Institutions.

The new AFD Climate and Development Strategy for 2017-2022 thus proposes four major commitments: (i) ensure activities are 100% Paris Agreement compatible, (ii) increase the volume of financing for projects with positive leverage effects, (iii) support the development of co-build solutions and bring influence to bear on standards.

### A.2. Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

Delivering the large investment flows needed to address climate change can only be achieved by leveraging the resources and capabilities of the financial sector. Local financial partners (LFPs), as the main financiers of the private sector in developing countries, can play a key role in financing this transition. However, market barriers continue to limit access to finance at scale, including insufficient access to finance, low internal capital, and a limited pipeline of bankable projects.

The TSFC Program aims to engage LFPs to scale up private sector climate finance. The Program builds on over a decade of experience in implementing the ATD SURE initiative (Sustainable Use of Natural Resources and Energy Finance Initiative).

The goal of the Program is to create a market for investments in climate technologies in 17 countries by removing the financial and technical barriers faced by LFPs to enter borrowing by, mainly, the private sector. The Program will facilitate project funding (through credit lines) in the target countries and will develop the capacity of LFPs and project developers to scale up climate finance.

The projects will deliver GHG reductions estimated at 36 million tCO2e over the 20-year lifetime of the mitigation investments, and increased resilience for an estimated 200,000 people from investments in increased resilience (total benefits) in water resources management, agriculture and food security, and disaster risk reduction.

The total project cost is USD 653 million, of which EUR 240 million of GCF co-finance and EUR 413 million of co-financing from AFD. The GCF finance, comprising EUR 31 million in grants and EUR 209 million in concessional loans will leverage additional private sector investment (USD 586 million) through LFPs (EUR 246 million of LFPs and EUR 613 million as equity from end-borrowers) on both mitigation and adaptation.

### B.3. Financial Markets Overview (if applicable)

Despite countries' commitment to fight against climate change in their NDC and a number of initiatives supported by Governments and bilateral/multilateral donors, the climate finance market is still nascent in many countries. This is due to the pre-existing financial infrastructure that has been built up over decades, which is often not well-suited for the new barrier and it is partially due to an unattractive risk-return because of high (real or perceived) levels of risk or insufficient risk-taking capacity of local banks. In addition, many countries have limited access to international markets, which further translates into inadequate sources of necessary long-term debt finance. In addition, LFPs usually have insufficient market experience and the financial and technical feasibility of climate-related loan applications and investment proposals, especially from SMEs.

The number of local financial institutions with a dedicated climate strategy and climate portfolio is still very limited. Most of the existing climate lending capacity is focused on a small number of big companies with high creditworthiness. The majority of the climate finance market is dominated by foreign banks and multilateral development banks in the local banking sector. Addressing the barriers to climate finance is crucial to unlock the climate investment potential.

The Programme is designed to remove or reduce the financial and technical barriers faced by local project sponsors, The Programme will provide a range of incentives for financial institutions to finance projects that have a long amortization period, especially renewable energy, environmental, and forestry projects. In some identified countries, depending on the financial gap and the local needs, the Programme will provide concessional financing in the form of grants or low-interest loans to help overcome the lack of access to favorable lending conditions to their clients. The objective of this incentive is to grow their market offer by adding an attractive offer for climate finance projects that are currently not considered financially viable.

Finally, the Programme will provide technical assistance and a significant grace period to allow financial institutions to build up their project pipeline, invest in internal capacities, design a dedicated marketing strategy, and finance the first eligible projects in good conditions.

# C.1 Strategic Context

To address climate change, the international community is converging towards a common agenda that recognizes the need to build a model with a reduced carbon footprint in order to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals and the objectives of the Paris Agreement.

Historically, the Paris Agreement marks the recognition by countries in the global North and South of the need to change current development models. Three long-term objectives have been defined in the Article 2: keep the increase in the global average temperature to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and pursue efforts to limit the warming to 1.5°C; enhance resilience and adaptive capacity, including through early warning systems, of all countries against the risks and impacts of climate change; increase the ability to adapt to the adverse impacts of climate change and foster climate-resilient development on a sustainable basis.

The achievement of these objectives concerns all Parties and, even in a context of differentiated responsibilities, a sharp reduction of emissions will be required in many AFD intervention countries. At the same time, many countries in which AFD operates are the most vulnerable to the effects of climate change.


In addition to the Agreement, one of the key results of COP21 is that all countries have published their nationally determined contributions (NDCs) and communicated them to the UNFCCC. These NDCs represent a commitment by each country to reduce its greenhouse gas emissions in order to limit global warming to 2°C or even 1.5°C compared with pre-industrial levels.

While these are indeed very crucial initial national references in terms of emissions targets, the targets defined for 2030 and beyond are not sufficient to achieve the Paris Agreement objective of limiting global warming to 2°C (target reduction of at least 15 Gt of CO2 equivalent per year in 2030, which would lead to a warming of about 3.8°C).

Local financial institutions have a key role to play in scaling up climate finance for local private investments. However, despite the dynamism and profitability of these sectors, investors still face numerous obstacles: lack of awareness on the environmental and social impacts of their activities; lack of information on the availability of public or commercial funding, etc. Contributing to removing these technical and financial barriers would allow these institutions to finance transformative change in order to contribute to climate action.


Therefore, AFD has for many years been engaged in an active partnership with many LFPs, notably through the SUNFIRE initiative in order to promote climate change mitigation and adaptation investments in developing countries. AFD has launched the SUNFIRE initiative in 2006. To date, 50 SUNFIRE projects have been successfully implemented in 18 African, Asian and Latin American countries, for a total commitment of over EUR 2.6bn of loans allocated by AFD, including EUR 1.9bn already disbursed.

SUNFIRE has been a successful "green finance" label. It is an innovative integrated approach to environmental finance through a combination of financial and technical support. It provides a range of tools and services for this, which are increasingly in demand from LFPs: co-financing; guarantees; risk-sharing; project preparation; capacity-building; support to structure green investments; assistance in the implementation of an environmental and social management system (ESMS); policy, and valorisation of the results achieved (e.g. energy savings, agri-foretal, CO2 emissions avoided, etc.).

Building on this experience, AFD wishes to take the SUNFIRE initiative further by increasing and diversifying its scope worldwide. More specifically, the aim of the Programme is to redirect financial flows towards more diversified investments in order to achieve a greater impact: - the financing of more adaptation projects in agriculture/land use, water management, and infrastructures/buildings resilient to climate change; - more green infrastructure, energy efficiency projects, forest programmes, and waste management on

According to the ND-GAIN index, all target countries are in the first quartile in terms of resilience, i.e., among the most vulnerable countries worldwide (Benin, Burkina Faso, Cameroon, Côte d'Ivoire, Kenya, Madagascar, Nigeria, Tanzania, Togo, and Uganda – ND-GAIN, 2014). Most of the target countries are therefore severely vulnerable to climate change, which means these are potential opportunities for the private sector in terms of adaptation investments. The medium-vulnerable recipient countries have similar challenges but with less severe impacts on their economies and populations. However, they also have significant market sizes (for example, Mauritius, and Morocco). In this context, the Programme aims to boost emerging markets in Africa by supporting the development of a climate-resilient private sector that can contribute to national sector and to demonstrate additional business opportunities in a changing climate, both for companies and financial services providers. Based on the analysis of the current market size of the renewable energy and energy efficiency pipeline identified in the country notes, it is estimated that more than 350 companies will develop climate resilience investments and services, which will contribute to the resilience pathway of their local economy. The total number of beneficiaries from these investments in climate resilience is estimated to be 1 million.

### Key barriers and challenges
Depending on the local context, a number of obstacles can hold back the implementation of climate investments. These are:
- **Policy and regulatory barriers:** lack of legal structures and systems to push the market and open up the sector; lack of climate and environmental regulation and legal binding targets which is often a hindrance to private sector investment in climate resilience projects; limited capacity in energy planning and modeling and integrating RE into energy planning, and capacity in integrated climate change and energy planning; lack of clarity on the role of the public sector in supporting private sector grids; Support for regulatory frameworks (PPAs etc.); Market regulation particularly for equipment standards.
- **Financial barriers:** high perceived risk of climate related investments, limited or inappropriate financial instruments to support investment in climate resilience projects, lack of access to finance and inability to secure their long-term benefits, lack of attractiveness of climate finance resources; lack of financing structures and systems to support private sector investment.
- **Technical barriers:** limited access to technology; insufficient experience of LPFs and private sector companies in originating commercially viable climate related projects; lack of technical knowledge among LPFs and private sector companies on the identification and implementation of adaptation and mitigation projects, and investment proposals; lack of awareness among LPFs and private sector companies about the benefits of climate resilience investments.

## D.1 Value Added for GCF Involvement

The GCF has a crucial role to play in mobilizing private sector investments in climate mitigation and adaptation in developing countries. The Fund will do this by providing concessional funding (e.g., through the AFD) to diversify/increase sectors of intervention and areas, strengthening the involvement of private sector (MSMEs, riskier projects). This will have a particularly positive impact on Climate resilience that are perceived by the private sector as too risky. The Fund will also provide technical assistance (TA) in order to help MSMEs understand and adopt best technical options, provide support for developing and appraising projects and remove non-financial barriers. Software solutions will be provided to facilitate access to climate finance and allow the private sector to take more risks on projects often seen as not commercially viable by banks. The Fund will also contribute to supporting a nascent green market.

i. To target new geographical areas where climate finance usually does not flow.
ii. To provide a wider range of financial instruments with the view to respond all types of needs
iii. To accompany second-tier banks
iv. To target smaller and less served beneficiaries: small enterprises, women, individuals in remote areas, and more vulnerable groups
v. To accompany second-tier banks
vi. To support dialogue through local public entities about different areas of impacts (financial regulation, fiscal policy, budgetary support mechanisms, etc.)
vii. To provide technical assistance to countries where AFD is not able to provide grants, to build banks' capacities in mobilizing and managing international climate finance and to support the sustainability and transformation of the programs
viii. To increase the volume of climate financing and fill the market gap thanks to concessional funding where necessary

Without GCF's contribution, it would take considerably longer for existing market barriers to be removed. In the meantime, private sector finance for climate investments would remain limited to a certain number of borrowers and remain too costly for many others. GCF's contribution will unlock the considerable potential for climate mitigation and adaptation action in developing countries.

More specifically, the added value of the GCF funding will help to operationalize the commitments made by the countries in the Paris Agreement and to serve as a concrete example of actions which can be implemented in the private sector.

### Solar Lighting Project implemented by SUNREF in Mauritius Island

For over a century, Medine Investment has been the main means of real estate and agriculture. This approach has resulted in energy savings of an extent 92,000 kWh/year, and an emission reduction of estimated 91 ICDG/tons per year.

The key innovations of the project include the installation of vertical sun-shades, verandas and deep eaves to reduce solar radiation entering the building by altering heights of the ceiling and the position of openings, as well as placing a layer of reflective and isolating material on the roof.

The reduction of electricity consumption linked to lighting was also implemented (motion detector devices), LED lamps, openings to improve natural lighting), as well as the installation of a solar water heating system.

To reduce water consumption for sanitary purposes, the rainwater harvesting system is used for all non-drinking purposes within the school. This system also serves as a pedagogical tool to raise awareness amongst students about water saving and usage. Additionally, a double-flush function and push-taps results in an automatic interruption of the water flow, resulting in substantial savings.

Thanks to SUNREF, the Middle Society benefited from financial support as well as technical assistance to realise these green building measures which are not only energy efficient but also contribute to its contributions to protecting the environment as well as the replicability of the measures available to other school buildings.

### E.4.2 Financial, economic, social and institutional needs

For local financial institutions which are evolving in a very competitive environment, their need is to meet the demand, to distinguish their offer compared to their peers, and to improve their image/social recognition; access to new markets related to climate change projects is also an opportunity for them.

However, local financial institutions generally lack expertise to assess and finance climate investments and do not always have a realistic view of the risks associated. Climate-related projects are still viewed as complex, risky, and having high transaction costs. As these projects are often large in scale and require significant collateral, they are, the requested collaterals are significant and represent a heavy burden for private companies (especially SMEs).

In addition, climate investments need mid-to-long-term loans that LPFs may have difficulties to propose.

For many companies, the needs are to develop new production methods strengthening the resilience of their company to extreme shocks and increasing their competitiveness. More and more companies realize the need to manage environment-related risks and reduce their carbon footprint, water use/waste load, etc., in order to reduce their risks of being non-compliant with environmental protection rules thanks to a better anticipation.

However, project investors do not always have sufficient skills to offer attractive and bankable projects. Most of the time, audits are required for the identification of these projects.

To overcome these barriers, the GCF contribution is needed to set up an TA programme for LPFs and companies and to develop concessional funding/financial incentives to trigger their investment. GCF contribution will allow addressing these needs by providing technical assistance to LPFs and companies in order to identify bankable projects from the basis of certain criteria (maturity of green financing market, type of investment, target client base) in order to reduce the transaction costs. As a result, it is expected that there will be an increased access to more affordable lending by commercial banks in the target countries for climate related projects.

The purpose of the Programme is also to answer indirectly to other needs of investors such as (i) the access to low-cost new technologies, (ii) the use of renewable energies, which are often the only way for isolated households to access energy, and (iii) the use of more environmentally friendly and less harmful solutions to adapt to climate change.

Note: AGF Group has set up a framework for the use of concessionality in blended operations dedicated to the private sector. Provided that the project has positive externalities in terms of sustainable development or public interest, concessionality can be mobilized to target the following objectives:

- Compensate primarily for the higher transaction costs and risks not acceptable for LFPs;
- Lower negative externalities linked to social exclusion that could occur from an operation implemented at market conditions (especially in education and health sectors);
- Support projects with a positive effect on specific communities or categories of population;
- Support emerging technologies that have a positive effect on a specific community or a given category of population;
- Facilitate the replication of projects that have a strong demonstrative effect;
- Ease the development of a nascent market;
- Support the implementation of public policy through private actors;
- Accelerate or reach higher impacts in terms of environment protection or social inclusion.

Concessional funding is a critical tool to spur investments in climate-proof technologies. Hence, transferring concessionality and all "positive" terms of AGF/GCF funding going forward to LFPs for the benefit of end-beneficiaries (On that respect, AFD has had practical experience in negotiating such transfer).

To be fully well-designed, the use of concessionality for private actors also has to be in line with the following principles:

- The amount of concessionality mobilized is adapted to the targeted investments and the market conditions;
- The mobilization of concessionality should not have any crowding out effect on private actors. On the contrary, it should help them access financing at more favorable terms than they would otherwise obtain;
- Concessionality tools need to allow the Programme to reach development results that could not be achieved with other types of funding;
- Exit strategies need to be designed and discussed with the stakeholders at the beginning of the Programme;
- The use of public resources is precisely monitored and justified over the duration of the Programme.

This approach will be used in the course of the Programme, which will ensure an efficient and appropriate use of GCF and AFD concessionality.

- **Instruction:** In-depth analysis of the LFP ESMS is conducted by an AFD E&S Expert through a review of EAS and other relevant documents. A gap analysis is conducted against ADF-GCF requirements and, as required, an EAS is developed or updated in accordance with AFD’s E&S contract clauses, including the ESAP, are drafted.

- **Consultation:** The E&S Due Diligence finalized and integrated into the credit facility agreement.

- **Supervision:** where applicable. AFD E&S Expert follows up the technical assistance activities dedicated to EAS support through regular meetings, workshops, meetings and exchange sessions, and a document review process.

## At Project Level

During the disbursement period, AFD will receive reports from LFPs implementation to track progress and identify potential issues as well as improvement opportunities. AFD local agencies will ensure the day-to-day monitoring of the implementation of projects, meeting with LFPs at least once a year. Following this mission, a project implementation report will be shared with all the project stakeholders (central and local governments, companies, NGOs etc.). The report will include information for overseeing project implementation and for approving funding disbursements. On a yearly basis, all projects will be assessed by AFD local agencies to ensure that they are on track in terms of progress, financial management (disbursement, procurement, outcomes, etc.). Red flag projects that meet any kind of difficulties are subject to a specific monitoring process before returning to a regular implementation pathway.

Consultants will be tasked with independently and objectively overseeing the correct implementation of projects. In particular, AFD will ensure that the required conditions have been fulfilled before proceeding with a disbursement. Accounting and financial audits could also be conducted by the Group AFD or an independent third party.

At the end of the disbursement period, AFD will prepare a project completion report summarizing the main events of the implementation phase, the results achieved, and the lessons learned. This report will also provide recommendations for future projects.

During the repayment period, a biannual monitoring of the use of funds will be carried out by the local agencies and all corporate level by the middle office.

In addition, about a year after drafting of the project completion report, the Evaluation department of AFD may consider conducting an evaluation of projects or appoint a local consultant to do so. In most cases, AFD prefers to select a local consulting firm based in the country where the project is located. This allows the Agency to take advantage of direct knowledge and experience with the project.

The consultant will be tasked with evaluating the impact of the project (economic, environmental etc.) and writing a report that will be shared with all relevant stakeholders (central and local governments, companies, NGOs etc.) and civil society organizations.

# ANNEX B: AFD Carbon Footprint

## I.1 The AFD Carbon Footprint Tool’s general principles

### A. The AFD Carbon Footprint Tool's guiding principle

The AFD Carbon Footprint Tool is a spreadsheet-based tool that uses an activity's operational data to estimate its GHG emissions. A carbon footprint calculation is created by making an inventory of a project's activities, using physical data inputs, e.g., kilowatt hours (kWh) of electricity and/or number of diesel consumption, etc.

### B. Gases measured

The AFD Carbon Footprint Tool’s emissions inventory counts the six main greenhouse gases identified in the Kyoto Protocol:

- **Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)**: which results primarily from combusting fossil fuels and from producing aluminum, steel, cement and glass.
- **Methane (CH₄)**: which results from burning and/or decomposing biomass (organic material) and from livestock manure, rice cultivation, landfills and natural gas leaks.
- **Nitrous Oxide (N₂O)**: which results from incinerating solid waste, spreading fertilizers, and/or various transportation means.
- **Hydrofluorocarbons**: which occur as a by-product of industrial processes making insulation, refrigeration and air conditioning.
- **Perfluorocarbons**: which are the result of a product of aluminum production.
- **Sulphur hexafluoride (SF₆)**: which is used for insulation and current interruption in electricity transmission and distribution equipment and electronic systems.

Each type of greenhouse gas has a different potency and atmospheric global warming potential. For instance, one metric tonne of carbon dioxide gas is equal to 1 metric tonne; it has an average warming potential 2930 times greater than carbon dioxide; one-half kilo of SF₆ has the same global warming impact as 11 tonnes of carbon dioxide.

To reconcile the differences between the warming potentials of various gases and have a consistent measure for their warming impacts, a specific measurement unit—the tonne CO₂ equivalent or tCO₂e—is used. It expresses the equivalence of one metric tonne of GHG to the number of metric tonnes of CO2 that would generate the same warming effect over one hundred years. (For more information on the IPCC's Global Warming Potential for various gases, see the Climate Change (CC) Fourth Assessment Report, page 36: http://www.ipcc.ch/pdf/assessment-report/ar4/wg1/ar4_syr.pdf)

## Why measure direct and indirect GHG emissions?

Measuring indirect emissions can pose problems: the risk of double counting, no 'ownership' or control over them, and regulatory frameworks that only cover direct emissions, such as the European Union Emission Trading System.

Notwithstanding these issues, AFD's choice to measure carbon footprints using the largest scope is in line with its goal to clarify the link between development and the climate, and follows a widespread trend:

- In the same way that a project’s development impacts are measured via indirect effects, e.g., the number of children in school or extent of road or air traffic etc., understanding a project's emissions must include its indirect emissions, e.g., vehicle or airplane emissions, final energy use, etc.
- A project's vulnerability to higher costs for fuel and/or tonne of CO2 is tied to direct emissions and to the upstream and downstream value chain, e.g., a carbon-intensive supplier and/or changes in demand due to such price increases.

### C. Certainty and accuracy

The AFD Carbon Footprint Tool's purpose lies in providing an order-of-magnitude accounting for the GHG emissions that a future project will create or abate. An approximate accounting is sufficient, the inherent uncertainties in some emission factors—as indicated in the AFD Carbon Footprint Tool—and the uncertainty of the data inputs used to calculate the carbon footprint must be taken into account once the calculation is made ex ante. However, for most projects, usually the main emissions sources are limited in number so any quantitative uncertainty will not affect their relative ranking and should not impede taking a decision. The AFD Carbon Footprint Tool's purpose lies in providing an order-of-magnitude accounting for the project's calculation accounts for only the main GHG emissions sources; they are indicated later in this document.

A carbon footprint measurement—even if approximate—helps AFD analyze projects and guides investment and financing decisions; an order-of-magnitude accounting suffices for that.

### D. Conservativeness

AFD, similarly to other development banks, uses a 'conservative' approach in measuring the carbon footprints of the projects it finances. The assumptions and values used to calculate the carbon footprint must tend towards overestimating emissions and underestimating reductions, especially if data inputs are uncertain.

### E. Project lifetime

The calculation of GHG emissions resulting from a project covers the project's entire lifetime, which the AFD Carbon Footprint Tool user determines. The project lifetime includes both construction and operating phases:

- Construction phase: If building the project will generate negligible emissions, its construction phase is not included in the accounting; if the project generates significant proven emissions, the Tool uses a one-year duration by default.
- Operating phase: For all other types of projects, standardised lifetimes for each type of project are suggested below. The Tool user can change them on a case-by-case basis as needed. The standard lifetimes are set as:
  - 20 years for power generation
  - 30 years for transportation infrastructure
  - 20 years for other projects

### G. Measurable projects

AFD's tool for measuring carbon footprints can be used only for a certain type of project, i.e., a "measurable" and "significant" project, as defined in the third category below.

AFD categorizes projects into:

- **Projects that cannot be measured with the AFD Carbon Footprint Tool**: This category includes budget support, some financial intermediation, budgetary financing for local governments, and capacity-building projects. AFD is currently developing tools and methods to measure emissions for its intermediated financing operations; the difficulty of accurately attributing funds to one project or another poses a challenge.

- **Projects that can be measured with the AFD Carbon Footprint Tool that have a negligible carbon footprint**: This category includes projects in sectors that AFD has identified as being weakly emissive.   - Agriculture
  - Education

- **Projects that can be measured and that have a significant carbon footprint**: Other qualified project types are measurable and generate emissions significant enough to justify calculating their carbon footprint. This category includes any project that will reduce more emissions than it generates (see section 1).
