## States targeted for project activities

The project will target a total population of 138 villages in dryland zones across 9 states in Sudan. The states share common characteristics, namely subsistence agriculture, poverty, and vulnerability to climate change, with few opportunities for household income diversification and adaptation. Despite their varied geographical locations, the states are all characterized by high levels of food insecurity and extreme vulnerability in the face of climate change. A brief description of the major targeted state characteristics, together with key target areas within each state is provided below.

- **West Darfur:** West Darfur is characterized by great environmental diversity with seasonal weather that can sustain a wide range of crops and livestock. The region has a history of food insecurity due to poor rainfall and livestock production. Nevertheless, the state has a history of chronic food insecurity—it is the most food insecure region in Sudan.

- **East Darfur:** East Darfur is largely characterized by ranching tribes facing acute water scarcity. Increasingly random variability has led to serious rangeland degradation and, in some cases, the disappearance of essential grasses and shrubs. This has resulted in a decline in livestock numbers and poor quality of livestock. Limited fodder options for their livestock, namely lower quality tree leaves, limited crop residues, or moving across the border into neighboring states for grazing, have led to increased vulnerability to climate change. The situation is worse for other Darfur states, all suffering from reduced rangeland. This has amplified the consequences of climatic change for these communities.

- **Central Darfur:** Central Darfur is characterized by diverse climate and soils, including volcanic soils in Jebel Marra. Most economic activities are focused on agriculture and pastoralism. The state is vulnerable to food insecurity due to poor rainfall and livestock production. It has a higher level of poverty than other counterparts, the state is vulnerable to the impact of forced migration. That is, as agricultural regions in other parts of Sudan have experienced droughts, they see an influx of climate refugees while lacking the infrastructure to accommodate rapid population growth.

- **South Kordofan:** The state is characterized by widespread poverty, lack of basic services, poor infrastructure and high levels of food insecurity. South Kordofan has a higher level of poverty than its northern counterpart, the state is vulnerable to the impact of forced migration. That is, as agricultural regions in other parts of Sudan have experienced droughts, they see an influx of climate refugees while lacking the infrastructure to accommodate rapid population growth.

- **West Kordofan:** West Kordofan is characterized by nomadic and transhumant tribes that concentrate in areas where rainfall is sufficient for livestock grazing. The region has a history of food insecurity due to poor rainfall variability, which led to crop failure, increased pest incidence, and out-migration for farmers. For pastoralists, lower humidity levels have resulted in reduced availability of water sources. In addition, the state has experienced diminishing levels of healthy drinking water due to lower rainfall as well as a higher incidence of certain climate-related epidemics.

## Project Background

### Kasai Valley
Kasai is characterized by widespread poverty and lack of basic services. Roughly 85% of the population lives below the poverty line and rely on traditional rain-fed agriculture. Flash flooding is a common occurrence, with frequent seasonal flooding from the Gash and Atbara rivers in the western part of the state. While floods have been occurring for many years, their frequency has increased over time, and their intensity has also increased, with two major droughts occurring in 2008 and 2014.

### Red Sea Coastal Region
The Red Sea coastal region is one of the most arid regions in Sudan. It is the only state with a coastline (750 km). The region supports varied and diverse coastal and marine habitats, including coral reefs, mangroves, seagrass beds, and sandy beaches. These ecosystems are home to many species that are found nowhere else in the world. These resources also provide food and income for the communities living along the coast.

### Bahr el Ghazal North
Bahr el Ghazal North is a region characterized by rainfall that depends upon both irrigated and rain-fed agriculture. In this region, rainfall is typically very low; temperatures are high in the extreme, and vegetative cover is sparse. The climate has become more erratic over time with increased frequency of droughts. Increased river Nile water levels, and increased wind speeds have contributed to a result of a mix of droughting and flooding. These climatic changes have also hastened the arrival of new plant diseases such as the date palm disease in the Elgei area, and near-salinity problems in some areas.

### Khartoum State
Khartoum is the capital of Sudan and is in the tropical zone around the River Nile. It is characterized by rapid urban growth and the largest concentration of infrastructure. About 20% of the state population lives within its boundaries, which are highly vulnerable to flooding due to regular occurrences and river fluctuations threatening marshland erosion and flooding. Increasing climatic variability have placed additional pressure on the water resources in Khartoum State.

## Lessons Learned
- Rural water supply for domestic and small-scale irrigation using solar pumping has been readily adopted and effective in several rural settings, resulting in availability of water for agriculture and clean water for human an animal consumption.
- Cultivation of drought-resistant horticultural crops (e.g., introduction of new vegetables and practicing cultivation techniques) has resulted in increased yields and income generation. This has contributed to improved crop productivity.
- Rainwater harvesting systems in irrigated production (e.g., at Wasil Hassan village of Qasdar State) contributed to the creation of new income sources and labor opportunities, which contributed to improved social well-being.
- Shelter belts around some farms in River Nile State demonstrated protection from heat waves and also created favorable microclimates, which helped to increase productivity and yields.
- Afforestation and reforestation activities have been carried out. In total 105,327 trees were established, and 53,300 trees were planted – effectively protected agricultural lands and residential areas.
- Awareness raising and capacity building through training women's farms led to improvement in crop distribution (e.g., more emphasis on cash crops) and led to women being more oriented to climate change adaptation.

# National barriers

## Weak contingency planning
Sudan’s current drought contingency planning framework contains a weak component for ensuring food reserves in the face of drought. The Strategic Reserve Authority (SRA) was established to manage these reserves but has not been able to achieve its objectives due to limited funding and coordination across state-level ministries.

### Low Institutional Capacity
Institutional capacities to address some of the urgent adaptation needs are inadequate, especially in relation to food security issues. There is an urgent need in Sudan for improving the link between climate change adaptation and development planning. This requires strengthening and participation in environmental activities that account for the specific needs of local communities.

## Limited investment in food security research
The Government’s Agricultural Research Corporation (ARC) has research stations throughout the country, but they are not adequately equipped to develop drought-tolerant varieties, improved crop husbandry practices, water harvesting technologies, and livestock improvement technologies. The ARC is also limited by a lack of funding for research and development (R&D). This limits its ability as a community-based organization.

## Limited smallholder access to financing
Financial and micro-finance institutions are limited due to the risks associated with lending to smallholders. Smallholder farmers (particularly female-headed households) in continuous cycles of poverty and food insecurity. Consequently, farmers and producers have little or no access to credit from commercial banks, financial intermediaries, or the private sector.

## Limited institutional capacity for climate change adaptation
Institutional capacities are limited by a lack of funding for research and development (R&D). This is linked to all three of the project’s strategic components. The project has incorporated successful innovations into project design to promote financial sustainability.

### Costs associated with the proposed interventions have been estimated utilizing accepted benchmarks from other projects undertaken in rural Sudan. A comparison of average unit costs associated with major categories of infrastructure is provided below:

- **Infrastructure for rehabilitating underground reservoirs designed for storing water (Hafras)**: USD 50,000 per hafr. This provides water in water-deficient areas at a very reasonable cost which is estimated to be around 80% less than the cost of supplying drinking water from boreholes or wells. The Hafras can store up to 12.5 cubic meter from mobile water tank during water shortage periods. Whereas the cost of supplying drinking water from boreholes or wells in rural Sudan is USD 3,600 per well. This means that after about three (3-4 months) traditional method will be depleted. The improved half will sustain its service to 5-6 months.

- **Installation costs for rehabilitating existing water yards**: USD 10,000 per water yard. Installation costs for constructing new water yards are USD 70,000 per water yard. These structures are proposed for water scarce areas where the population density is less than 25 people/km². The rehabilitated water yards provide irrigation at around 80% to 90% efficiency compared to 40% to 50% under the traditional methods. Although these costs appear high, they are in line with those reported by other projects undertaken in Sudan. Yemen and Morocco indicates that water, energy and labor savings would return the cost of investment within a period of two years.

- **Installation costs for the introduction of integrated women's farms**: USD 20,000 per farm. Integrated agriculture systems will be introduced to improve food security; increase income generation opportunities; and enhance climate resilience. These schemes will be undertaken in the context of links to customers, suppliers, and markets, as well as through the involvement of local communities and women's groups.

- **Installation costs for constructing water storage dams**: USD 70,000 per dam. Due to relatively extensive cross-sectional area of the seasonal water courses in project sites the cost of building such dams is high compared with other projects undertaken by the Fund. However, it should be noted that the benefits generated (volume of water stored in the subsurface) are high, achieving 100,000 m³ of water storage.

- **Installation costs for drilling and installing new shallow wells/boreholes and associated solar pumping systems**: USD 48,130 per well/borehole. Costs depend on the depth to the groundwater table (typically between 25m and 75m) and the type of pump installed (e.g., 108 m³/hr) with over 75% increase in water delivery to sustain both domestic and irrigation needs. The estimated cost for drilling and installing new shallow wells/boreholes is USD 4,362 per well/borehole at project sites and use of solar-powered pumps.

#### 1. Sudan is one of the African countries with high susceptibility to drought. The country has four physiographic regions with a sharp rainfall gradient along its latitude (north to south). Agricultural activities account for about 30% of the gross domestic product of the country, while over 60 per cent of the gross domestic product is derived from agriculture. Agriculture employs more than half of the total population and provides employment in Sudan. Over 80 per cent of the net cultivable area (nCA) is rain-fed, with only 2 million hectares under irrigation. The main source of water for irrigation is the use of water from the Nile River. 1.3.2 Social co-benefits

In persistent drought conditions there are often conflict situations regarding the access to scarce water resources which precipitates into capture of social bonding among groups that have diverse competing water demands. This project will directly contribute to the greater availability and equitable distribution of water, thereby promoting community cohesion and prevent conflict over dwindling water resources. Moreover, harmonious interventions aimed for local-level adoption are likely to facilitate collective management of the rain-fed pastures and agro-ecosystems. The mechanisms created for peer-to-peer learning from social interactions will contribute to strengthening social bonds among groups that have diverse competing water demands, thereby improving food production, which in turn will improve nutrition for beneficiaries, particularly the women and children.

One of the social co-benefits likely to be enjoyed by the poor people of these regions is access to a supply of good-quality drinking water (for both humans and animals), which in turn will have positive spillover effects on their health. The project also aims at raising awareness and community-level training on sanitation and hygiene, the absence of which will reduce the effectiveness of the agenda regarding quality of the water supply (associated with SDG 6).

The project intends to offer resilience-building support without having a negative impact on the environment. This project is therefore considered as a social or benefit for agro-pastoral producer groups involved in the project.

5. Gender and social inclusion in the context of climate resilience among subsistence farmer communities in Sudan

Climate change is a multidimensional problem that is a priority on the international development agenda. The impacts of climate change are likely to disproportionately affect certain groups in the community particularly vulnerable. As projections indicate that climate change will cause less secure access to water, food and energy resources, coupled with increasing social inequalities (including gender inequalities) and more environmental degradation, it is likely that the poorest and most vulnerable groups in a society will be most affected.

Emerging evidence suggests that climate change will lead to increased rainfall variability and reduced precipitation. Higher temperatures, more intense sandstorms have led to the degradation of natural resources and have led to the increased exposure of farmer communities and pastoralists heavily reliant on rain-fed agriculture. The Sudan Rural Agricultural Development Project (SRADP) has identified five strategic components for effective adaptation to climate change in Sudan's rural areas including:

- Improved seed varieties: Early maturing, stable and drought tolerance varieties of millet, sorghum, groundnut, sesame and cowpeas. Here, activities will focus on the implementation of crops in smallholder farms.

- Control strategies: Improved crop cultivation practices such as spacing, seed priming and micro-dosing, or intercropping. Here, project interventions will focus on the implementation of range and livestock management practices to improve soil fertility and water conservation and nutrition technologies.

- Management practices: Integrated Water Management practices such as water harvesting techniques (ponds, dams, tanks), small scale irrigation, implementing new or improving existing irrigation systems contour bands and ridges. Here, activities will focus on introducing and adopting improved water management practices at different scales, including community, local, national and state-levels; protection of water sources, water harvesting techniques, or protection of large catchments areas.

- Quality product enhancement: Harvest and post-harvest packages to improve crop quality and reduce losses;

- Animal husbandry practices: Livestock health and nutrition (diseases control (vaccination), livestock management practices, improved diet quality, strategic supplementary feeding, feed resource improvement and others).

Within each of the five strategic components, a set of adaptation options are offered that have proven to be effective in improving resilience among subsistence farmers in the Sudan context, and contribute to increasing resilience in Sudan's rural areas. In further sections of this report, interventions of considerations will be given on how these interventions benefit women and men.
