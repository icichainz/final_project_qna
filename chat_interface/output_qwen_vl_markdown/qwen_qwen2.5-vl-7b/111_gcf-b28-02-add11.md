India faces a major climate change challenge. During the last century, India's average temperature increased by nearly 0.7°C (1901-2018), largely on account of anthropogenic Greenhouse Gas (GHG)-emission-induced warming (see table). In the coming decades and beyond, temperatures are projected to rise further. By mid-century, these temperatures are projected to rise by approximately 2.4°C to 4.7°C. Extreme weather events and natural hazards such as droughts, floods, heatwaves, and cyclones will become more frequent and intense. These climate change impacts on critical resources such as water, damaging infrastructure and threatening livelihoods of its population (see climate impact table). The economic costs associated with the adverse effects of climate change are likely to pose challenges to the country's economic development - India can lose the equivalent of 1-10% GDP by 2050.

To date, India has proactively pursued mitigation and adaptation activities and achieved a reduction in emissions intensity of its economy by around 34% on the period 2005–2014. India is on track to meet its Copenhagen commitments, however, to meet its Paris commitments and fully implement its Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) it is timely to leverage international climate finance. The total investment required for meeting India's NDCs in the power sector alone needs to reduce low carbon and climate resilient pathways is estimated at USD 2.5 trillion over a 20-year period. A study by the International Institute for Applied Systems Analysis (IIASA) estimates that India will need to invest $138 billion per year between now and 2030 to meet its target (CPI, 2020). The mitigation and adaptation gaps are especially evident in the infrastructure sector, with the government estimating a USD 4.5 trillion investment gap by 2030.

The proposed program blends equity and grant capital to accelerate the uptake of green infrastructure projects in India. For the purposes of this funding proposal, the term green infrastructure includes investments into the energy value chain (sector), including power generation, transmission, distribution, low carbon and climate resilient options in line with India's climate objectives and sustainable development goals.

This proposal seeks USD 152 million in Equity and USD 4.5 million in technical Assistance (TA) Grant from the Green Climate Fund (GCF). The proposed program is structured as follows:

### Component I: Equity investment into Green Growth Equity Fund (GGEF) - an alternative investment fund with a mandate to mobilize public and private sector capital on scale for long-term, equity investments into India's green infrastructure projects. The GGEF will be established by the Government of India in partnership with the World Bank. It will leverage USD 15 million from the Green Climate Fund with a devolving feature to catalyze new and additional finance from investors.

### Component II: Technical Assistance (TA) Grant funding to set up a complementary USD 4.5 million Technical Assistance Facility at GGEF to address capacity, knowledge and policy gaps hindering uptake of green infrastructure projects in India.

The program is innovatively structured to provide additional finance, which is complimentary to existing initiatives in India's climate change mitigation and adaptation efforts. The program will focus on the following areas: energy value chain infrastructure projects (refer to section B.2 for type of change). This integrated approach to finance enables acceleration of the transfer of low-carbon technologies as well as innovative business models in targeted sectors, while contributing towards India's NDCs. It also supports the development and implementation of policies, strategies, building policy dialogue and knowledge building activities.

The program aligns with India's National Adaptation Plan (NAP), as well as various policy and regulatory incentives available to the programme in response to India's mitigation and adaptation needs under section B.3). In particular, this programme through its investments across the energy value sector chain, transport and water resources sectors will contribute towards India's NDCs by reducing GHG emissions from power generation, increasing water resources in India through investments into wastewater treatment and desalination plants. The total emission reduction potential of the program is estimated at 105 million tonnes CO2e per year (MTCO2e/yr) from power generation and generating additional 4,700 million m3 of water from alternative resources for use by households, farmers and businesses.

The programme also contributes to sustainable development goals, across economic, environmental and social dimensions. In particular, the programme contributes to job creation and increased economic activity; as it results in the transfer of low-carbon technologies and innovative business models into India's energy sector. The program is particularly timely now, in a covid19 pandemic environment which is threatening Indian economy with slowdown, job losses and unemployment. It also addresses other pressing issues such as air pollution, noise pollution, water pollution prevention and health benefits. It is also noteworthy that the Executing Entities under guidance of the Accredited Entity promotes strong corporate governance standards in line with GCF policies as well as its own.

## Emissions from Energy Sector and Impact on Infrastructure

Rising temperatures will increase energy demand for cooling and heating, leading to global warming by increasing GHG emissions. Warmer air and water temperatures may decrease the efficiency of nuclear and thermal power plants, which could lead to increased electricity prices and reduced reliability of the national electricity system. Increased demand for water by power plants would directly compete with water withdrawal for agriculture and other uses. Extreme weather events such as heavy rain and floods can damage critical energy infrastructure** and thus impact reliability of the country’s power supply.

It is noteworthy that India, being a long coastline, where some of its largest cities are located, is among the most densely populated regions of the planet, making it vulnerable to the impacts of sea-level rise. Potential coastal risks include loss of land due to erosion, increased flooding and storm surges, saltwater intrusion into freshwater aquifers, damage to power plants, salinization of freshwater supplies and a heightened vulnerability to flooding. Higher sea levels and repeated flooding events can also lead to soil contamination with cyclonic storms. These impacts are compounded by sea-level rise may be additionally compounded by land subsidence occurring in parts of the country due to its high sedimentation rate.

# Assumption: Energy sector contributes to 73% of GHG emissions in India

Energy sector contributes to 73% of GHG emissions in India, with power generation contributing to ~50% of CO2 emissions from the energy sector. India's energy system is largely based on the use of coal for power generation. To meet growing demand and ensure energy security, India plans to increase its coal-based power capacity by 40%, for a total of 40% of non-fossil fuel-based energy resources. There is an urgent need to introduce alternative RE technologies - such as solar, wind, hydro, etc., which are not only clean but also have high potential.

# Assumption: Transport sector contributes to 13.1% of total emissions from energy sector

Transport sector is largely oil based, contributing to 13.1% of the total emission from the energy sector with road transport contributing to 90% of GHG emissions from this sector. Recent report from International Energy Agency (IEA) has projected that India's road transport sector will be responsible for about 25% of global CO2 emissions by 2030, up over last decade. This trend is also contributing to increasing GHG emission in the country. To reduce GHG emissions from this sector, there is a need to introduce alternative fuels such as biofuels and electric vehicles.

# Assumption: Increasing population, urbanization and industrialization contributes to increased GHG emission from waste, industry and buildings sectors - especially from cities

India's population has been growing at CAGR of 2.5%, which is leading to rapid urbanization and industrialization, and hence increasing the demand for energy and water resources. The country’s population is expected to reach 1.6 billion by 2030 and become the most populated country by 2048, while urban population is likely to increase from current 300 million to about 500 million in 2030. This will put a huge pressure on energy resources as well as water resources. The shift public to e-waste and waste-water treatment will be critical to reduce GHG emissions in India by 2030.

# Assumption: Increasing anthropogenic GHG emissions and associated temperature rise, will disrupt precipitation patterns and hydrological cycle, thus impacting water availability in India

The rapid increase of GHG emissions from various sectors is leading to an increase in global average surface air temperature. The per capita availability of freshwater in India is expected to drop from 1545 Cubic Meter (Cubic meter) (2011 data) to below 1,000 cubic meter by 2050 as a result of the combined effects or climate change and population growth. Availability of alternative water sources to meet increasing demand for water is critical to ensure food security.

The target sectors for the Fund's investments are E-mobility, Resource Efficiency, Energy Services and Renewable Energy and related services. ERIM India Private Limited ("ERIM") has been appointed by Eversource for conducting a Gender Assessment Study and development of a Fund-level Gender Action Plan. ## Brief Information on the Programme

Eversource is the fund manager of the Green Growth Equity Fund (GGEF or the Fund), established with anchor investment from India's National Investment and Infrastructure Fund (NIF) and the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT). The Fund invests in select private sector companies and platforms with high climate impact and innovation.

The Gender Action Plan (GAP) details specific minimum actions to be implemented on a Fund level to ensure that all projects under the Fund are aligned with the findings of this Programme level Gender Assessment. The findings of the project-level gender assessments will be used as inputs while developing the Gender Action Plan for individual projects to make sure that infrastructure works for everyone including women and disadvantaged groups. It is important to note that the implementation of the Fund level Gender Assessment Plan will be performed at individual portfolio level under guidance and supervision from GGEF.

The objective of the assignment is to undertake Gender Assessments and formulate a Gender Action Plan for the Green Growth Equity Fund (GGEF). The assessment has been conducted based on secondary literature review and consultations with the Eversource team, and covers the following activities:

- Review of the current portfolio of GGEF and the current coverage or inclusion of gender related aspects in the project documentation, including monitoring and reporting;

# Gender Mainstreaming and Corporate Governance

The inclusion of gender-sensitive planning and gender mainstreaming at corporate organizations is driven by several factors: first, compliance with international conventions and national-level compliances; secondly, investor requirements (some of which are Development Finance Institutions [DFIs] or entities funded by DFIs); and thirdly, the internal initiatives and objectives of the companies' leadership.

There are various regulatory provisions introduced to manage governance of gender aspects in privately owned Indian companies. These regulations were introduced with the intention of being followed by the government. In order to promote gender diversity at the senior leadership in Indian companies, the Companies Act of 2013 had made it mandatory for publicly listed companies as well as those with a turnover of more than Rs.500 million to have at least one independent director who is a woman. This was revised by the Securities Exchange Board of India (SEBI) in 2017 so that there are at least one independent women directors.

Corporate organizations develop various means to fulfill minimum regulatory requirements as there is limited oversight and scrutiny by the regulators. This is also reflected in the Credit Suisse Gender Scorecard, which shows that the average number of women on corporate boards in India is even for every six men, while the global number is about one for every four men on corporate boards.

The employers in India presently do not undertake gender-focused assessments or initiatives at the institutional level. This is because there are no specific guidelines and frameworks to guide them. This explains the absence of gender mainstreaming strategies, actions, and outcomes. Furthermore, there is a lack of awareness among the employees about gender equality and its importance.

The cultural factors discussed earlier as well as limited awareness that gender-equitable participation requires more than just hiring women in leadership positions also contribute to this situation. # Energy Efficiency

According to "India Energy Policy Review," the primary energy demand in India has grown from about 450 million tons of oil equivalent (mtoe) in 2000 to about 770 mtoe in 2012. This is expected to increase further by 2030, reaching around 980 mtoe.

Government of India has undertaken various measures to cater to the growing energy demand while ensuring environmental sustainability and energy security. These include increasing the share of renewable (such as solar and wind) shifting towards superior technologies for coal based power plants. The country is also focusing on improving the efficiency of existing thermal power plants by applying side wall as well.

Energy efficiency essentially means using lesser amount of energy while producing a great amount of output. This not only means less cost incurred to produce a commodity, but also implies reduction in pollution and greenhouse gas emissions. India has taken several initiatives aimed at energy efficiency through implementation of Environmental Conservation Act in 2001 and setting up the National Energy Efficiency Development Agency (NEEDA) under the Ministry of Power.

There are various policy measures for energy efficiency including standards and labeling of appliances, building codes, energy conservation building codes for commercial buildings, and energy consumption norms for energy intensive industries.

# GCF GENDER POLICY REQUIREMENTS

The GCF Board adopted their Gender Policy and Action Plan in March 2015 and an update to this policy was issued in February 2019 followed by another update to their Gender Action Plan in November 2019 for the years 2020-2023. The updates to the Policy and Action Plan of GCF respond to a March 2018 decision by the Board to adopt a gender-sensitive approach, recognizing the importance of reporting and disclosure of gender-related impacts created by its downstream partners (Accredited Entities). The updated policy is intended to provide guidance on how GCF can best encourage a paradigm shift towards low-emission and climate-resilient development pathways. The updated policy emphasizes gender responsiveness rather than gender sensitivity. Being gender-responsive means that the design, implementation, monitoring, evaluation, and reporting of projects will encourage and enable women and men to participate in decision-making processes and actions. This approach will substantially help to overcome historical gender biases. Gender responsive approaches are embedded within GCF’s policy, including decisions and actions by women and men.

## GCF's Gender Policy aims to:

- Achieve greater, more effective, sustainable, and equitable climate change results, outcomes and impacts, through the adoption of a gender-sensitive approach;
- Build on existing gender equality commitments made under the Fund as well as ensure that men and women equally contribute to and benefit from activities supported by the Fund;
- Address or mitigate risks for women and men associated with adaptation and mitigation activities funded by GCF;
- Reduce the generation of social, economic and environmental vulnerabilities exacerbated by climate change.

For projects implementing Gender Equality Policy, GCF requires that women and men, including marginalized and vulnerable groups, be provided with an equal and equitable opportunity to be fully and effectively engaged in meaningful consultations and decision-making throughout the project cycle.
