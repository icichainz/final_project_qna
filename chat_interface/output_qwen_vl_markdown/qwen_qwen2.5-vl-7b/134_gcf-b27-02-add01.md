#### Pasture Land in Mongolia
Pasture land is a public good and not yet adequately regulated to prevent unsustainable herding practices, which can lead to overgrazing and desertification. Traditional pastoral management approaches, however, have proven successful to foster the necessary cooperation for sustainable pasture management. The current law provides for the use of traditional grazing areas and communal lands as well as private land. These are recognized as entities under the Civil Code (4.8.1.). Current land law provides for the option to allocate uses rights to individuals or groups, but does not provide a clear mechanism for resolving conflicts between the state government and a group of herders/customary users resources for management and protection of an area.

#### Local-level Administration
At local level, aimag and sovem administrations have critical roles in providing technical assistance to herder associations and herder households in joint planning and co-management of natural resources. In this regard, annual land use plans are prepared by the government officials and submitted for approval to the relevant government officials or officers in charge of land, environment and agriculture. This community-based rangeland management approach is known as Community-Based Rangeland Management (CBRM). The herders' associations and user groups are active. Through this approach, herders use grasslands in accordance with the pasture management plan. Herders can also be involved in the planning of land use plans for their areas. These plans are more detailed than previous CBM plans and include a greater emphasis on water features. Pasture landscapes are managed as herd units rather than individual plots. The aim is to reduce competition between herders through pastoral practices that are protected by fencing and large area protection zones. This approach has been found to be more effective in reducing overgrazing, especially when the grazing season is better defined for duoz and leads a smaller proportion of their herds, indicating that CBRM can be an effective tool for sustainable land use management. ### Drying water sources due to climate change and limited wells presenting challenges for herders to access water for livestock

Excessive droughts, floods, and related ecosystems are facing increasing pressure by grazing animals due to the decline in functional livestock water wells. The national IWRM plan indicates that between 1990 and 2005 the number of livestock water points per herd increased from 3.4 to 6.7, while the number of livestock increased from 8 million to over 10 million. This drop in functional livestock wells was mainly due to the collapse of the state systems to maintain and renovate livestock water points.

In 2009, the GEF initiated a new program to construct new wells and rehabilitate existing structures under the Mongolian National Livestock Program but progress has been slow with most investments being close to Ulaanbaatar. To date, only about 15% of the total number of livestock water points have been constructed or rehabilitated in the pastures. In addition, around 500 reservoirs and ponds with a capacity of 92,500m³ are used for livestock water supply.

### Support is needed to expand the coverage for water well construction and rehabilitation to reach underserved remote herder communities

In order to improve access to underutilized pastures and relieve pressure from overgrazing in areas where there are no functional livestock water points, it is necessary to construct new wells. Wells must be informed by climate change to avoid oversaturation of groundwater in areas which will become increasingly arid. In the western part of Mongolia, there are opportunities for better grazing land that remains underutilized. In the western Altai Mountain ranges and Great Lakes Valley, there are opportunities to better harvest grassland resources.

### Limited haymaking and storage impacting livestock health and survival

Limited haymaking and storage impacting livestock health and survival, resulting in significant economic loss to herders during winter/spring/autumn.

As a coping mechanism, some herder households allow for a larger herd. This approach has several set backs: 1) more animals against limited natural resources result in unhealthy animals and low quality livestock products; and 2) lack of hay storage results in the need to sell off livestock at lower prices during winter/spring/autumn.

The availability of forage decreases in August and the grass starts to dry from late August/September. After October, feed is deficient and the herders are forced to sell their livestock at low prices due to the lack of hay storage. The number of animals that die is increasing with corresponding increases in losses, particularly for subsistence herders who make up the majority of the population. This has a significant impact on the livelihoods of these communities as well as preventing animals from reaching and it causing high mortality among the livestock. The winter death of 1999, 2000 and 2003 resulted in the loss of over 5 million head of livestock with an estimated cost to herders of 40 million animals out of 43, at an estimated cost of more than USD $40 million. As a result of these two deaths, more than 200,000 people were affected.

### Policy, regulatory or market factors that contribute to increased number of livestock and thus pressure on land and water resources

Overpopulation of livestock degrades pasture and water resources resulting in substandard animal health and the sale of low quality livestock products. This has a negative impact on herders’ livelihoods as they are forced to develop markets and ensure reliable income for herders. Breaking this cycle and promoting sustainable pastoral management is critical to improving the livelihoods of herder communities, reducing poverty levels and to reducing vulnerability of herder communities.

* Government subsidies – public transfer payments and commercial credits tied to number of livestock owned

# D.6 Efficiency and effectiveness (max: 500 words, approximately 1 page)

This document outlines how the Green Climate Fund has been successfully piloted by a number of projects at varying scales.

## The project builds on best practices of cost-effectiveness and efficiency of delivery

Traditional cooperative approaches to addressing cross-sectoral challenges have not yet achieved significant scale. This project brings together key ministries, MET and MoAFL, acknowledging the link between natural resources and herder livelihoods and the need for cross sector collaboration, to maximize the impact of combined resources and efforts.

## Cost-effectiveness analysis

The cost-effectiveness analysis was conducted by O&M with a focus on the pilot phase. The project cost-share and co-funding were considered in conducting the economic analysis during project design. Given the costs and benefits associated with the pilot phase, the project is expected to be cost-effective over its lifetime.

## Financial feasibility

A financial feasibility study was conducted using a discounted cash flow (DCF) model at a 10% discount rate, with an internal rate of return (IRR) of 21%. Three sensitivity test cases were examined: (i) total cost decreases by 5%, (ii) total cost increases by 5%, and (iii) total cost decreases by 20%. In all three scenarios, the project remains economically feasible.

## Project timing

The project will also ensure cost-effectiveness through complementarity with related efforts. From May 2018, the Government of Mongolia started implementing a GCF readiness grant project entitled Building capacity to support climate change adaptation and mitigation in Mongolia. The project is expected to be completed by March 2020.

## Project areas

One such area is a policy review process through the readiness grant (1:2.5) to enable cross sector collaboration, including the livestock sector, for the development of a national land use plan. While the readiness grant will focus on national level planning and climate integration, the proposed UNDP project will address sub-national level planning and implementation.

## Project activities

The UNDP project will support the Government in developing a robust institutional framework for the GOMUSDNP livestock sector adaptation project with improved climate projection data and computing capacity. The project will also develop an agrohydro-geomorphology model as well as an updated groundwater database.

## Implementation

Through the readiness grant, adaptive measures will be prioritized with detailed cost-benefit analysis (3:2) with consideration of sector and geographical specifics, which will help validate UNDP interventions at landscape level. The project is expected to have a significant impact on the livestock sector and could be an important addition to the NAP financing plan and strategy resulting from the readiness grant.

## Conclusion

The UNDP project has the potential to provide technical assistance for climate change adaptation in Mongolia, as well as agrohydro-geomorphology, livestock sector and emergency management professionals.

The impacts of a dramatically warming climate are already being felt in Mongolia, where temperatures have risen by over 3°C over the past 70 years; average precipitation is declining; and extreme weather events are more frequent, posing challenges to its vast pastoralist and livestock production that are already fragile after decades of unsustainable herding practices. Accidents of nature and human actions have combined to create a vicious cycle: with less pasture to graze their animals, they lose their stock and income. And in turn, many herders compensate for the losses by increasing herd size, putting even more

### Gender Indicators in Mongolia

The Gender Inequality Index (GII) reflects gender-based inequalities in three dimensions: reproductive health, empowerment, and economic activity. Reproductive health is measured by maternal mortality and adolescent birth rates; empowerment is measured by the share of parliamentary positions held by women or the ratio of female to male parliamentarians; and economic activity is measured by the labour market participation of women and men. The GII can be interpreted as the loss of human development due to inequality between women and men's achievements in the three dimensions.

The Gender Development Index (GDI) is a composite index that measures gender equality, defined as a ratio of female-to-male HDI. The GDI measures gender inequalities in three basic dimensions of human development: reproductive health, empowerment, and economic activity. Reproductive health dimension (measured by expected years of schooling for children and mean years for men and women aged 25–49), empowerment dimension (measured by the share of parliamentary positions held by women or the ratio of female to male parliamentarians) and economic activity dimension (measured by the sex-disaggregated GNI per capita for women and men). The female HDI value for Mongolia is 0.744 in contrast with 0.723 for males, resulting in a GDI value of 1.068 among 180 countries in 2015.

The Global Gender Gap Report 2017 by the World Economic Forum (WEF) measures gender gaps between men and women in four categories: economic participation and opportunity, educational attainment, health survival and political empowerment. Mongolia ranked 53rd out of 144 countries in 2017 with the score of 0.689.

Although there are no specific and fact-based gender-related problems in water and soil management reported in any gender-related studies, there are common observations. ## Women’s double burden and transforming gender relations

Data from recent Participatory Living Standards Assessment of the NGO of Mongolia have identified that a disproportionate number of female-headed households are living in poverty and its proportion is growing. This phenomenon is closely related to women’s limited access to productive assets, which are often limited to engage in livelihood activities during their free time. Women who are employed or engaged in small enterprises need to work longer hours than men do, just to meet the tasks at home and in the workplace.

Specifically, while women who are involved in livestock sector carry double burden to work in productive as well as reproductive area all year long, the main characteristic mentioned in relation to Mongolian men’s work is seasonal with busy periods of work as well as rest. Women’s workload reaches its peak during the months of June and July, and again in September, October, November and December. The average daily workload of women during the year is 11.3 hours while the workload of men is 9.4 hours.

Women are also responsible for other aspects of household herding life, such as decision-making on major spending and purchases, household business management, participation in community activities and leadership.

## Women’s limited access to running small and medium enterprises (SMEs)

According to the SME Law (2010), SMEs are defined as regular business entities with 39 or fewer employees and an annual turnover of less than 5 million tugrik ($10,000 USD). The law also differentiates between sectors in terms of number of employees and annual turnover for a company to be considered small. For example, the maximum annual turnover is 250 million tugrik while the minimum annual turnover is 1-50,250 million. While women face higher barriers to loan access compared to husbands, they are fully responsible for financial risk in case of business failure. Further gender-based discrimination is also evident from the fact that women are subject to direct discrimination against certain groups of populations: such as women who are leading households, breadwinners, women with disability, senior women etc.

Sex disaggregated data on female ownership of SMEs at a commercial banks is currently not disclosed. ## Legal and Administrative Framework Protecting Women and Protecting Gender Equality

Mongolia has seen a national movement towards gender-sensitive climate change response guided by the Law of Mongolia on Enforcement of the Law of Promotion of Gender Equality (LGPE), the National Action Programme on Climate Change (NAPCC), the Environmental Sector Gender Strategy 2014-2030, Green Development Policy and Mongolia Sustainable Development Vision 2030.

The adoption of the LGPE and the revision of the Criminal Code led to the implementation domestic violence law. The LGPE requires that at least one-third of the members of committees nominated by a party or coalition shall be represented by women (Article 126.2) and the LGPE stated that representation of women in local government bodies should not less than 30 percent on national, 15 percent on regional, among the capital city levels, 20 percent on district, 25 percent on soum and 30 percent on village levels (Article 126.4). The LGPE also requires that women's representation in leading positions in public administration shall not be less than 15 percent among state secretaries and heads of agencies, 20 percent among managers in other central agencies, 30 percent among heads of departments and divisions on national level, 25 percent among heads of departments and divisions on regional levels and divisions on aimag, city, soum, duung and khoroos levels (Article 126.1). The LGPE introduces concept of gender equality in all areas of life including education, employment, health care, etc., through incorporation of gender equity concepts into laws, government policies, programs and projects.

The NAPCC, which was approved by the Government in 2000, highlights to ensure justice, transparency, human rights and gender equality are upheld in the implementation of the program.

The Environmental Sector Gender Strategy 2014-2030 gives gender-sensitive strategic guidelines for activities related to climate change. The strategy is based on the principle that women participate in particular through the objective 1.2, which aims "to facilitate introducing new content into the Bulganai National Park and other protected areas of Mongolia by involving schools, colleges, and universities on the subjects of social development, gender discourse, social impacts of climate change and natural disasters, etc." The strategy also states that a gender-sensitive approach to data collection and information packaging work be carried out jointly with the local community people on the climate change, desertification and disaster prevention by emphasizing gender roles of women and men that are related to these issues.

Even though the Green Development Policy does not make direct, explicit references to gender equality, the preamble of this policy emphasizes inclusiveness, social justice and better human well-being. These notions include gender equality as a key element in achieving sustainable development goals.

The Mongolia Sustainable Development Vision 2030 states that one of the principles for achieving the sustainable social development is to ensure gender equality in social development and create a pleasant environment for equal participation in social welfare.

A good practice of undertaking comprehensive analysis and developing recommendations, policies and measures to address gender inequalities related to climate change through UNEDD programme has been applied for the proposed project as has a social and environmental safeguards framework. In the context of Mongolia where women are more vulnerable than men in many aspects, it has been found that the poorest and most vulnerable, including elderly, disabled and women headed households are at the risk of becoming left behind, as the most active and better-off herders benefit more by participating in the project activities and receiving benefits from the project.

This also includes application of block chain technology in traceability of sustainable livelihoods and products, which is a good practice that can be applied by women more effectively as a group, not as individual households, to improve quality of their practices in animal husbandry and tangible resources.

The herder group model has been applied throughout the last 20 years at all development partner projects and there are proven advantages of joining the efforts to improve their livelihoods and overcoming economic and disaster shocks.
