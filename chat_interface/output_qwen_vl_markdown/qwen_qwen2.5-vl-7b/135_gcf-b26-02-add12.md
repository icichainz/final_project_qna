## Component 1 addresses technical barriers faced by manufacturing industries, mining and agriculture sectors by facilitating identification of high-impact climate technology projects. In addition, Component 1 addresses the lack of corporate capacity to conduct climate change planning, and supports the integration of climate considerations into business strategies, including risk management frameworks, target setting and investment planning.

## Component 2 introduces an innovative concessional financing mechanism to address financial gaps in the deployment of low-carbon technologies for a shift to lower carbon processes and operations, and in addition to promoting the uptake of corporate climate governance and management practices. The financing mechanism will be based on blended finance with a focus on leveraging private sector capital to support climate and corporate governance performance. To participate, corporates and projects will comply with eligibility criteria related to (i) sector; (ii) behavioral change; (iii) technology; and (iv) impact potential.

Strategic investments in the energy, transport, industry, agriculture, and land use sectors are being developed in advanced markets, and involve borrowing terms pre-defined sustainability key performance indicators (KPIs). The blended finance mechanism will be based on a combination of grants and loans, and SNL or PBF climate instruments are available for the industrial, agriculture and mining sectors in the participating countries. These investments will support the development of sector-specific roadmaps at the sectoral level to create an enabling environment for climate-related investments by supporting the development of sector-level decarbonisation roadmaps. These roadmaps encompass technical pathways, policy frameworks, and financial mechanisms that are required to achieve the targeted emissions reductions.

The Programme will provide total financing of USD 10 billion (including USD 9.5 billion with USD 250 million from the GCF), of which USD 3.55 billion is grant funding from the GCF. Alongside concessional financing from the GCF, the EBRD and other co-financing will offer USD 757.5 million to corporates through loans. The EBRD will provide these loans from its own capital resources for specific, convened projects. In addition, the Programme will support the development of a blended finance facility with an initial USD of $1.360 million. Support from the GCF will innovate an innovative funding mechanism that is not currently available in the market and will enable the EBRD to provide concessional financing to corporates that enable energy-intensive industrial sectors to shift to a low-carbon gender-responsive pathway. The EBRD has extensive experience working with industrial, agriculture and mining corporates, and has offices in all of the participating countries, providing access to local and national stakeholders.

Overall, the Programme is expected to reduce emissions related to GCF results area (buildings, cities, industry) by approximately 15.489M tCO2 (reduced over 20-year asset lifetime), and GCF results area as "energy access and power generation" by 1.722HM tCO2 (reduced over 20-year asset lifetime). Context: The corporate sector

7\. The corporate sector is critical to delivering global climate change mitigation goals. As noted in an Energy Transitions Commission report*, it is essential that energy and industrial systems achieve net-zero CO₂ emissions within themselves to reach a fully decarbonised economy. This report also emphasizes the need to reduce emissions from hard-to-abate sectors (e.g., aviation, shipping, cement, steel and chemicals). The private sector is integral to achieving the investment volume required in the low-carbon transition and will be key to scaling up climate action at scale. It is therefore important to engage and support countries in the implementation of their respective national climate change agendas.

8\. While there are some encouraging signs that the corporate sector is beginning to take action, across the Countries sectors and corporates lack well-developed approaches and guidelines on how to address climate change mitigation and adaptation. The private sector has not yet developed a comprehensive approach for aligning with the Paris Agreement. In particular, sector-specific low-carbon roadmaps are required to induce and support the needed change in management practices and technologies deployed and to facilitate the respective investments; and to ensure that these changes are aligned with the countries’ national climate change strategies. This is an important consideration in green technology investments to meet sustainability and the countries' ability to give a strong signal of their commitment to the Paris Agreement, which will be critical for attracting private sector investment.

9\. As the impacts of climate change become visible and the severity and disruptive nature of future threats becomes clearer, financial institutions and corporates have started to assess climate risk in the context of their business strategies. The increasing prominence of the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) is a key driver for companies to publicly provide climate-related information supporting the shift to a low-carbon pathway for companies in advanced economies, as evidenced by the increasing prominence of the TCFD recommendations and the growing number of companies that have adopted them. However, regulators request more transparency to understand and respond to the potential risks that climate change poses to their sectors. This is particularly true for emerging markets where the impacts of climate change are continuing to grow, changing the shape of traditional industries and value chains.

10\. The private sector can play a critical role in achieving climate goals in a gender-responsive way that requires that manufacturing industries, agribusinesses and mining companies take responsibility and accountability for their environmental impact. This will require a shift from a compliance-based governance (CCG) practices to a transformative approach. A transformative shift by the corporate sector to a low-carbon pathway within a sector will be facilitated through a two-step approach: step 1 – uptake of high climate impact technologies, and step 2 – adoption of climate change targets and climate governance principles are incorporated into strategic decision making (see Figure 1).

## Paradigm shift potential of technology and behavioural changes

The innovation transition towards a low-carbon economy can be accelerated through a two-step approach: shift 1 – uptake of high climate impact technologies; and shift 2 – adoption of new business models, policies, and governance structures that deliver change targets and climate governance principles are incorporated into strategic decision making.

### Barriers addressed by the Programme

1. The innovation transition towards corporate sector can only be achieved by (i) accelerating investments in climate technologies paired with the introduction of production processes and management practices; and innovative and sustainable business models (e.g., integrated recycling, new low carbon products, recyclable packaging, etc.). These require a significant increase in public and private investment. The key financial, technological, capacity, policy and knowledge barriers as well as gender biases need to be overcome through industry, agribusiness, and mining sector potentials to deliver relevant benefits.
2. Critical financial barriers to the transition:
   - Limited access to corporate funding. Long-term financing from commercial banks in the countries due to a timeframe for capacity building related to energy efficiency and renewable energy markets and uncertainty about performance. Banks may also lack the relevant expertise to assess projects.
   - Early-mover costs/disadvantages: Early adopters of new technologies face unaffordable or expensive times of funding for financing. Transaction costs of developing these investments are usually high as such costs can be reduced by the scale of investment. The cost of early adoption and implementation costs can be increased by the lack of adequate familiarity and experience to identify and prepare projects both technically and financially.
   - No commercial funding that links price to sustainability/corporate governance issues: While instruments for financing climate change mitigation and adaptation are being developed in advanced markets, these instruments are concentrated in the European debt market; and no instruments that involve borrowers meeting pre-defined climate and sustainability KPIs are available for the industrial, agriculture and mining sectors in the participating Countries. Local commercial banks do

### Climate-related policy barriers exist

1. Countries may have identified impacts and visions through NDCs but have not translated these into national level action plans and guidance on how to achieve them.
2. There is a lack of clear sectoral-level policy actions (to be pursued over a 5- to 10-year time frame) based on analysis of main challenges, opportunities, and constraints in the sectors, as well as on existing strategies and plans that exist that can be used as models (e.g., the development of the policy roadmap for the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia).

To overcome the barriers noted in section B.1 and thereby enable a regional paradigm shift unlocking the potential of high-impact climate change mitigation technologies, the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development (EBRD) is seeking to partner with the GCF to provide the financing and tools needed to effectively and sustainably address cross-sectoral challenges. ## I. Assessment of the independent Technical Advisory Panel

### 1.1 Impact potential - Scale: Medium

#### 1.1.1 Global energy consumption in the industry sector is the highest among all end-use sectors.

- According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), the industrial sector accounted for 37 per cent of total final energy use in 2017.
- Growth in energy consumption has been driven by an ongoing increase in demand from industries such as chemicals, iron and steel, cement, pulp and paper, and aluminium production.

#### The sector’s energy intensity (energy consumed per unit of output) is decreasing at a rate faster than the global average for all sectors of energy.

- The share of fossil fuels in the mix decreased from 73 per cent to 70 per cent since 2009 but was due to an increase in the amount of electricity in non-energy-intensive subsectors.
- The industry sector is therefore critical to delivering global climate change mitigation goals. Many countries have set targets for reducing emissions from the energy and materials-intensive sectors (excluding fossil fuel sources) and have significant potential to deliver decarbonization of the global economy.

#### Energy-and materials-efficiency measures, together with deployment of low-carbon technologies, are expected to be a key driver in achieving cumulative emission reductions in the industry sector by 2030. According to IEA and the International Energy Agency (IEA), energy efficiency improvements will account for one-third of the dominant method of achieving the cumulative carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions reductions that can be achieved by 2050.

#### However, the businesses (corporates) in the industry sector in the seven participating countries face barriers to the uptake of climate change technologies. Financial barriers include:

- The above-average cost of low-carbon technologies.
- High transaction and upfront investment costs associated with the deployment of low-carbon technologies.
- Lack of funding available to support climate change related corporate governance initiatives.

#### Non-financial barriers include a lack of knowledge about the availability of climate impact financing, as well as a lack of awareness among industry leaders, investors, corporates and policymakers to make well-informed decisions. The governments of the participating countries need to address these barriers by providing incentives for the deployment of low-carbon industrial needs to promote low-carbon technologies. Nevertheless, strategies – including action plans to achieve the targets - remain underdeveloped.

#### The project will focus on energy-intensive industries, such as (non-fossil) energy mining companies, agribusinesses and agribusiness value chains (and related logistics) in the participating countries. # Jordan gender profile

The Constitution of 1982 (and last amended in 2016) enshrines the principle of equality and prohibits discrimination on the grounds of race, language, and religion, but not on the grounds of sex (Constitution of the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan, 2016: Article 6(1)).

## Female participation in the labour force

In Jordan, women's labour force participation rate is lower than men's (12.5%) compared to the SEMED average (22.9%). Despite advances in women's educational attainment and health, labour force participation rates remain low for women across all age groups. Only 17% of working women, compared to 28% of working men earn their salaries in the informal economy or more than 50% of women work part-time (CEDAW, 2010). Women are poorly represented in energy-intensive sectors: more than half of Jordan's electricity generation is for educational and public administration (DS, 2017). They concentrate on lower and intermediate hierarchical echelons (World Bank, 2014).

## Barriers to economic opportunities for women

Women still face important barriers to participate in the labour market. Despite having substantial education levels, Jordanian women continue to have one of the world's lowest rates of women's workforce participation (12.6%) (ILO, ILOSTAT, 2014). The existence of discriminatory laws, in particular, in the Labour Code, the Family Protection Act, the Personal Status Law, and the Child Protection Act, continue to limit women's empowerment opportunities (CEDAW, 2017). When it comes to education, while primary education is near-universal, a gender gap manifests itself on higher educational levels. 94% of girls are enrolled in secondary school compared with only 85% of boys (World Bank, WDI, 2014).

In addition, Jordanian women face a number of obstacles to accessing finance, including discriminatory attitudes from banks. This manifests in lower collateral requirements for men and the requirement of spousal guarantees for women, but not for men (World Bank, 2013). Women are also less likely than men to own property or have access to land titles. They are only half as likely as men to own an account at a financial institution (World Bank, FinStat, 2014). Women represented only 17% of clients in savings in 2012 (World Bank, 2015).

Moreover, women face barriers in accessing services, and this curtails their participation in education and employment. In particular, women lack access to affordable and adequate public transport. The situation is particularly acute in rural areas and other regions still persist in terms of transportation connectivity (World Bank, 2016a).

1.3 Kazakhstan gender profile

### 1.3.1 Female participation in the labour force

In Kazakhstan, women still face important barriers to participate in the labour market: Men's share of employment is 78%, compared to women's at 69% (WDI, 2017). 66% of women and 77% of men participate in the labour market. 5% of women are unemployed compared to 3% of men. Women are not allowed to perform 299 jobs in areas such as mining, metalworking, construction and factories (Government of the Republic of Kazakhstan, 2016).

Due to the lack of access to free and affordable preschool establishments is limited. According to a recent report, rural women encounter greater barriers in accessing education than their urban counterparts.

When it comes to access to finance, women in Kazakhstan have limited opportunities to own land or other assets. Only 3% of women are considered financially literate (ADB, 2013a). # 1.4 Morocco gender profile

Moroccan laws are primarily based on French civil law and Islamic Sharia law (UNICEF, 2011) and men and women are equal before the law (Art. 6 of the Constitution).

## 1.4.1 Female participation in the labour force

Women's low level of labour force participation in Morocco (25%) is characterised by high levels of unemployment, informality and low levels of education compared to men (ILO, 2014). Women are more likely than men to be unemployed or inactive: only 37% of women are in formal employment (Morikawa, 2015). Despite progress to ensure gender equality in the labour market, women face significant barriers to equal participation against women, domestic violence or sexual harassment; women legally inherit less than men, and women are barred from undertaking certain jobs that pose "risks of excessive danger," like "mining, construction, fishing, etc." (UNICEF, 2011).

Rural women, in particular, face multiple discrimination including poor and limited access to transportation, health services, schools and employment opportunities. Rural women are more likely than urban women to be illiterate or have low levels of education: from 45% to 60% of urban women), face higher rates of maternal mortality (ADB, 2016) and less likely to be in a stable union (ILO, 2014).

## 1.4.2 Barriers to economic opportunities for women

While there is no substantial gender difference in primary level education enrolment, a gender gap is visible in secondary education. In 2012, 59% of men compared to only 53% of women were enrolled at the secondary level (UNICEF, 2014). Women are also underrepresented in tertiary education: 26% of men and 28% of women in 2015) (World Bank, WDI, 2012).

There is a large gender gap in Morocco's labour force: 73% of men are in the labour force, compared to only 49% for women. This gap has remained relatively stable over the past twenty years (ILO, ILOSTAT, 2014).

Women predominantly work in low-skilled, vulnerable positions: 47% of working women are contributing family members and only 35% of them have a job title. Working women devote 5 hours daily to care work while men only spend 0.7 hours on these tasks (ILO). Thirty percent of women work part-time compared with 12% for men, whereas centres for children under the age of 6 are privately run and concentrated in urban areas.

The exclusion of women in the workplace has a significant financial impact over the course of their life: only 30.5% of working women have access to social security; women do not receive a retirement pension and 83.7% have no health coverage (CNHD, 2015).

Overall, many gender gaps remain:

- Only 27% of women have access to a formal financial institution compared to 60% of men.
- Only 4.3% of firms have a female top manager.

# 1.5 Serbia gender profile

The Constitution guarantees the principle of gender equality and explicitly prohibits indirect and direct discrimination (Constitution 2006, Art. 15, 2(3)). There are no restrictions on women entering certain professions and they may work the same night hours as men (World Bank, 2018).

## 1.5.1 Female participation in the labour force

With 45% of women participating in the labour market, compared to 62% of men, female labour force participation is lower than that of men. This difference can be influenced by the fact that women spend an average of 5 hours on unpaid care work per day, which is double the time spent by men (UN, n.d.). The lack of access to childcare institutions negatively affects the ability of women to work in comparison to men (World Bank, SIFRU 2016).

In addition, women's earnings are 17% lower than men's and a horizontal segregation is most pronounced in the health care sector which employs 13% of working Serbian woman and 2% of men. In contrast, the construction sector employs 25% of working women, as compared to 45% of men (Revs, 2012).

## 1.5.2 Barriers to economic opportunity for women

There are significant disparities in education: in primary (96% of boys and girls) and secondary education (95% of girls and 93% of boys). However, 67% of women as compared to only 50% of men pursue tertiary education (World Bank, WDI, 2015).

Although men own more land than women there is significant discrepancy between genders in terms of women's land ownership (FAO, 2015). While 51% of property owners are women in the North of Serbia, only 13% of women own private property in Preslav (FAO 2015).

Despite their lower levels of education and fewer opportunities for entrepreneurship, 83% of both women and men have a bank account (World Bank, FinDev, 2014) but only 26% of SMEs are owned by women (Kalinec et al., 2014).

Roma women face multiple discrimination on the basis of sex and ethnicity, with greater challenges in terms of education, health and economic participation opportunities (CEDAW, 2013). Roma girls have lower educational attainment and higher dropout rates than Roma boys.

1.6 Tunisia gender profile

In the Tunisian Republic, men and women are equal before the law (Constitution of the Tunisian Republic, 2014). In addition, Article 46 indicates a commitment to eradicating all forms of violence against women and protection of women's rights in all domains.

### 1.6.1 Female participation in the labour force

In Tunisia, large labor gender gaps persist. While 43% of women, compared to 26% of men, are economically active (ILO, ISTAT, 2013), female employment rate has increased from 70% of men participate in the labour force (ILO, ILOSTAT, 2013). In addition, a gender wage gap exists. The average monthly salary for women is 859 dinars compared to 1460 dinars in 2012 and in 2011 respectively (INS, 2015b). Contributing factors to this large gap in labour market participation include for instance that women spend on average 5.3 hours on care work a day compared with men who spend only 4.7 hours per day (World Bank, 2016). Access to public childcare services is very limited. Moreover, women's access to employment is restricted by Labour Code provisions that prohibit women from working in mines or with hazardous materials and in jobs involving heavy physical work. Women's access to education is also limited: the average age of girls leaving school is 15 years compared to 18 for boys (UNICEF, 2016). In addition, women's access to employment is restricted from 10 a.m., to 6 p.m. (Code du Travail, 2010). In addition, 91% of women indicated that they had been physically assaulted by a man in public transportation (CREDIF, 2016).

### 1.6.2 Education

There is a substantial gender gap in literacy rates in Tunisia. While close to 90% of men are literate, the same holds only for 73% of women. 97% of girls and 98% of boys go to primary school at least once compared with 65% of girls and 72% of boys who complete education. 43% of women compared to 26% of men enjoy tertiary education (World Bank, WDI, 2015).

Concerning access to finance, 34% of Tunisian men and 21% of Tunisian women hold a bank account at a financial institution (World Bank, WDI, 2015). According to a report by the French Development Agency (AFD), Tunisia is one of the few countries in Africa where progress for women working in the informal sector, as well as for rural women (AFD, 2016). This might well be due to the fact that women have historically had less access to land and therefore equal access to land ownership to both sexes. In practice women frequently transfer their entitlements to their husbands in the case of marriage and to their brothers in the case of succession. Women's inheritance rights are also limited: they inherit only one-third of the amount due to half the amount of sons, while female surviving spouses are entitled half the amount of male surviving spouses (Code du Statut Personnel 2012 Articles 103, 101-102). It has been noted that women's access to property rights is also limited by cultural and social norms due to discriminatory practices that favour men, as well as the desire to keep property within the family (AFD, 2016).

In terms of female firm ownership and managerial responsibility, only 8.5% of firms have a female top manager while women participate in the ownership of 50% of firms (World Bank, Enterprise Surveys, 2013).

1.7 Uzbekistan gender profile

The Constitution of the Republic of Uzbekistan (1992) establishes the principle of equality between men and women (Art. 46) and provides protection from discrimination on the grounds of sex (Art. 18).

### 1.7.1 Female participation in the labour force

In Uzbekistan, women's labour force participation is very low compared to the Central Asian regional average. Women are less likely than men to be economically active. There is a small gender gap with 44% of men being economically active and only 36% of women (World Bank, ILO). In addition, women face discrimination in certain high impact industries (mining, construction, and metalworking) which affect women's access to employment opportunities (Government of Uzbekistan, Art. 229; Order No. 865 of 5 January 2000). There is no legislation prohibiting and criminalising sexual harassment in the workplace (CEDAW, 2014).

#### 1.7.2 Equal access to education opportunities for women

While higher education attainment rates are overall low, women have slightly less access than men do: Literacy is near-universal for both women and men (World Bank, WDI; 2015). Women's gross enrolment in secondary education is 93% compared with 97% for men. In contrast, the challenge lies in tertiary education, where gross enrolment rates are very low: 7% for women and 11% for men.

Women more so than men are restricted in their labour market access. There is a declining trend of female employment; however, rates are significantly higher than the regional average. 8.65% of women are unemployed compared to 4.39% in the region, whereas 6.01% of men are unemployed as opposed to 2.75% in the region (World Bank, WDI). Moreover, Uzbekistan has a gender pay gap of 36% (ADB, 2014).

In 2013, women's share of employment was only 18%, compared with men's entrepreneurship, compared to a 12% average within Europe and Central Asia (World Bank, Enterprise Surveys, 2013). Most female entrepreneurs in Uzbekistan are engaged in small-scale businesses such as food stalls, retail trade, and professional services such as education and healthcare (ADB, 2014). In addition, 4% of the female population is employed in agriculture compared to men's 7%. This is two times higher than the regional average, which is 2% (Walk Free Foundation, 2016).

Women face challenges in their access to finance. Access to bank accounts for both women and men in Uzbekistan is higher than the Central Asian regional average: 39% of women have a bank account at a financial institution compared to 31% of women in Central Asia, which is significantly lower than the regional average (World Bank, WDI; World Bank, Findeks, 2014). However, the number of women and men obtaining loans in Uzbekistan is very low: only 5.8% of women obtain a loan compared to 7.3% for men which is a regional average of 7.3% of women and 8.3% of men (World Bank, Findeks, 2014). According to the Asian Development Bank, in practice very few women own land or real estate (ADB, 2014) which exacerbates the restricted access to finance.

# Gender strategy in High Impact Programme for the Corporate Sector

EBRD’s mandate in line with its private sector approach in terms of gender and economic inclusion is enhancing equal access to economic opportunities for women and men. EBRD's Gender & Economic Inclusion team have developed a number of gender approaches for green climate finance, including (1) promoting gender equality as an objective; (2) access to finance and entrepreneurship; (3) access to infrastructure and services that mainly put the EBRD’s client at the core of the activities.

The design of these strategies is based on the analysis of the gender profiles of the High Impact Programme's target countries and the relevant indicators, and on EBRD’s unique private sector approach and experience working with the GCF on previous programmes. The aim is to mainstream gender equality in all aspects of EBRD’s operations to ensure equal economic opportunities for both men and women in these high impact sectors, with EBRD’s clients as the main focus.

The gender activities will contribute ultimately to gender equality and the empowerment of women in the target countries by addressing the following gender inequalities:

a. Access to employment and income: Women have limited access to employment in high impact sectors, hence equal access to decision - making and planning on low-carbon strategies.
b. Women are still underrepresented in the workforce, specifically in the energy-intensive sectors and even more so in management positions in the target countries.
c. Low labour force participation as well overrepresentation in low-skilled poorly paid, part-time jobs, lack of access to education and training, or lack of education or persisting gender biases create a gender pay gap across all targeted countries.
d. Women continue to experience gender-based violence and sexual harassment in the workplace.

These gender inequalities will be addressed by specific gender activities in collaboration with clients in high impact sectors as per EBRD approach to leverage the private sector’s opportunity for sustainable development.

Under Component 1 (High impact technologies identification and corporate low-carbon strategy development), corporate low-carbon strategy development that considers gender as a driver of innovation, competitiveness and sustainability is an important element. Similarly, a gender action plan at the corporate level will equip key staff with the ability to recruit, promote and retain female staff while ensuring a safe work environment that promotes equal opportunities for all.
