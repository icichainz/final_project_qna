### Rwanda is among the highly vulnerable countries to climate change both in terms of mitigation and adaptation.
Climate change has led to more frequent and intense rainfall, seasonal temperature shifts, droughts, and landslides have become increasingly common** and extremely difficult to manage with serious consequences for agriculture, health and livelihoods. The country faces a range of challenges including the spread of vector-borne diseases, increased frequency of extreme weather events, changes in crop yields, loss of biodiversity, desertification, soil erosion, water scarcity, forest degradation, land use change, coastal flooding, reduced agricultural productivity, increased food insecurity, and other socio-economic impacts. To tackle this challenge, Rwanda has set ambitious climate and development goals to achieve low-carbon emission while creating economic social impact and job opportunities.

The Government of Rwanda is pursuing several measures for low-carbon and climate-resilient growth and development in collaboration with the national partners and international community under the Paris Agreement’s updated Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) 2020 have identified adaptation and mitigation measures presented in the Green Rwanda Action Plan. These measures include electricity generation, energy efficiency, water management, land use change, forest conservation, agriculture, biodiversity, waste management and soil management. The RGIF is specifically designed to mobilize the financial resources required for implementation of these NDCs. It will focus on three priority sectors of the NDC and contribute to the implementation and realization of the NDC targets.

Furthermore, as the country looks towards what will be needed for Rwanda to address the unprecedented challenges posed by climate change, it is important that the Government of Rwanda has a clear roadmap and relief to what will be needed in the medium-to-longer term to build resilience and re-grow Rwanda’s developing economy. The role of catalytic innovative finance capacity to support critical infrastructure and social sectors is therefore crucial.

The RGIF project aims to strengthen Rwanda's ownership of climate finance and address market challenges to facilitate access to climate finance for projects in priority sectors. Specifically, RGIF will be composed of two windows working in an integrated approach: 1) The Project Preparation Facility at the Rwanda Green Fund (FONERWA) offering grants and reimbursable grants to support project preparation and implementation; and 2) The Climate Change Credit Facility (CCCF) at the BRD offering direct loans for the eligible pipeline.

Additionally, the RGIF project activities will include strengthening the institutional capacity of the FONERWA and BRD to upgrade and become the co-executors of the RGIF and have the mandate, structure, functions, investment focus, financial instruments and systems, strategic and business plan, operational systems, and reporting mechanisms in place.

This will include for BRD as the manager of the RGIF Credit Facility, project origination and screening credit analysis, deal structuring and credit approval (investment), risk analysis and management, portfolio monitoring and reporting, and transaction execution. It also includes providing technical assistance to the market (project developers and local banks included) and advisory services for bringing the pipeline projects to a stage of readiness.

The expected reduction in carbon emissions based on current pipeline and very conservative estimates is about [0.92 million] tonnes CO2eq over a 25-year lifetime across 4 sectors in the near term: renewable energy, agriculture, waste management, and water management. The RGIF will also contribute to improving livelihoods for 31,600 households and will ensure women have equal access to the facility finally. RGIF programme will also enhance Rwanda's capacity to mobilize green investments and upgrade local capacity to provide green finance and support national green development.

Although Rwanda is located within the equatorial belt, its climate is not typical of tropical climates. The country's climate is defined by humid conditions including rainy forest and dry savanna regions. The eastern part of the country is generally of semi-arid to arid nature with a strong influence from the rainy shadow of the western highlands.

The rainfall is 5% characterized by a large seasonal variation, which is attributed to varied topography and existence of large water bodies near and within the country. In general, two rainy seasons are experienced in Rwanda: the short rainy season (May-June) and long rainy season (July-August). The dry season occurs from December to March.

The rainfall patterns are largely controlled by the position and intensity of anticyclones such as Mascuranes, Saint Helens, Agapes and Siberian. Spatial distribution of both observed and modelled climatological data shows that most parts of Rwanda, although the show overestimation in the west and underestimation in the north-east region of the country. The average annual rainfall can range from 100 mm to more than 2500mm (Figure B.1.3.a-d) and distributed differently across the four main climatic zones, namely, eastern plains, central plateaus, highlands, and regions around Lake Kivu, while temperatures range between 11°-28°C across the climatic regions (Figure B.1.3.b-e-f).

The investment criteria of the RGIF are: 1) projects with adaptation benefits; 2) projects with co-benefits across sectors: energy, green buildings/green urbanization, clean transportation, and climate smart agriculture. Several of these sectors have overlapping benefits: for example in climate smart agriculture: efficient drip irrigation systems that use solar energy have both a mitigation (reduced GHG emissions) and adaptation benefit (increased resilience to drought and other impacts). Similarly, green buildings/green urbanization have both a mitigation (reduced energy consumption/CO2 emissions) and adaptation (improved resilience to storms) impact. The sectors are described in more detail in the bullets below.

6. Energy sector

The Government of Rwanda’s plan to mitigate GHG emissions in the energy sector focuses on a significant increase in renewable power generation, a sharp decrease in the use of biomass as a primary energy source and an overall reduction in the share of fossil fuels (coal, oil and natural gas) which are one of the primary sources of air pollution in Rwanda). Additionally, Rwanda plans to invest in energy efficiency and renewable-energy in industries as well as in commercial and residential buildings.

## B

**Rwanda has been growing with an average WtGDP growth rate for the last 10 years**, which has resulted in higher demands for energy services. Energy and waste sectors are the major contributors to total GHG emissions in Rwanda. From 2004 to 2015, the sector has seen an annual increase of about 6% estimated GHG emissions per year. The most significant contributor to this growth is the energy sector. **The largest share** that contributed the most to GHG emissions include biomass* (fuel wood and charcoal), and fossil fuel for road transport. In addition, the urban population in Rwanda has increased from 10 million people in 2004 to 15 million in 2019. The area under urban areas is also increasing with 3.2% urban population growth in 2019* from a low base. Under a business-as-usual scenario, Rwanda's emissions are forecast to more than double between 2015 and 2030 (from 87 million tCO2eq to 164 million tCO2eq). This is due in part to the increase in population from 10.9 million people in 2015 to 12.5 million by 2030, increasing energy demand and road transport.* The Rwanda's transport sector for example, driven by increasing urbanization and economic growth, is expected to increase its emissions from 0.53 million tCO2eq in 2012 to 3.1 million tCO2eq in 2030*. Additionally, regarding Energy Efficiency in buildings (heating, cooling, lighting, cooking, etc.), the country has a potential for energy savings of about six times from 2012 CO2 emission of 2,818,690 GgCO2-eq to 457,300 GgCO2-eq by 2050.

### 7. Electricity access

Under the electricity mitigation scenario, renewable power generation sources will largely come from hydro and PV plants. Based on current mitigation plans, it is expected that hydropower will provide 48.13% of the total energy mix in 2050. This will need significant investments in renewable energy infrastructure to meet future demand trends especially in the deep grid network.* As a result of the target mitigation measures, the country expects a cumulative emission reduction of 20.7/19.8 Gg CO2-eq by 2050 compared to business as usual.

### B. Energy Efficiency and Green Cities

Energy efficiency measures such as efficient lighting, efficient (heat) heating systems, and energy-efficient appliances are expected to result in a cumulative GHG emissions reduction of 36,919.79 Gg CO2-eq from 2012 to 2050. The new law* setting up an minimum green compliance for new public and commercial buildings is expected to reduce the energy consumption by 40% compared with current efficiency. Rwanda's Green City project also focuses on energy efficiency with resiliency components such as smart grid, solar power, and electric vehicles.* As a result of the target mitigation measures, the country is projected to have a cumulative GHG reduction impact of approximately 894.75 Gg CO2-eq by 2050 (compared to 2012 emissions for the sector estimated at 1,178.0 GgCO2-eq). Rwanda's Green City project is also expected to reduce energy consumption in urban areas and populations in major cities like Kigali. RGF plans to offer co-financing of selected projects with clear investment criteria and environmental social risk policies to ensure that projects are adhering to best practices.

## Agriculture

Agriculture provides about 33% of Rwanda's GDP, 89% of its employment, and 65% of its export revenues (mostly tea and coffee). However, steep terrain, limited land, and a lack of modern technology pose serious constraints on agricultural productivity. The country is also facing increasing demand for household energy needs (used by 80% of the population), and increasing urbanization create significant pressure on natural resources, notably land, water, and forests. Over 60% of households subsist on rainfed agriculture, which is highly vulnerable to climate change. Rwanda's agricultural sector has grown more than 10% per year which results in high levels of erosion and surface runoff into waterways. Erosion causes soil loss and sedimentation in rivers and lakes that transform the country’s rivers from clear to muddy, costing $40 million or, about 2% of GDP equivalent.

Rwanda's dependence on traditional rain-fed agriculture makes it vulnerable to changes in rainfall and climate change. The combination of these factors can have a negative effect on steep slopes. Increased intensity of rainfall can cause flash floods and landslides which are exacerbated by erosion (caused by agricultural activities) and deforestation. In addition, the country's dependence on irrigation for rice production has a significant impact on agricultural production (hence food security), infrastructure, and electricity generation.

Rwanda is expecting a climate-related decrease in mean rainfall amounts and the number of rainy days in regions like the south-eastern lowlands and central plateaus, which already have a limited water supply as they are located at lower altitudes. The average number of rainy days per year (150-200) and rainy days is projected by mid-century (2041-2070) in most parts of the country with the western region experiencing an increase in rainfall intensity. This will lead to a decrease in mean annual runoff, evaporation, and a shorter crop growing period. Additionally, the expected increase in the number of very wet days and maximum consecutive 1-day rainfall (Figure B.1A.b., c & d) in the north-west highlands and south-western lowlands will cause flooding which could lead to soil erosion and landslides in steep slopes areas which might in turn lead to the destruction of existing infrastructure and impact other human activities. Hence, there is a clear need to implement climate adaptation and mitigation measures in the agriculture sector.

## Background and Context

Over the last decade, Rwanda has experienced flooding events affecting mostly the Northern, Western, Southern and Central parts of the country. ## Barriers to Risks
To achieve these increased levels of investment, new interventions are needed to address the specific barriers and risks that hold back the local Rwandan market. Rwanda is a small country with a small banking system and a limited group of local project developers. Many local development projects are too small for international banks or investors to consider as equity or debt. While some international project developers can raise money internationally (in foreign currency), they often do not have the expertise in local markets, which makes it difficult to structure deals that will be attractive to local banks and investors. Lack of experience with local commercial banks can lead to poor risk assessment, higher costs of debt, and high bilateral requirements. Furthermore, local banks often do not have deep expertise in project finance, and instead rely on foreign consultants or advisors for this type of financing. This is a significant barrier for local green projects, which need affordable tailored financing solutions (including long-term debt) to make the economics work for end-customers.

### Project Activities
Based on the RGF feasibility study and design work, the RGF has been developed as an integrated program addressing specific barriers of the Rwandan market. The RGF takes an integrated program approach, offering both grants and financing support to eligible projects across a wide range of development timelines. Refer to Section 8.4 for the full integrated implementation framework.

### Grants (under the PPF managed by FONERIVI)
Grants are used to address the following local market issues:
- Lack of perceived "bankability" of projects, and lack equity capital for nascent developers looking to scale up: The PPF will offer grants and reimbursable grants to cover early-stage project activities to meet collateral requirements. These include feasibility studies, environmental impact assessments, studies, securing permits and lease agreements, financial models, and contract development, etc.
- High collateral requirements of local banks: The PPF will offer reimbursable grants to serve as partial collateral for projects that are bankable but have high collateral requirements. This nature can significantly constrain the finances of developer companies and can limit their ability to grow their business. By providing a grant to cover part of the collateral, it allows developers to focus on raising finance for more projects, and further grow their customer (beneficiary) base.
- While also further proving the viability of green projects in the local market: The PPF will only provide partial grants to projects that are bankable but have high collateral requirements. This is done to ensure that local developers have sufficient skin in the game. The AE had discussions with the EE on mandatory equity requirements for projects, and it was agreed that this would not be appropriate because the focus is not on sharing concerns but comfort for investors with the collateral.
- In addition, a guarantee instrument would mean the Government of Rwanda pays a guarantee fee from the onset, which would result in charging a higher fee to the sub-projects to cover the entire instrument. This would make the project less attractive and more expensive, making it less likely that the project becomes viable, more prohibitive, and therefore not yield the intended results.

### 53. RGF Project Preparation Component executed by FONERWA

The Project Preparation Facility (PPF) is a new component created jointly with FONERWA that provides reimbursable and non-reimbursable grants to support early-stage project development and potential deals deemed fit for RGF’s eligible projects. The purpose of the PPF is to help promote transition from feasibility to full RGF-ready projects, which are then eligible for funding under the main arm of the RGF. With grant money often in limited supply, PPF funds are intended to provide "catalytic" funding at an early stage of project development. These grants will be provided on a reimbursable basis and are not intended as seed capital or equity investment.

PPF support is intended for feasibility studies, technical studies, legal and contract support, business plan refinement, and other activities that help move projects forward to the RGF-ready stage. PPF support will prioritize early-stage, high-impact, innovative projects and businesses that have a high probability of successful completion based on developer track record and sound business plans. That is, PPF grants are not intended for seed capital or equity investment in projects. In general, no equity should be used to make these projects bankable.

## High cost of capital, negatively affecting project economics:

High risk perception, as well as prevailing high interest rates in the country, result in high cost of capital for green projects, or outright inability to access finance. Prevailing interest rates are high in Rwanda, especially for new sectors and technologies. The current 10-year government bond yield is 9.5% (see para 28). The 3-month T-bill rate is 16% and the GCF concessionally allows the lending rate to be reduced to 11%. (See para 29) There are also other costs associated with accessing finance, such as transaction fees, which have a disproportionately negative effect on green projects. Higher cost of capital and shorter tenors make project-level economics challenging for the end-users/beneficiaries (e.g., higher cost of electricity required to power a solar pump). The high interest rate is further exacerbated when the GCF concessionally is used to enable projects that otherwise struggle to access finance, and to enable projects to generate revenue to commercialize new technologies.

### Small size of projects limits access to finance:

Non-utility-scale green projects are often "distributed" or "light touch" projects. These include small solar PV systems, small scale irrigation projects, and irrigation PV and pumping equipment, etc. The small size of these projects makes them difficult to finance by the local commercial banks. Small and large projects can have similar loan processing and underwriting requirements, but small projects are more challenging for lenders because they cannot project their revenue on a per-project basis.

### Lack of equity capital:

Rwanda's green sector is small and nascent, with few well-financed international developers. The local financial system is also small, with limited access to green sectors such as energy efficiency, irrigation and non-utility scale energy, including PV and SWH. The local financial system is also small, meaning that most projects are not able to raise equity capital from the private sector. Without available equity conditions local project developers: capital that would go towards things like feasibility studies, environmental studies, engineering studies, site control and lease agreements, legal reviews and other pre-construction costs. These costs can be significant for small projects, making it difficult for project developers to bring robust, well-structured projects to commercial banks to seek financing.

### Constraints on sovereign borrowing:

Rwanda's debt-to-GDP ratio is constrained, due to high levels of debt in the public sector and a lack of revenue-generating assets. The government has been unable to access international capital markets for large-scale infrastructure such as large-scale construction, which has consumed a significant amount of sovereign debt. The Central Bank of Rwanda (CBR) has also been unable to generate sufficient foreign exchange revenues, and local industries (e.g., tourism, airlines and conventions/conferences) hit by the slowdown of international travel. These industries have required support during the economic crisis, which has further constrained the government's ability to access sovereign debt markets for large-scale projects or sovereign finance for green projects, and in particular for small distributed projects, or green projects in the private sector.

83. Paradigm Shift

Globally, estimates of the investment required to achieve a low-carbon transition range from USD 1.6 trillion annually between 2015 and 2030 for supply-side energy system investments alone (IPCC 2018), while the Global Commission on Adaptation (GCA 2019) estimates adaptation costs of USD 180 billion annually from 2020 to 2030. However, climate finance flows reached a record high in 2017 and continued at a similar level in 2018, with the largest increases observed in China, the U.S., and India, as well as increased public commitments to land use and energy efficiency. This was not enough to close the investment gap.

The existing international climate finance system will not be able to fill this investment shortfall alone. 73 Not only does more public capital have to flow into climate investment, but that public capital has to achieve a higher return on investment than private capital. The Green Climate Fund (GCF) and most of DFIs are appropriately designed to minimize risk-taking and maintain high credit ratings, change the current paradigm. The GCF’s innovative financial architecture, including its unique risk-sharing model being demonstrated by the RGIF is specifically designed to provide specialized intermediation and risk mitigation mechanisms, and to offer tailored solutions to fill existing gaps in country-specific scenarios.

As noted above in Section D.2., the RGIF will be the first application of the Green Bank model in a context where it can be scaled up and replicated across Africa. The programme will be financed and operated across two phases. Phase I will include start-up and the initial deployment of the funds under both the PPF and Credit Facility. * **Gender Monitoring Office (GMO):** GMO is an independent organ monitoring and evaluating compliance with the National Gender Policy on a periodic basis.
* **National Women's Council (NWC):** NWC was established to serve as a forum for women at all governance levels. NWC carries out advocacy for the most vulnerable women, mobilizes women to know their rights and responsibilities, and ensures that women's voices are heard in every aspect of its grass-root levels. The woman elected as a representative of the National Women's Council at District level becomes a member of the District Council.
* **Rwanda Women Parliamentary Forum (FWF):** lobbies and advocates for legislation to support gender equality and women's empowerment.

Girls' education in Rwanda has been improving for the last twenty years thanks to clear policies and initiatives from the government. # Focus on RGF priority sectors: Agriculture and Energy

## Agriculture sector gender assessment and opportunities for RGF

Rwanda’s agriculture sector employs the majority of Rwandan workers, a majority of whom are women involved in subsistence farming or in smallholder production. The G芙 has implemented various programs to support women empowerment in the sector, offering agriculture extension services for both crop production and livestock development. These programs have benefited women. In crop production for example, women-headed households received 10% more benefit compared to men-headed households. Regarding livestock, while both women and men received equal support regarding livestock extension services, it is worth noting that women-headed households have benefited more from the Giraffe project (a government initiative) than men-headed households. Specifically, 25.6% of women headed households has been impacted compared to 11.4%.

However, it is worth noting that men are more likely to be involved in market or business-oriented agricultural activities compared to women (87% vs 38.4%)**). Only 1.2% of women households involved in agriculture were engaged in livestock production as opposed to 6.6% for men headed agricultural households. Additionally, women-owned land is less likely to be used in land consolidation schemes (only 11%)** of land owned by women was used in land consolidation schemes compared to 15.9%** for men’s land that has been consolidated. This means that women are less likely than men to have access to better exploit their land and increase their financial revenues. Consequently, women often lack the necessary resources to be able to get access to financial support from commercial banks or SACCOS. RGF investments in this sector should target to build women capacity it to be more involved in business-related agricultural activities.

In Rwanda, women constitute the majority of people working in agriculture and living in rural areas and are therefore more likely to be more vulnerable to climate change effects in the sector. Energy sector gender assessment and opportunities for RGF
Energy demand in Rwanda as in most countries in East Africa is driven by household consumption. With the main source of primary energy for residential use being biomass, mostly used for cooking. In most Rwandan households, women are responsible for collecting firewood and other forms of solid fuel energy especially in rural areas with consequences to their health. Despite a persistent reliance on biomass as the primary source of energy, women have recently increased their use of alternative solutions to biomass as a source of energy. The proportion of women using solar panels has increased from 0.1% in 2013/2015 to increase from only 0.1% three years before. This is the result of the government efforts to promote clean cooking technologies including biogas as well as solar panels for lighting.
Regarding access to electricity, only 20%9 of women headed households had access to electricity as a source of energy in rural areas compared with 45% of men headed households in 2016/2017. It is interesting that 76% of female headed households were still using firewood for lighting compared to 21% for male headed households.
