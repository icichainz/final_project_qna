### CIO comprises two separate but operationally inter-linked funds.

The first fund in the facility, the "CIO Development Fund" (DF), is mandated to provide development loans to fund the early stage development of a project lifecycle. The second fund, the "Construction Equity Facility Fund" (CEF), will be used for the construction and operational phase of projects, with an all equity solution. The funds are structured with a 15-year investment period with a mechanism to recycle capital. Capital invested in the DF will be recycled into the CEF after five years.

Once the CEF's project companies become operational will be reinvested in additional projects over the course of the 15-investment period, thereby creating a self-sustaining cycle. The combination of the two funds allows for investment in projects at a faster time and through the same commitment of capital by investors, resulting in a greater global social and environmental impact.

In the future, the DF and final fund will be raised by CIO to complete the lifecycle financing of renewable energy project companies. This fund, the Refinancing Fund (RF), will, once raised, provide debt to CEF project companies once they have a proven operational track record. At the moment the RF is not being raised and, as such, is not part of the scope of this proposal.

The Development Fund and the Construction Equity Fund will be referred to as Climate Investor One (CIO) throughout this Funding Proposal on the exclusion of the Refinancing Fund.


## How CIO works

The successfully developed, and therefore bankable, projects generated by the Development Fund (DF) act as proprietary deal flow (pipeline) for the Construction Equity Fund (CEF). The CEF pays the DF a premium to cover the cost of the development and then constructs the renewable energy project through equity only financing. This process ensures that the DF remains focused on developing projects with strong commercial viability, while the CEF is able to focus on executing. Once constructed and operational (with stable cashflows), the Refinancing Fund (RF) will provide debt (a partial refinancing) to the project company in order to complete the lifecycle financing of the project. The RF will be structured as a revolving fund and retains the ability to finance further projects. Remaining equity and outstanding loans will be directed back to the CEF and RF, respectively, upon sale of the asset. The RF, once raised, will complete the lifecycle financing concept of CIO.

### C. Climate Investor One Composition & Structure

#### Development Fund

Climate Investor One's Development Fund is designed to finance the development stage of a project’s lifecycle. The DF seeks to find early stage project development opportunities whereby finance at this stage is scarce to come by or expensive, and where there are significant risks associated with moving forward into later stages in the market.

Not only does the DF enable ready available capital for a project developer, it also reduces the complexity of the overall transaction structure. With 50% of the cost of a project’s engineering, procurement and construction focus less on capital raising and more on project execution. This methodology is designed to reduce complexity and timelines throughout the development stage.

Throughout the development stage, the CFM team along with 3rd party advisors will provide project development support assistance in areas such as technical assistance and finance modeling, legal assistance, structural assistance, implementation of international best practice environmental, social & governance standards, Know Your Customer (KYC), due diligence, structuring, and cost/financial modeling processes to add the expertise to what and when it impacts the development process.

It is estimated that a Construction Equity Fund of USD 775 million will require a Development Fund of USD 46.9 million. This figure is the conclusion of several factors. Firstly, CFM estimates a failure rate for each dollar invested in projects in the development stage of approximately 10%. Secondly, the Development Fund will be repaid by the premiums from successfully developed and finances close within the CEF; the investors in the project company (including CEF) will repay the Development Fund to the CEF. Thirdly, the Development Fund will be repaid by the premium paid out from failed projects from the premiums of successful projects, the size of the Development Fund should cover approximately 30% of the total cost of a project. Fourthly, the Development Fund will be repaid against capital gearing being locked into stalled or paused projects and enable the Fund to provide loans at attractive and impactful prices.

The goal of the development loan premium is to maintain the capital of the Development Fund meaning that the premium should compensate for failed projects. The premium is based on the estimated failure rate and may be adjusted over the investment period of the Development Fund if required.

## Construction Equity Fund - Multiple Tranche Approach – Tier 1, Tier 2 & Tier 3

Climate Investor One’s Construction Equity Fund is designed to finance and expedite the construction phase of a project's lifecycle. CEF will provide up to 70% of construction costs in all equity capital to projects developed with investments from the Development Fund. The CEF is appropriately divided into three tranches so as to attract multiple investor classes.

### Tier 1

The first tranche, Tier 1, holds a junior equity position in the structure of the CEF, which absorbs a higher portion of risk through its lower priority ranking and lower return expectations. Tier 1 investors are paid interest on their capital at an interest rate that is less but to the CEF. All Tier 1 funding is routed through the Development Fund, which is the sole seller in the Tier 1 tranche. The Development Fund is funded with donor capital in the form of reimbursable grants.

### Tier 2

The second tranche, Tier 2, holds an ordinary equity position and targets commercial investors seeking commensurate returns within the fund, at an acceptable risk profile. Tier 2 is supported by the first loss position of Tier 1 and affords a higher return to Tier 2 investors than Tier 3 investors. Tier 2 investors receive their capital plus return on their capital at a higher rate after Tier 3 investors have been repaid their capital plus return. After Tier 3 investors receive the return of their capital, the Tier 2 investors receive the remaining returns.

### Tier 3

The third tranche, Tier 3, ranks in a senior equity position and provides investors a guaranteed return on the back of an Export Credit Agency (ECA) guarantee. This tranche is designed for investors with no or minimal pre-developing markets investment experience. Tier 3 investors receive their capital plus return at a higher rate than Tier 2. Tier 3 returns are supported by the first loss position of Tier 1, as well as the greater risk exposure of Tier 2.

Dividing the CEF into three tranches enables an effect across the three tiers that the risks-the investment proposition for commercial investors is more attractive and the risk profile for Tier 3 investors is higher than the risk profile in Tier 1. Without the provision of this type of donor capital, it is suggested that the investment proposition would not be attractive enough to enable risk averse, OECD-provided commercial capital at scale into such an early stage of a project's lifecycle.

## Capital Recycling

After the project is constructed and reaches CO2, it will be refinanced with debt to reduce the cost of capital and replenish CEF. There are no maximum refinancing limits, though CEF estimates that leverage will be between 50-70% on a project by project basis, depending on project structures, size & technology. At a certain point after refinancing CEF will exit its equity stake in the project. The debt will be structured to provide for an interest rate of 3.25% and a maturity date of 10 years or earlier if the project is sold. If the project is not sold at that time, the debt or existing project will be refinanced into new projects until the end of the investment period (15th year of CEF's life). At this point, CEF estimates that with a debt of USD 775 million, CEF capital will be recycled and reinvested by a factor of 3.37 over the lifetime of CEF. The Development Fund will be repaid in full from the proceeds of the project sale as explained in the section above; CEF estimates that Development Fund capital will be recycled and reinvested by a factor of 10.

Refinancing will be part provided by the Refinancing Fund, the third CEF fund to be set up in the short-term future. It is designed to "gear" the project company with debt finance post construction, removing the need for complicated (and time consuming) multi-party refinancing structures. The Refinancing Fund will be comprised of commercial and international investors seeking to provide debt on well-structured and low operational risk cash flow projects.

CEF have received a letter of interest from FMO stating a soft commitment for an investment of USD 25 million in CEF's Refinancing Fund, which is expected to be set up by the end of Q1. The Refinancing Fund will be structured as part of the scope of the present Funding Proposal or the requested financing from GCF.

### CIO Investment Strategy

Climate Investor One targets medium size renewable energy projects of between 25 MW – 125 MW. However, CIO may finance projects that are slightly less than or below the targeted size limit in order to fund e.g. a project’s subsequent phase expansions or otherwise scale up CIO’s investments, which would be made possible by a GCF contribution.

In order to achieve its investment objective to deliver clean energy at affordable prices across emerging markets, the strategy of Climate Investor One is to:

- **target projects that are expected to have sustainable and relatively predictable cash flows. Limited exposure to demand and pricing risk and limited dependence on the economic cycle;**
- **target projects with a sufficiently long-term horizon to allow for market risk adjusted return recognition targeting that short-term yields from some investments may vary from the long-term target because of requirements for capital expenditure and/or availability constraints;**
- **acquire positions of strategic influence in order to ensure, as far as possible, that Climate Investor One’s requirements are met during the development, construction and operational stages and for the duration of Climate investor One’s investment.**
- **participate in the early development cycle of the Project Companies – i.e., in the planning and development phases;**
- **target Project Companies that can benefit from Climate Investor One’s expertise in areas such as project development, environmental and social risk management, financial structuring and engineering.**

Project finance is a widely used tool to finance infrastructure development; the technique is used successfully in many markets across different sectors. It, however, a complex tool as any underlying project goes through a series of different phases, each with a different risk profile and success requires appropriate funding at each stage.

Simplistically, the project finance life-cycle can be broken down into three phases: project development, construction and the operational phase. Each phase has its own unique characteristics and is funded from different providers of capital. There is also added complexity as successful projects require political support and enabling regulatory regimes.

The challenges are even greater in emerging markets where investors face not only greater political and regulatory risk, but also higher levels of credit risk. Nevertheless, finding financing solutions is vital for scalable infrastructure investments in emerging markets to help address the global development challenge.

To date, various efforts have been made by governments, financial arrangers and DFIs in the development community to find solutions to this challenge. Some initiatives have focused on specific parts of the value chain - such as project development or construction financing - while others have sought to provide a more comprehensive approach to financing solutions. These facilities have sought to bring all the various parts of the financing structure under a single management structure, thereby reducing some of the uncertainties that exist with regards to a particular project being able to find investors as it progresses.

CIO was designed specifically with the aim of supporting infrastructure projects from development through to completion. Thus, CIO comprises two sub-funds which coincide with the key main phases of project finance: project development and project construction. The CIO Development Fund (CDF) is focused on providing funding for the project development phase while the CIO Construction Fund (CCF) provides funding during the project construction phase.

CIO has been designed to overcome some of the perceived weaknesses in infrastructure investment into emerging markets that transpire into an overall lack of bankable projects.

Time is the biggest risk for a project developer. Processes always take longer than planned. In developing countries, a project developer typically commences development with its own capital and within a short period of time seeks grant and TA funding because the development stage is too early for institutional, private sector financiers to participate. As TA and grant units are usually underfunded, they cannot provide sufficient resources to complete the development phase. The project developer needs to procure more capital to complete the development phase. If sufficiently advanced, the project developer can sometimes obtain a loan from an international financial institution or a commercial bank. However, this process is repeated. The net result is that the project developer spends most of the time raising capital instead of developing the project. This results in an even greater risk for the end user and in instances poor quality of development.

### B.3. Financial Markets Overview (if applicable)

There are many barriers in financing renewable energy projects in developing countries. The main factors are as follows:

1. Lack of Long-Term Financing
Renewable energy projects are normally characterized by high up-front costs and low ongoing operating costs, which makes long-term financing for such projects necessary. This is a disadvantage compared to traditional GHG emitting technologies, which are often viable with short term loans. In many developing countries long-term financing is difficult and sometimes impossible due to the lack of credit history and collateral. Furthermore, institutional investors are unfamiliar with the renewables market and perceive technology and country risk to be high and difficult to estimate. As a result, institutional investors are looking for long-term assets to match the profile of their liabilities such as pension funds. In developing countries, such funds often don't exist or investment activities largely to the purchase of government debt owing to its low risk.

## Lack of Project Financing

Renewable energy projects also seek to access funds on a project finance basis. With project finance the security for the loan comes from future project cashflows and tilting-up-front collateral is required. There is still, however, the need for a share of the project to be funded from equity. This type of funding allows renewable energy technology projects to be developed more quickly than if they were dependent on equity financing alone which can only be raised during operations.

The alternative to this would be to rely heavily on equity funding, payments to which can be delayed until the later stages of a project's life cycle.

Renewable energy technologies are more exposed to the limited availability of project financing than most conventional technologies as the share or capital costs in their total cost is much greater

## High and Uncertain Project Costs

Renewable energy projects are quite vulnerable to changes in the regulatory framework. Due to their lack of cost competitiveness, these projects are dependent on a supportive regulatory framework to proceed—including commitment from governments to provide long-term support for renewable energy projects, including subsidies investments and guarantees of purchases of their output. Severe problems for project viability can arise where the regulatory framework is not supportive.

Furthermore, such projects are often located in environmentally and socially sensitive areas. For example, with larger solar and wind projects, land use requirements can be very significant. All these factors make it necessary for renewable energy project developers to have access to a wide range of financing sources, including equity capital, debt finance and project finance. Generally, such funds come from their own resources or from sources of risk capital. In developing countries, the small number of large-scale project sponsors means that the availability of this source of funding is limited.

Generally, there is little availability of this capital in developed countries financial markets.

## 5. High financial cost relative to other technologies

The high costs of renewable energy relative to conventional generation technologies are a key risk to their success. These higher costs are often reflected in the relatively low take-up rates for renewables in many underdeveloped financial markets. The high up-front capital costs of many renewable technologies compared to conventional technologies further worsen their commercial position and make them less attractive.

For grid-connected projects, the high cost of renewable energy can be overcome, at least in part, through priority rights to dispatch and/or must take obligations on take-s. This means that these projects are effectively removed from having to compete with other forms of generation for access to the grid. However, this is not always possible. The revenue streams from renewable energy projects are generally recovered from electricity customers as a whole either through the monopoly power of the distribution or where the electricity market is competitive, through some form of levy or universal charge.

But if costs are too high relative to alternatives, affordability concerns may mean that such priority treatment is not given. There may also be political pressure on governments to ensure that renewable energy projects are affordable and that consumers will have commitments to pay them honoured, whether governments will continue to make the necessary funds available to cover any shortfall or simply voided off-takers, or whether attempts will be made to renegotiate these commitments on the grounds of affordability.

The world is at a critical juncture in its efforts to combat climate change. Governments are increasingly recognizing that the short-term economic benefits of fossil fuels will have long-term and irreversible impact across the world. In December 2010, the 196 Parties to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) agreed to keep global warming below two degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels or even below one-and-a-half degrees Celsius. In order to meet this objective, it is of key importance for countries around the world to proactively manage their energy systems and transition towards low-carbon technologies. It has been estimated that an estimated USD 2.3 trillion will need to be directed at low carbon technologies and energy efficient solutions by 2035.

Since 1995, annual global GHG emissions have risen by more than one-quarter. Energy production and use accounts for around two-thirds of global GHG emissions today, of which carbon dioxide (CO₂) forms the great majority of trace gases. On a per capita basis, CO₂ emissions in OECD countries are about 10 times higher than those in non-OECD countries. By 2050, it is expected that energy-related CO₂ emissions will have risen by 40%. Effective action in the electricity sector is consequently, essentially, to tackling the climate change problem.

In the beginning of the 20th century, energy-related increases in CO₂ emissions originated almost exclusively from Europe and the US. Since then, the share of OECD countries has fallen from around two-thirds to less than one-third as economic and population growth. As of 2015, OECD countries account for around 40% of global CO₂ emissions with non-OECD countries accounting for about 60%. By 2030 it is expected that energy-related CO₂ emissions in OECD countries will have grown by 10%, and account for around 68% of global CO₂ emissions. As a result, climate change and the willingness to reduce CO₂ emissions are playing an increasingly important role in the political agendas of developing markets.

The EPC Contract contains scope of work, price and payment terms, performance obligations, availability guarantees, insurance policies, land lease agreements, construction risks (e.g., geological risk, adverse weather conditions), performance bonds, warranty periods, liquidated damages and limitation of liability.

The Finance and Shareholders' Agreements are a set of legal documents that deal with the terms and conditions of the debt and equity finance, sponsor support, cash waterfall and security.

Financing for renewable energy projects can take a number of forms but are typically either secured through an owner's balance sheet by way of corporate finance or on a limited or non-reverse basis through project finance. A key difference between the two approaches lies in how debt is structured. Both project and corporate finance often include a debt component that is secured against the cash flow generated from the project, which can be used to service the debt and pay dividends to the shareholders. In project finance, the project company borrows against the cash flow generated by the project alone (additionally using any available assets and guarantees), while corporate finance leverages the company balance sheet, not the cash flow itself.

A key point in project finance is that the cash flows from the project alone must be sufficient to service the debt or to repay the debt without corporate guarantees. The magnitude of the cash flow that is deemed sufficient depends on the risk profile and credit rating of the borrower. Project finance also has a number of covenants imposed by lenders, which lend a degree of inflexibility into the financing structure, which makes it difficult for the project company to make any significant changes in its operations or strategy in the early years. The use of equity only from the CIO Construction Equity Fund is designed to remove this complexity (time lag and rigidity), and thereby increase the probability of a project reaching successful operations.

Access to affordable, sustainable energy is an enabling factor for economic development, poverty reduction and the facilitation and promotion of more gender inclusive societies. ### Environmental Co-benefits

Whilst CIO's focus is climate change mitigation through the reduction in GHG emissions, the renewable energy projects that will result from CIO's financing will have other environmental externalities depending on the country in question. For example, in countries where deforestation is a major problem, the implementation of renewable energy projects would contribute to alleviating this problem. Furthermore, CIO follows strict environmental and social responsibility guidelines which ensures the proper ration of biodiversity and migratory species.

### Economic Co-benefits

The economic benefits of providing developing countries with clean energy are unquestionable. Electricity allows businesses to grow faster and can be used to increase labour productivity. Energy is necessary for developing countries to reach their full potential, as it enables them to build infrastructure such as roads, bridges and other transport structures, hence allowing countries to grow faster. Some of CIO's countries have specific energy related economic issues, such as dependence on imports. For example, Morocco imports over 90% of its energy, which makes it vulnerable to price fluctuations and exposes it to a fiscal deficit risk.

### Social Co-benefits

All of CIO's projects are subject to guidelines on social governance, which ensure the most benefit for local communities. The positive externalities of providing clean energy are numerous, especially if the population did not have electricity access before. Access to clean energy can improve health and reduce mortality rates in developing countries that rank as the highest in pollution, which serves as a severe health hazard causing a variety of respiratory diseases and conditions such as the country's mortality rate.

### Gender Co-benefits

CFO recognize that the energy shortfall in many developing countries is a gendered issue, as women are typically more vulnerable to the negative consequences of energy poverty, and men often disproportionately benefit by increased access to energy. In many of these jurisdictions, women are often solely responsible for household and community energy provision.

CIO's intervention in its CCF target countries seek to help reduce the exposure that women have to energy poverty through the implementation of the Gender Integration Action Plan, which is designed to help benefit women as renewable energy stakeholders, workers, and end-users.

### F.2 Technical Evaluation

#### Solar PV

Solar photovoltaic (PV) devices generate electricity directly from sunlight via an electric process that occurs naturally in certain types of material, called semiconductors. Electrons in these materials are freed by solar energy and can be induced to travel through an electrical circuit, powering electrical devices or sending electricity to the grid.

Solar PV technology works as follows: Photons (light particles) strike and ionize the semiconductor material on the solar panel. The electrons are forced into a conduction band. Due to the crystalline structure, the electrons are forced in one direction creating a flow of electrical current. Solar cells are not 100% efficient; in fact, this is because some of the light spectrum is reflected, so it's too weak to excite an electron and generate electricity instead of energy.

A typical crystalline silicon solar cell, which is the most used PV technology, is wafers of high-purity silicon with various impurities and are fused together. The resulting structure creates a pathway for electrical current within and between cells.

#### Run-Of-The-River Hydro

The run-of-the-river hydroelectric concept uses the natural flow of water from a river to produce electricity. Run-of-the-river hydroelectric projects are dramatically different in design, appearance and impact from conventional (impounded) hydropower plants. They do not require large reservoirs or dams to store water for later use. Instead, they rely on the natural flow of rivers. Second, there is no limited alteration of downstream flows since all diverted water is returned to its natural course before being used.

Climate Investor One focuses solely on the run-of-the-river technology as it offers the least technology risk and the impact on the natural and social environment is limited in comparison to conventional impounded hydropower projects, which require a significant reservoir and/or often alter the downstream flows.

### Wind Turbines

Wind turbines generate electricity by harnessing the natural force of the wind in order to power an electrical generator. The commonly used technology is based on the gearbox technology (although there are increasing numbers with direct drives) whereby a low speed shaft is turned through the rotation of the blades. The shaft goes into a gearbox, which increases the rotation speed. The generator that is linked with the gearbox converts the mechanical energy into electricity.

Wind turbines typically have one, two or three rotor blades and range between 30 and 90 metres in diameter. The power of wind turbines installed on land varies from 10 kW up to several MWs, with the crucial parameter being the diameter of the rotor blade. The larger the diameter of the rotor blade, the more energy is captured by the wind and the power generated. Currently, the most powerful turbine is rated at 8 MW and has a rotor diameter of 164m.

Large modern wind turbines are typically equipped with a yaw mechanism that actively controls the turbine to face the wind (the angle between the wind direction and the turbine pointing direction), the power output is maximized.

The energy produced by a wind turbine varies in terms of the site potential of the site itself (function of the wind speed), the arrangement of devices in the wind farm, and the presence of wind shear (the difference between near and turbine pointing direction), the power output is maximized.

### Why Solar PV, Wind & Run-of-River Hydro?

Relative to traditional forms of energy production, energy generation through renewable resources is now in a post growth phase. The cost of solar photovoltaic cells has fallen by 80% since 2010 and continues to fall. Similarly, wind, sunlight and water specifically make wind plants, solar PV plants and hydro plants a central source for harnessing energy. These technologies are also becoming more efficient with time.

Technological sophistication and increased production scale have reduced pricing, led off-takes and overall time to project completion. The cost of renewable generation has fallen by 80% since 2010. In the case of wind there was a reduction of 61% in solar and 14% in onshore wind costs over the past five years. Wind pricing has reduced threefold in the past three decades and various studies predict at least a 20-35% reduction in the next two decades. Resultantly, the cost of renewable energy is now competitive with conventional generation options (on a levelized cost basis). Solar costs are lower than conventional generation options already (on a levelized cost basis). The further cost reductions expected over time will continue to motivate the introduction of conventional generation options within a heavy carbon footprint in nearly all jurisdictions.

From a risk perspective CFM's technology and investment team will concentrate on proven technologies that are mature, have been widely deployed and have a track record. CFM will also seek to engage with a wide variety of governments, utilities, regulators and financial institutions to absorb unfamiliar technologies. Thus, CFM concentrates on wind, solar and hydro as proven and mature technologies with a downward cost-of-energy trend. CFM does however also consider other renewable energy sources such as geothermal, biomass, tidal, wave, ocean current or biofuels. These will however be the exception rather than the rule and will be judged on a project by project basis.

Fundamentally, CFM believes that all projects should have a positive environmental impact. In all cases, each project will be thoroughly examined as to what of the mentioned technologies can best harness the available renewable resources to mitigate carbon emissions whilst supplying affordable energy and diversifying a green country's energy mix.
