# PROJECT / PROGRAMME SUMMARY

## GREEN CLIMATE FUND FUNDING PROPOSAL | PAGE 2 OF 47

In the past few decades, demographic growth and rural exodus have been the cause of a major development of urban population in Senegal. Among other development challenges linked to fast-growing cities in Africa, damages caused by heavy rainfall are becoming more frequent and severe. The Government of Senegal has been implementing flood risk management measures in Senegal since 2005, when rainfall began to cause serious flood problems in major cities. Projections on the effects of climate change indicate that this trend will continue. In addition, extreme events of intense rainfall will be more frequent and might cause heavy damages to most vulnerable areas and population.

Confronted with these challenges, the Government of Senegal has set up flood management as one of its top priorities, investing in a number of projects aimed at reducing vulnerability and risk. The Senegalese government has included flood risk management on its list of priorities, with an estimated investment budget around 2 billion dollars. As the first stage of emergency response to the growing flood concern has been completed in 2015, Senegal now faces the medium- to long-term perspective for effective flood management.

The Senegal Integrated Urban Flood Management project will aim at supporting Senegalese policy on flood risk management through a disaster risk reduction perspective. It will participate in the drainage infrastructure investment that is necessary to reduce vulnerability and risk of flooding in major cities, while contributing to the overall objective of establishing a national-scale integrated policy for disaster risk management in order to optimize investment at national scale and ensure long-term sustainability.

The proposed project will be instrumental in making the shift in policy-making regarding flood management that has been initiated by the Government of Senegal: a reality. Through an integrated approach, not limited to hard-engineered infrastructure solutions but also including soft engineering measures (e.g., green spaces), it will contribute to reducing vulnerability in existing and future urban centers, (ii) reinforcing prevention, especially for drainage infrastructure management, and (iii) improving the overall resilience of cities through sectorial governance. Senegal has the opportunity to be at the cutting edge of flood-management policy in West Africa.

# C1: Strategic Context

Surging flood risk worldwide. Of all natural disasters, floods are the most frequent and recurring, and the number of flood events has been steadily increasing over the past 50 years. Recent history has demonstrated the dramatic increase in loss of human lives and economic damages caused by urban floods, especially in Africa. In 2014, it was estimated that more than 3 billion people live in areas that are more affected by floods worldwide, and associated economic losses amount to more than 40 billion USD.

This phenomenon can be explained by two combined factors: (i) the increasing vulnerability of urban zones, caused by rapid urbanization, population growth, and climate change; and (ii) the increased exposure expected climate change, including extreme meteorological events and sea-level rise. In Senegal, those trends are already visible.


## Flood risk in Senegal

In 2014, urban population was estimated to be 43% of the total population and is growing at an annual rate of 3.9%. Greater Dakar region is the main urban center with almost 25% of the country's GDP. The city of Dakar is located on a coastal plain, which is subject to flooding during high tides; the county’s economic activity is located in Dakar. Rapid urban-coastal migration as well as natural population growth induces strong land pressure and often unplanned urban fabric in the suburbs of the main cities.


## Climate change

Climate change in Senegal is predicted to manifest as a decrease in the amount of rainfall, however with increased intensity, increased temperatures, and sea-level rise. Doubling and saline intrusion threaten water services, agriculture, and coastal zones. The results from climate models indicate that the country will experience more extreme weather events. Climate models in the ensemble project a wide range of changes in the mean annual rainfall averaged over the country, ranging between −20% to +15%. This is consistent with the conclusions drawn by other studies on the conclusions of changes in precipitation; however, it is likely that a greater proportion of precipitation will occur in shorter and more intense events.


## Growing vulnerability

Growing vulnerability and seemingly increasing hazard pave the way for more frequent disasters. Indeed, major flood events have been witnessed in 2005, 2009 and 2012, in the capital city Dakar, the latter affecting 34% of its population. The frequency and intensity of these extreme hydro-meteorological floods have been a continuous concern for Senegalese authority, as flood events now occur almost each year during the rainy season. These floods are particularly devastating because they affect the most vulnerable people. Dakar is the most heavily touched part of the country but cities like Kaolack, Saint-Louis, Diourbel and Thiès also suffer from flooding.


## Flood management strategic framework in Senegal

Taking action on this observation, the Senegalese Government progressively set Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) as part of its national development strategy in 2013. The DRR strategy is based on the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction adopted by 187 Member States of the United Nations including Benin), identifies four priorities: (i) undertaking a comprehensive risk assessment; (ii) strengthening disaster preparedness and response capacity, investing in disaster risk reduction for resilience and (iv) enhancing disaster preparedness for effective response to disasters. The strategy is supported by the National Strategy for Disaster Risk Reduction (SNDRR).


## Operationally

This led to the elaboration of a 10-year Flood Management Program for the 2012-2022 period (Programme Décentral de Gestion des Inondations – PDGI). This PDGI accounts for structural measures, such as flood protection works and urban drainage systems, but also non-structural measures. In September 2015, Senegal submitted its Intended Nationally Determined Contributions (INDCs) to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), which includes a target of $3 billion for the period 2016 to 2030 in terms of investment amount required to build resilience to floods.

# C2. Project Objective against Baseline

## Impacts of floods in Senegal: Between 1980 and 2009, floods have affected around 900,000 people in Senegal. In 2009 alone, the number of people affected by flooding reached a record high of 142,536 USD*. Given the impact of the floods in the past 30 years and the impact of the last major events, the trend has clearly been accelerating.

- In 2009, six flood events have affected between 485,000 and 507,000 people in Senegal. Throughout the national territory, the economic evaluation of damages amounts to over 104 million USD*. The most affected sectors are agriculture (63%), health (20%), education (19%) and transport (10%). Greater Dakar area has suffered the most from those events, with almost 360,000 people affected and 82 million USD worth of damages (33 million USD on habitat).
- In 2012, floods caused the death of 26 people. Approximately 264,000 people were affected: more than 75% in Greater Dakar area.

The Senegalese coastal area is particularly prone to flood risk. The capital city Dakar is installed in a geographical area characterized by the « Naiyes » landscape - fresh water bodies situated in depressions between sand dunes and mangroves. These areas are flooded during high tides, especially at the Sine River. Most of Senegal's population lives in coastal areas and almost one quarter of the overall population is located within 10 km from the coast.

The return period of the rain events that caused the 2009 and 2012 floods are quite short - 1.5 years for the 2009 event. Those events and their impact have been subject to extensive studies for the Greater Dakar Region. However, knowledge of the actual risk flood risk on the national territory still shows some gaps and there is no evaluation of the potential impacts of extreme events like this 100-years return period rain.

## Impacts of floods in Greater Dakar: Unplanned urban growth and soil sealing have had a major impact on the water balance. The lack of vegetation cover has led to an increase in runoff, which reduces the amount of rainwater retained by the sea. During exceptional droughts in the 70's, houses were built in the dry Naiyes low-lying areas. These houses are not adapted for flood risk and are therefore vulnerable to flooding. The soils are latrines and cannot be used for drinking water purposes. As a consequence, the piezometric level of the groundwater is very high (15 m below sea level) which leads to the presence of pathogenic bacteria of fecal origin, even in case of low-intensity precipitation.

Pikine Irregulier Sud is one of Greater Dakar's main informal settlement region with 300,000 people. The population density is very high (15,000 inhabitants per km²) and this category has little access to basic services. Moreover, the neighborhood is regularly and heavily affected by floods. Polluted and stagnant water from the Sine River are a major source of disease transmission in the area (the majority of the population is affected by waterborne diseases), damage to existing assets and decrease in economic activity. Those problems drive people already living in poverty to leave their unhealthy habitat.

Projected impacts of climate change. Major uncertainties remain as to the effect of climate change in Senegal in general and in particular on the amount and frequency of rain events. However, models tend to show that rainfall will increase over time. The worst-case scenario for flood risk is an overall dry weather which might continue to cause urban migration, misused land use and increased flooding in low points otherwise prone to floods. While a majority of Senegal has a tropical climate, the country's northern regions - located in the Sahel - are part of the semi-arid zone (from north to south), from June/July to September/October, and is driven by the movement of the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone. ### Index des pluies moyennes annuelles

#### Figure 2 - Variation index on mean annual rainfall in Senegal, 1996-2008

As far as global climate change is concerned, General Circulation Models project a wide range of changes in precipitation for Senegal. The models predict an increase in the total amount of rain. However, despite the projected decreases in total rainfall, the proportion of total annual rainfall that falls in heavy rain events has increased.

The document "Vulnerability, Risk Reduction and Adaptation to Climate Change – Country Profile of Senegal" of 2011 financed by World Bank, GFDRR and Climate Change team describes Senegal current and future climate profile. The past climate variability is characterized by the following patterns:

- Mean annual temperature has increased by 0.8°C since 1960, an average rate of 0.20°C per decade.
- Available data indicate that the average number of 'hot' nights per year increased by 27 (an additional night) between 1950 and 2004.
- A period of particularly high rainfall occurred in the early 1960s, while the early 1980s were particularly dry. Statistically significant decreases of around 10 to 15 mm per decade have, however, been observed since approximately 2000 in the southern regions of Senegal (during the wet season of June through September).
- Some increases in rainfall have occurred in the dry season in recent years (2000–2006), but this has not been part of a consistent trend.
- Senegal has experienced a statistically significant decrease in wet season rainfall between 1960 and 2004, with decreases being more pronounced on the inland areas than those closer to the coast.

The current models that describe the future of Senegalese climate project the following trends:

- Mean annual temperatures are projected to rise by 1.1 to 3.1°C by the 2050s, and 1.7 to 4.9°C by the 2080s, with projected rates of warming faster in the inland than areas closer to the coast.
- All projections indicate an increase in the number of days considered 'hot' or 'very hot', on the inland rather than coastal regions that are considered 'hot' in current climate, such with increases occurring more rapidly in the south and east of the country.
- All projections indicate decreases in the frequency of dry days and nights considered 'cold' in current climate.

Different models in the ensemble project a wide range of changes in the mean annual rainfall averaged over the period 2041–2060 (3.1 to +84% by the 2050s) but more models show decreases. It is difficult to draw robust conclusions on changes in precipitation; however, it is likely that a greater proportion of precipitation will occur in heavy rainfall events.

### Description of baseline scenario:
If the present trend persists, it is expected that in years to come vulnerability will continue to increase. Indeed, urban population will continue to grow at a fast rate. Today, urban growth amounts to about 2 million per year, which represents over 35 years (16.7 million new urban dwellers) and over 2.3 million new houses.

If urban planning still fails to integrate - by lack of knowledge or adequate tools - food management concepts, the number of people exposed to food risk will continue to increase faster than population growth. This will lead to a situation where the majority of the urban poor will be living in slums and informal expansion leaving most vulnerable people in a situation of extreme vulnerability and dramatic sanitary living conditions.

### Project objectives against baseline:
Against this baseline scenario of increasing urban vulnerability in Senegal and potential risk elevation due to climate change, the general objective of the project will be to reduce the exposure of the most vulnerable populations to food risks through integrated approaches to flood risk management, with expected benefits tackling the problem from a two-fold perspective: (i) optimize investments in resilience already urbanized areas and (ii) take preventive action to include flood-risk in urban planning.

### Specific objectives:
Derived from the priorities set out by the Sendai Framework for Action, the specific objectives of the project are as follows: (i) to implement integrated flood risk management measures; (ii) to upgrade monitoring and response for better risk prevention and (iii) to strengthen flood risk governance.

## C.3 Project Description

### Component 1 - Kicking Off The Risk:
This component of the project will aim at enhancing and making available knowledge about flood risk in Senegal, through the development of a map of flood risk in Senegal, at national scale and at local scale in six priority urban areas – those most affected by the floods. The knowledge thus produced can then be disseminated to all relevant stakeholders (local authorities, disaster management actors) as well as affected populations in order to create a real risk culture.

#### Sub-component 1-1 Flood Risk mapping:
A Comprehensive Geographical Information System (GIS) for flood risk will be developed through participatory policy-making, land planning and infrastructure design. The GIS will present information at the national scale – and to much finer details – at the local scale in six priority urban areas.

First, based on the compilation of the existing information and on-going studies, a digital terrain model (DTM) with a 50 meters mesh covering the national territory will be developed. This basic topographical data will then be used as background for the flood risk GIS. The second layer to be added in this DTM will be the background map of the flood-risk GIS, which would be composed of two main layers:

- **Hazard Layer:** This layer will pinpoint flood-prone areas for ten-year floods (standard design floods) and other extreme events such as flash floods or storm surges. ### Sub-component 2.1 – Real-time hazard monitoring in Greater Dakar
Rain events in Dakar and its suburbs are characterized by a strong spatial and temporal variability. Moreover, flood risk in this area is strongly dependent on the rainfall intensity and duration as well as the soil infiltration coefficient. This subcomponent of the project will include supply and installation in Greater Dakar of precise meteorological monitoring tools to ensure real-time knowledge of local climatic phenomena and quick response capacity.

A polarization diversity X-band radar antenna (frequency range 8.0 to 12 GHz), less expensive and bulky than standard radars, will be installed at the top of a building in the city center. The radar will cover an accumulated rainfall on an 80 kilometers range, covering most of the Cap Vert peninsula. Coupled through an operating software, this radar will provide real-time information about the evolution of rain events and real-time knowledge of rain events, and predict heavy rainfall with two or three hours anticipation.

According to existing and operational piezometers in Greater Dakar, some equipment will be rehabilitated, replaced or extended to extend knowledge of the water table. Water quality will also be tackled to address the health risks associated with flooding.

This Sub-component will also provide inclusive support to the National Agency for Civil Aviation and Meteorology (Agence Nationale de l'Aviation Civile et de la Météorologie – ANACIM) and Directorate for Water Resources Management (Direction Générale des Ressources en Eau – DGERE) to enhance their capacities to forecast flood risk and ensure sustainable use of the new equipment.

### D.1 Value Added for GCF Involvement

The GCF investment is critical for the project in order to finance the operationalization and strengthening of an integrated flood management policy at national scale. The GCF grant is critical for the project because it will allow Senegal to finance the operationalization and strengthening of integrated flood management. ## E.3.1 Economic and social and economic co-benefits, including gender-sensitive development impact

Floods have direct impact on human life, health, economic assets and activity, land value and social well-being*. Human life and health. Two-thirds of human lives lost related to floods are caused by drowning whereas other causes (trauma, electrocution, fire, poisoning) account for less than one third. Water-borne diseases tend to thrive in flood-prone areas and can be transmitted through contaminated water or food. These diseases affect the population. Death caused by these diseases can occur after the event, thus are not recorded as directly related to the disaster. The most common water-borne diseases include cholera, typhoid fever, dysentery, malaria, dengue fever, and leishmaniasis. Floods also have an impact on health protection, health services and sanitation. For instance, floods over the past five years have contributed to cholera resurging in Senegal. The 2015 flood has led to a resurgence of malaria cases in the region. In total, 34 people died national scale and almost 500 people were hospitalized.

Economic assets are also significantly affected on housing and households, as well as industrial assets and public infrastructure. Economic assets vulnerability might be difficult to measure and monitor, especially when the urban fabric is not stabilized. Land value. Another positive economic impact of flood vulnerability reduction is land value increase*. Social well-being.
