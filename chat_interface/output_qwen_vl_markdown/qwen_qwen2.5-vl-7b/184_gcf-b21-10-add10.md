#### A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

The project will provide water for the entire population of South Tarawa, estimated to be about 62,298 people in 2018, more than half of Kiribati's population, with a reliable, safe and climate resilient water supply. To achieve this, the project comprises:

- A new solar-powered pump and treatment plant (PWT) for South Tarawa
- Rehabilitation of existing water supply network infrastructure to reduce leakage and ensure that all residents can access the new clean water source.
- Building capacity through training building and long-term performance-based contracts for operation of the new infrastructure; 
- An awareness program on climate change, water, sanitation and hygiene with strong involvement of local civil society organizations.

The project will lead to avoided GHG emissions through the following: households will no longer need to boil water using kerosene or diesel generators to purify and treat water due to reduced losses from the network; and the solar PV plant as opposed to diesel generators. 

This project will be transformative for Kiribati and promote a paradigm shift in both the knowledge and practice of how small island developing states can increase the resilience of their water supply systems to climate change.

The Republic of Kiribati consists of 33 islands with a total land area of 815 square kilometer (km²) spread over 3.5 million km2 of sea in the Pacific (Figure 1A, and Annex H). South Tarawa, the nation's capital, lies on the Tarawa atoll. South Tarawa is located between Betio to Bikenibeu. Official population censuses show an increase of Kiribatian population from 92,533 in 2005 to 110,236 in 2015 and is expected to reach approximately 158,000 in 2040 (UN 2017). The Tarawa atoll (comprising both North Tarawa and South Tarawa) has a population of about 90% of the total Kiribati population. ## Water resources and supply

As with all Pacific island states, the provision of water to the population is a priority and a challenge. The Government of Kiribati (Government) is committed to providing a climate-resilient potable water supply to its people, including through the development of new sources of freshwater. This is reflected in the National Water Resources Implementation Plan. Demonstrating the importance attached to this sector and reflecting the vision for the future, the Government has developed three national plans: (i) the National Water Resource Master Plan 2010-2030 (Government, 2010); and the Tarawa Water and Sanitation Roadmap 2011-2025. These planning documents highlight that groundwater abstraction is the most practical, cost-effective and climate-resilient approach to meet South Tarawa’s future water needs.

The existing sources of fresh water for consumers on South Tarawa are: (i) the Boniki and Busta lenses located at the eastern end of South Tarawa; (ii) the Boraiki lens located in the western part of South Tarawa; (iii) small community groundwater wells; (iv) household groundwater wells (drawing from small lenses located below residential areas); (v) small-scale rainwater harvesting tanks; and, (vi) imported water. The quality of these sources varies between households, islands, season and year. It should be noted that the following will be observed in Government, 2010:

- **Boniki and Busta lenses**: Combined, these two lenses can currently provide an estimated 2,000 m³/day of good quality freshwater, based on empirical observations and estimates of long-term average rainfall recharge. They are also used for irrigation purposes by farmers who have access to the water through a network of pipes and 3 galleries on Boraiki lens. The water from each individual gallery is pumped to the main trunk and then into the existing reticulated water network.

- **Rainwater harvesting**: This is considered a useful but small-scale source of freshwater. It is estimated to provide up to 10,000 m³/year in South Tarawa. However, this supply can only be used for domestic purposes during a period of weeks or even months due to low rainfall.

### Groundwater lenses

In recent decades, rapid population growth on the land over these lenses, and localized animal farming have led to the contamination of the water in these lenses. As no treatment is provided at the point-of-use, this water is not suitable for drinking purposes. It is generally estimated that the water from the local groundwater wells is unsafe to use and continues to deteriorate; however, it can be used for other domestic purposes such as washing clothes.

### Imported water (bottled)

This provides good quality water. However, this remains a very marginal source, notably because of its high cost.

At the operational level, the main actor in the Public Utilities Board (PUB), a state-owned enterprise. PUB was established in 1977 to coordinate and manage power generation, water supply and sewage disposal in urban South Tarawa. PUB operates the reticulated water network which supplies potable water to households on South Tarawa. The PUB is responsible for the maintenance of these assets, and the provision and regulation of water supply throughout South Tarawa.

## Climate, wave climate and climate change

Kiribati has a hot and humid tropical climate. Kiribati's climate varies considerably from year to year, driven largely by the El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO).

Air temperatures are closely related to the temperature of the surrounding oceans. Average temperatures are relatively constant year-round, with changes in the temperature from season to season no greater than approximately 1°C (PACOZMA et al., 2013).

Rainfall in Kiribati is affected by the movement of the South Pacific Convergence Zone and the Intertropical Convergence Zone. These bands of heavy rainfall are caused by air rising over warm water where winds converge, resulting also in thunderstorms. The average annual rainfall on South Tarawa is 2053 millimeters (mm). Observations show a slight upward trend during this period. However, there is significant inter-annual variability with the wettest years being from December to April and the driest May to November.

Monthly rainfall is also characterized by extreme variability with monthly rainfall ranging from 0 to 825 mm. The overall monthly mean is 171 mm. The mean monthly rainfall varies from approximately 116 mm for October to 277 mm in January. Typically, there is a wet season from December to April and a dryer season from May to November (White, 2011).

Drought, White (2011) provides a definition of drought in the Tarawa context as a function of rainfall in the preceding 12 months. The most severe droughts occurred between 1947 and 1953 with an average duration of 68 months. The second most severe droughts were between 1947 and 2010 with an average duration of 236 months. The most severe drought occurred in April 1994 when only 217.5 mm rain had fallen in the preceding 12 months. The time between successive severe droughts also varies widely, with the shortest gap being 2 years and the longest 18 years.

### Wind waves in Kiribati are strongly influenced by both north-eastern and south-eastern regional trade winds, and the location of the South Pacific Convergence Zone, and by the Nino-Southern Oscillation from year to year. In Tarawa, waves consist of locally generated trade wind waves from the east and northeast from December to March, and from the east and southeast during April to November. These are generally small in size (less than 10m), but powerful, and some swell propagating from extra-tropical storms in the North Pacific and Southern Ocean (PACCSAPP) can reach Tarawa.

A key parameter is over-topping — whereby seawater flows onto the island and ultimately into the island's groundwater, damaging infrastructure. The aim of this project is to assess the vulnerability of Kiribati’s coastal communities and oceanic parameters in order to determine how wave height, wave direction, wave periods, storm surge, swell, tides, sea level, and wind speed will change over time.

* **Sea level:** the astronomical tide is by far the dominant influence on sea levels in Tarawa, followed by fluctuations due to the lunar cycle. The highest high water (HHW) surge has a relatively minor affect. As a result, the higher the sea level, the higher HHW and the intensity of overtopping.
* **Waves:** models show that swell conditions will cause the most significant occurrences of wave set-up, wave run-up and wave over-topping. Wind waves are far less significant. Hence, over-topping is also a function of swell wave conditions.

### Sea overtopping

Several factors contribute to the risk of sea overtopping and contaminating the lagoon. These include:
* (i) storm surges in which low atmospheric pressure leads to a temporary rise in sea level, possibly exacerbated by local winds;
* (ii) high tides, particularly during the full moon period when the tide is at its highest point of amplitude;
* (iii) high spring tides;
* (iv) high lunar tides;
* (v) high neap tides; and
* (vi) long-term sea level rise, such as that caused by climate change. At any given time, the risk of overtopping is a function of all these factors — if all factors combine to drive a high sea threat, it is very high regardless of conditions. Climate change will most significantly affect (ii), sea-level rise.

## Activity 1 - Construct desalination plant. Based on projected climate impact (see Box 1) and other factors, water demand forecasts until the year 2041 have been prepared (refer Annex B). To meet the projected demand, the single most climate-influenced factor is the amount of seawater available for desalination. The desalination plant will be built in 2023. This activity will install a seawater reverse osmosis desalination plant with an initial capacity of 4,000 m³/day in Te Makon, West Bank, Ngerulmud. The plant is expected to be operational by the end of 2025 and will repair existing tanks with a replaceable water source; while the balance 2,000 m³/day will cover increasing demand.

The plant's capacity (4,000 m³/day) is based on supplying a minimum of 7 liters per capita per day (l/cpd) for consumption in the year 2035. This is equivalent to an increase from the current average daily consumption of approximately 1 liter per person per day (ppd) to around 22% of ppd). The project has adopted World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations of a maximum daily water supply of 7 liters per capita, and will ensure end-use water quality. A minimum additional capacity of 5 l/cpd is added for commercial and institutional consumption. Finally, an additional 2 l/cpd is needed to account for the impacts of increasing air temperature on domestic water consumption. Importantly, given a changing climate and population growth, this capacity may need to be increased in future years. The design team has developed a modular design so as to allow production to be easily and quickly adjusted to meet South Tarawa's demand. The plant has also been designed with the ability to be upgraded to 6,000 m³/day at any time during its operational life cycle, without the need to upgrade supporting infrastructure. These are considered robust engineering design features to better ensure long-term sustainability.

A climate event (as foreseen in Box 1) would lead to a sudden reduction in available water for some time. Whereas this would not pose an existential threat to South Tarawa because the new desalination supply is in place, it would have an impact on network reliability and resilience. The most likely scenario is that of a prolonged drought or other extreme event that severely reduces or eliminates the lens supply. Government will employ rationing as a response. This will be supported by the design team to ensure that water is distributed fairly across the island. There are two scenarios for rationing. However, the level of rationing imposed in the event of a reduction in lens supply is expected to be manageable given that the population is small and there would be no need to ration drinking water (the most critical situation where the lens supply is the main source). The latter suggests that in an extreme event where the lens supply drops to zero, customers would only need to adjust their consumption down by around one third.

## Box 1 - Translating Climate Change Risks into Design Implications

As indicated in the previous section, the mean of sea level rise projected for the waterfront areas at Bonriki and Buada is one of the main pathways by which the water supply on South Tarawa is vulnerable to climate change. After projected climate change impacts are considered, it can be estimated that the lens supply will be reduced by 20% in some years; this would vary and cannot be accurately forecasted, but it is reasonable to estimate it would be between 3 and 5 years duration. The return period for such an event is approximately 100 years (i.e., a 1-in-100 year event). In other words, there will be no water available from the lenses or PUL during these events. In other years, that is to say in most years, there would be 2,000 m³/day per available from the lens of PUL.

To translate this climate vulnerability and risk into network design parameters, the network planners and the project design team must focus on three key factors:

1. On average, one year in five will be an 'event' year, or 20% of years will be 'event' years.
2. The lens supply is expected to be reduced by approximately 20%, so that a resilient water supply network has to be capable of fully replacing the lenses during event years. It should also be capable of meeting additional demand due to population growth and climate change.
3. The lenses will provide water during the 80% of years that are not 'event'. During these years, a slightly higher volume of consumption is expected.

- Boreholes for connections at selected distribution areas.
- Upgrades to reticulation networks in communities across South Tarawa;

# D.1 Value Added for GCF Involvement

GCF contribution to the project is critical to ensuring the resilience of South Tarawa’s water supply to climate change. Without GCF support, this project as designed cannot proceed. The cost of ensuring the climate resilience of the water supply system (including desalination plant and network) has been estimated at US$120 million. This includes reducing greenhouse gas emissions; it has been calculated to be 49% of the total project cost. GCF support is essential due to fiduciary risk, vulnerability and the severe economic and fiscal constraints it faces in addressing these climate change challenges.

Kiribati is a SIDS and one of the least developed countries of the world. In 2015, GDP per capita was estimated to be $1,839 (World Bank). Kiribati’s economy is heavily dependent on remittances from its citizens working abroad (mostly Afghanistan (IMF, 2017)). It is the lowest GDP per capita of all Pacific countries. Kiribati is also classified as a Fragile State by the World Bank and is one of the most vulnerable SIDS Countries by the Human Development Index; it fell to 137 in 2015 and 2016 (UNDP, 2014, 2015). Kiribati is an AID-only country, meaning that it does not accept commercial loans or provide third party loans for Kiribatians.

The economy of Kiribati is highly vulnerable to external shocks and climate volatility due to its exposure to climate change. The country has a high level of debt (30% of GDP) and reliance on remittance inflows from overseas workers. Private sector growth is constrained by the small-scale of the economy; the high costs of doing business, and the country’s supply dependency on imports. Kiribati’s public sector accounts for 58% of the country’s GNP (13% in government employment), maintaining fiscal sustainability is the Government’s key priority.

In its 2017 Article IV consultation, the IMF noted that despite economic growth over the past six years (that was substantially higher than the average for the region) Kiribati remains highly vulnerable to shocks from fishing and a lack of long-run economic development prospects remain high. A significant contributor to these risks is climate change. As a result, staff advised that the Government should continue to focus on fiscal sustainability while also taking into account its exposure on long run growth. Budget provision should explicitly recognize climate change adaptation costs and infrastructure spending.

The STWHP is part of Government’s continued efforts to enhance resilience to climate change, and Government’s financial and in-kind contribution to the project over its lifetime is substantial (given the balance of financial resources needed for the operation and maintenance of water supply systems) as highlighted by the IMF. However, considering Kiribati’s severe constraints and climate vulnerability, grant support from GCF is critical to the project.

## Project emissions

The project will result in the emission of **21,869 tons** of CO₂ over its life, between 2020 and 2041. These emissions result from using South Tarawa's electricity grid to meet the excess energy needs of the desalination plant and network not offset by the solar PV plant.

The project includes installing **2,500 kW** of PV plant capacity (Activity 1.3) to largely cover the energy needs of the desalination plant and network. The total incremental energy demand for the desalination plant and network is an excess demand for energy (around 2% of the energy needed in 2020, rising to 27% of the energy needed in 2041) will be met by the solar PV plant. The excess energy generated from the solar PV plant will be used to reduce load on the grid's diesel generators, taking into account that the existing solar PV feeding into the grid is already at capacity.

The calculations are as follows:

- Energy index for the desalination plant and network: **3.86 kWh/m³**
- Desalination plant capacity: 4000 m³/d
- Energy consumed per day by the project = (3.86 x 2,500) ÷ 16 = 607.5 GWh
- Energy consumed over the projects' 20-year period = 607.5 x 365 = 1,459,050 GWh = 1,635,600 kWh
- Annual energy provided by the Solar PV plant = 4,142.474 kWh
- Energy consumed per over the projects' 20-year period = 20 x 5,635,800 kWh = 112,710 GWh or 112.71 million kWh
- Energy provided by the Solar PV plant over the projects' 20-year period = 20 x 4,142,474 MWh = 82,850 GWh or 82.85 million kWh
- Excess energy demand to be met diesel generators = (112.7 – 82.85) GWh = 29.86 GWh

Hence, in the project, over 20 years, the incremental energy demand of 29.86 million kWh leads to the emission of **29.86 x 10^3 x 7.33 kg** of CO₂, or **21,869 tons** of CO₂.

Total avoided emissions

The total avoided emissions are the reduced emissions minus project emissions, that is: (107,409 tons + 3,914 tons) – 21,869 tons = **89,434 tons** of CO₂.

Allofs are found mainly in the Pacific and Indian Oceans with just a small number in the Atlantic Ocean. Allofs are small and low-lying and they do not have river systems, natural freshwater lakes or deep groundwater with fossil water qualities. They rely on rainwater for drinking and other uses but also depend heavily on imported diesel fuel to access local sources of freshwater.

Many allofs are remote. Remote allos often have limited locally available governance and management capacity, due to the small size of the island population. Also, remote allos often rely on imported fossil fuel for energy - meaning they are energy dependent and they have relatively high GHG emissions per capita. For such remote allos, in the future sustainable water supply systems will need to be developed that can (i) increase access to freshwater; (ii) reduce reliance on diesel independence; and (iii) help reduce energy related pollution. Finally, many, but not all, remote allos face similar challenges as they are often located in low-lying areas with high population density. This means that traditional water use systems may no longer be adequate.

Recent studies (described further in F2) concluded that seawater reverse osmosis desalination represents a practical and affordable long-term option for supplementing water supplies in South Tarawa. Energy consumption is one of the critical factors in determining the cost-effectiveness of incorporating desalinated seawater into water supply systems. The energy consumption for reverse osmosis desalination is directly proportional to the plant. The use of saltwater wells on Ebevei, as opposed to an open intake or beach well which are typically used in desalination plants, will significantly reduce the energy consumption required by the plant. In addition, the seawater that has been naturally filtered to remove suspended solids and also diluted with rainwater, resulting in a total dissolved solid (TDS) content of approximately 100 mg/l, will further reduce the energy consumption for reverse osmosis. Since energy consumption of reverse osmosis is directly proportional to feedwater TDS, the resultant energy consumption per cubic meter of water produced by STWSP is estimated at 3.5 kWh/m³.

STWSP has also been designed to draw feedwater from wells below the atoll. Like Ebevei, the desalination plant will likely not require a pretreatment system (resulting in CAPEX and O&G savings). This will be confirmed following the results of the pilot project on Ebevei.

# Methodology

The economic analysis of the proposed project was carried out in accordance with ADB’s guidelines for conducting the economic analysis of investment projects. The economic efficiency of the investment was determined by computing the economic net present value (ENPV) using ADB’s internal rate of return (IRR).

For the economic analysis, domestic currency estimates have been adopted. Taxes and duties have been removed from the cost estimates, which include all physical works, supply, international and local labor. Cost estimates reflect end-2017 prices and are held constant throughout the period of analysis (to 2041), incorporating a 3.5-year implementation lag for the project. The exchange rate used is the official exchange rate as published by the Bank of Indonesia in the currency of the foreign cost estimates, the exchange share factor is estimated to be 1:0. ## Outcome of the economic analysis

The cost estimates are presented in Section B and the benefits in Section E.3.1. The project IRR is estimated to be 13.0%, comfortably above the 9.5% threshold. The ENPV of the project calculated at the 9.0% discount rate is $11.8 million.

Sensitivity analyses showed the economic efficiency of the project to be robust in the face of increased costs or decreased benefits. When all capital and O&M costs are increased by 20%, the ENPV remains positive at $0.1 million and the IRR falls to 9.5%. Similarly, when all revenues are reduced by 20% (resulting from low total demand), the ENPV is -816.7 million and the IRR falls below the ADB social discount rate of 5.1%.

The project appears most sensitive to changes in savings on fuel costs due to increased transportation, but estimates show that this would not be significant enough to change the economic efficiency of the project. Similarly, the impact of health care cost increases (e.g., treatment costs), productivity losses, and (if fatalities from water-borne diseases) by 20.0% cuts the ENPV to $58.2 million and the IRR falls below the ADB social discount rate of 5.1%. Conversely, if all capital and O&M costs are decreased by 20.0% simultaneously, ENPV becomes negative at -3.15 million while IRR falls to 6.8%.

It is important to note that the preceding analysis excludes any economic valuation of consumers’ willingness-to-pay for safe water. This is because the project is not designed as a stand-alone project but rather as part of the overall water supply tariff structure that is resulting in high incidences of water-borne diseases. To illustrate, valuing willingness to pay at a level consistent with the ADB’s guidelines (as described in Annex B) would place the project’s estimated ENPV at 15.9%, with an ENPV of $20.5 million. Proceeding with the results of a survey to fully quantify these benefits will be properly valued and included in the economic appraisal of the project.
