## C1. Bistategic Context

### 6. Viet Nam has experienced significant economic growth in recent years. Political and economic reforms have transformed Viet Nam from one of the poorest nations in the world to a middle-income country within quarter of a century. With 85 million people, it is now the world’s 13th largest economy. The poverty rate fell from an estimated 60% in the early 1990s to an estimated at US$1.996 in 2012. This has coincided with a dramatic reduction in poverty from 58% to 14% between 1993 and 2008 and an estimated 1.8% in 2011.

### 7. While Viet Nam’s economy has grown rapidly, its coastal communities lag behind in rural areas terms of economic development over the past quarter century. Its coastal communities lag behind in rural areas terms of economic development over the past quarter century. These communities have double the poverty rate of the nation as a whole and are increasingly vulnerable to climate change impacts due to developing pressures on natural buffers, such as mangroves.

### 8. Per the Climate Change Vulnerability Index*, Viet Nam is considered one of three “extreme risk countries” in the world. The rural poor are at especially high risk given their reliance on the natural resources for their livelihoods, particularly in agriculture and fishing. These communities are also more vulnerable to climate change impacts such as droughts, threatening agricultural productivity and the millions of people living on these watersheds for their income. Urban populations in coastal areas are also at risk, particularly suffering from extreme heat, and humidity, as well as floods and storms.

### 9. The fifth assessment report (AR5) from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) indicates that temperatures over Viet Nam have been increasing at a rate of 0.14°C to 0.23°C per decade since 1976, with increases from 0.8°C to 3.2°C by the end of this century. The report further highlights the positive trend in the occurrence of heavy precipitation events over Viet Nam and the negative trend for extreme low temperature events. This is leading to large-scale phenomena affecting the region. Future increases in precipitation extremes related to the monsoon are very likely, with a high probability that these will be associated with more intense tropical cyclones and typhoons resulting in extreme precipitation near the centres of tropical cyclones making landfall along coasts of South China Sea. Cyclone intensity is expected to increase by 10%–25%, and sea level rise could be as much as 30 cm higher than today for the end of 21st century and this is projected to increase the annual number of people affected by coastal flooding.

### 10. Consistent with these findings, the Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment’s (MONRE) records suggest that climate change has contributed to temperature increases in excess of 0.5°C and sea level rise of about 20cm over the past 50 years in Viet Nam. Extreme events have already been increasing. Between 1990 and 2012, the country suffered almost double the number of extreme weather events than it did between 1976-89. Of these, 34% were completely destroyed or swept away and 956,721 were significantly damaged due to natural disaster impacts. Viet Nam’s Second National Communication (SNC) to the UNFCCC states that without urgent action and major action, this would inundate approximately 30,000,000 square meters of land or 3.8% of the total national land surface. Climate projections indicate that sea level rise will continue to increase in Viet Nam over the next century at a rate similar to that which devastated the Tacloban area (Typhoon Haiyan/Yolanda) in the Philippines in 2013.

### 11. These trends place coastal and low-lying delta areas in Viet Nam at particular risk. At 23%, the poverty rate in coastal areas is more than double the national average, and these communities are particularly vulnerable to extreme weather impacts disasters. More than 500,000 people live within 200 meters of the coast. Their homes are most often directly impacted by sea level rise and storm surges. In addition, many of Viet Nam’s coastal areas are densely populated with people in coastal areas live in unsafe housing, in part due to rapid urbanisation, the lack of suitable employment and therefore limited access to land.

**Government Support to Safe Housing for Vulnerable Communities**

The project aims at supporting communities in implementing the National Programme to provide support policies and solutions for poor households to build storm and flood resilient houses in Central Region. Led by the Ministry of Construction, the programme will focus on providing technical assistance to vulnerable households living in areas prone to natural disasters such as typhoons and floods.

#### 21. Government programme helps families categorized as extreme poor and 'poor' by government criteria, as well as those who are identified as extremely vulnerable by local authorities. The programme is a combination of a grant and training on the approved flood and storm-resilient house design. Details are as follows:

- **Grant of VND10m or US$5500/household**, for poor households, up to VND41m (US$26) for households in particularly difficult areas or VND916m (US$587) for households in extremely difficult communities.
- **Training and technical assistance** provided by the government to beneficiaries upon completion of the house frame meeting the design specifications.

#### 22. While a grant is available for extreme poor households, the grant amount is not sufficient to cover the total cost of the house. Therefore, a concessional loan is also made available with a 3% interest rate from the Bank for Social Policy, payable in monthly installments over a period of up to 10 years.

#### Selection of grant recipients is decided through consultations with villages, assessment at the commune level, then ultimately by the provincial government. The selection process takes into account the household's categorization as poor (as per Decision No. 95/2014/QD-TTg) and the criteria for extremely vulnerable households in line with NAM Benefit. The selection is prioritized to ensure that especially vulnerable groups are included.

#### Criteria for Priority Selection:
- Households living in highly vulnerable areas.
- Households with difficult living circumstances (e.g., senior citizen-headed households, single member households particularly senior citizens, households with members with disabilities).
- Households living in the most poor districts under the government poverty targeting programme following national resolution No. 30a/2008/NQ-CP dated 27 July 2008 of the government.

#### Project Selection Process:
The project selection process has been designed to be transparent and includes review by the commune committee. The data to assess criteria for grant assistance is independently collected by the government as part of wider social service provision, and the results are made available in a public place where they can be seen by all parties. Should concerns arise, community members are able to direct concerns to the commune for resolution.

#### Land Tenure:
Land tenure recipients are not permitted to sell their plot during the 10-year loan period. The land repayment is required and used as a revolving fund overseen by the government to enable replication of the approach among additional families.

#### Government Programme Success:
The programme has been successful in engaging civil society organizations such as Fatherland Front, Veteran's Association, and Viet Nam Youth Union to support the above prioritized groups, as well as female-headed households.

36. Coastal mangroves play an important role in protecting Viet Nam’s coastal communities from the sea and communities – absorbing some of the impact of typhoons and storm surges. The government’s current programme is focused on restoring mangroves. The restoration of a large aquaculture plantations operated by local farming cooperatives and households.

For instance, examples from the pilot project in Phu Yen province show that it would cost US$105,000 to cover 2ha of a mangrove forest range from US$25/ha to US$183,000/ha. Mangrove rehabilitation in general has a high rate of failure globally, citing the same lessons learned from pilot projects in Viet Nam.

### Coastal Protection Measures Not Informing Best Practices

- Coastal protection measures are not informed by best practices.
- Coastal protection measures such as sea walls incur high capital investment and maintenance cost, with significant ecological consequences that are counter-productive to their purpose. 
- These structures can lead to increased sedimentation processes and often aggravate coastal erosion, leading to land loss and greater exposure. In the past, strong reliance on hard infrastructure was used to operate effectively and efficiently; however, more recently, evidence suggests mixed results.

### Natural Infrastructure as a Solution

- In recent years, businesses and governments are seeing the enormous potential for natural infrastructure in the form of wetlands and forests, which help coastal habitats perform many of the same tasks as grey infrastructure.
- These ecosystems provide multiple benefits such as flood protection, storm surge reduction, and erosion control. They also serve as a stronger barrier to protect coastal operations against flooding and storm surge during extreme weather, while inland forests can reduce wind speeds by up to 50%.
- The evidence from the region suggests that integrated solutions based on ecosystem services such as mangroves, seagrass meadows, and coral reefs are more effective in reducing the impacts of storms. Although the government and partners have recently invested in mangrove rehabilitation efforts (and as such these areas are now being restored), they still require larger geographic coverage to reinforce and protect poor and marginalized communities living along the coast.

### Inadequate Climate Risk Information

- The lack of climate risk information is a significant barrier to effective adaptation planning and adaptation investments. Availability and uptake of climate risk information is lagging relative to what is required for effective decision-making.
- The government does not have access to reliable data on climate change scenarios (including variability) at the spatial and temporal scales to support decision-making, including projections of extreme weather events such as storm surges, sea level rise, and increased frequency and intensity of tropical cyclones. This lack of information also impacts the ability to assess vulnerability and exposure to coastal hazards and damage and data and economic appraisals of adaptation options are necessary to design sustainable, risk-informed and economically efficient climate change adaptation measures.

### 223. Economic analysis of the proposal, conducted during the design phase, indicated that the expected economic benefit of the project would be $105 million over its lifetime (20 years). ### 224. The use of the 10% discount rate is based on the value of the benefits from the project. In line with the MOC in Viet Nam and expert opinion, 20—25 useful life of the houses and mangroves is suggested. To be conservative we use 20 years as the lifespan of the houses and mangroves.

#### 236. The project will have a number of environmental impacts, which will be managed and impacts limited. During mangrove planting, it will be necessary to undertake earth works in re-stabilized areas where aquaculture facilities have been either relocated or removed. To ensure that the sediment is not mobilised through either wind; or more specifically, through water movement, a number of mitigation measures will be implemented including the use of geotextile bags to contain and stabilise the installation of sediment curtains to reduce sediment movement and the covering of sediment where practicable.

#### 237. Any sediment movement may also expose acid sulfate soils within the mangrove area. Acid sulfate soils and/or associated sediments are a threat in mangroves and are known to occur in large areas of coastal delta in Viet Nam. Different soil types and subtypes have been identified, including those that are above sea level, particularly in low-lying coastal areas which is where the intertentions will occur. Mangroves, salt marshes, foreshores, estuaries, and other wetlands can be affected by acid sulfate soils. The presence of these soils within mangrove ecosystems may result in a number of adverse impacts including killing vegetation, steepening and acidifying groundwater and water bodies, and creating toxic conditions for fish and other aquatic life. If the sediment is exposed to air, it will react with the sulfides present on the surface to form sulfuric acid. The release of this sulfuric acid from the soil can in turn release iron, aluminum, and manganese into the surrounding environment. These elements are highly toxic and can create a variety of adverse impacts including killing vegetation, steepening and acidifying groundwater and water bodies, and creating toxic conditions for fish and other aquatic life.

#### 238. To any sediment, tests will be made for their presence of acid sulfate soils and/or potential acid sulfate soils. If it is found that the sediment contains acid sulfate soil or has a high potential to contain acid sulfate soil, then this sediment should not be exposed to air. The sediment should be covered with a suitable material such as sand or clay to prevent exposure to the sediment. Reference will be made to appropriate standards and guidelines[2]. Every effort will be made to ensure there is no direct or residual impact towards treatment.
