#### A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary

The main objective of this project is to promote rural electrification through isolated solar photovoltaic systems as a low-carbon and resilient solution to the effects of climate change in the energy sector in Mali.

The project will: (i) strengthen the capacity of public institutions (AMADER, AER and FER) engaged in rural electrification to accelerate the deployment of renewable energy solutions; and (ii) provide access to electricity to 70 communities through installation of isolated solar photovoltaic minigrid systems for a total capacity of 4.83 MW. The project will thus catalyse the transition from fossil fuels towards renewables, in line with Mali’s Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) and greenhouse gas (GHG) reduction targets. In this case, it will help avoid 41 049 tCO2/ year; and 1 027 227 tCO2 for the entire life of the electrification project. The project is expected to be implemented in two Tranches: Tranche 1 (35 communities) within the project area. Around 31,000 new households will have access to electrification in rural areas (28,378 for Tranche 1). The project will also contribute to the national renewable energy transition plan. Tranche 2 will achieve 90% of the impact (521 762 tCO2 eq) and will target 50 localities (283 777 inhabitants).

The project has significant potential for paradigm shift in the electrification sector in Mali. Traditionally, the growing energy demand is met through fossil fuels, which are not sustainable or resilient to climate change. This project proposes a new approach to rural electrification by shifting from fossil fuels towards renewable energy solutions. The project will positively contribute to the regulatory framework and institutional capacity of public institutions engaged in rural electrification. It also aims at placing an institutional framework for FER to strengthen its capacity to further promote the deployment of renewable energy.

The paradigm shifting potential lies in the joint efforts to create a conducive environment by putting in place an enabling regulatory framework, financial mechanisms, and technical support. The project will contribute towards creating a sustainable and innovative structural arrangement. It is anticipated that the project model can be replicated across the whole country and in the wider West African subregion, notably to support different African countries to meet their commitments under the Paris Agreement (Nationally Determined Contributions [NDCs]), and to achieve their 2035 Agenda as well as Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).

## Financial instrument: 1 Senior Loan

The loan will be used for installation of mini-grid systems and needs a lower interest rate, for concessional loans are needed to finance projects as this one in a least developed country such as Mali, where most of the population is still not connected to the national grid. The concessionality level of the country will increase and IMF controls will be triggered, resulting in the impossibility for Mali to take the loan.

Regarding BOAD's loan, it is made in local currency, which limits foreign exchange risks and favors a sustained debt. The interest rate is indicative and will be negotiated with the government at the time of project appraisal. Actually, BOAD is in commercial terms with a 10-year grace period and a 25-year maturity for this type of loan. However, and though it mobilizes resources at a market rate, its financing conditions are quite the lower one in the sovereign's financing portfolio. The interest rate will be fixed at 3% for the first five years and then adjusted to the LIBOR plus an additional spread of up to 5%. The government decides in a sovereign way which project will benefit from those resources. So, BOAD loan is not automatically eligible for consideration to its Board; negotiations with the government will help to agree on the appropriate rate to be applied.

The loan is directly given to the Malian Government. The beneficiaries will benefit from the concessionality of GCF through this loan and the interest rate will be capped at 3% per year, which corresponds to 0.26 EUR/kWh (174 XOF/kWh), which is within concessional rates as per information by the feasibility study. According to the baseline scenario, the average tariff generally applied in rural areas for mini-grid operated by private sector is 0.35 EUR/kWh (225 XOF/kWh). The government applies a higher tariff of 0.48 EUR/kWh (316 XOF/kWh) on diesel generators and grid connected systems, a substantial part of 0.26 EUR/kWh (174 XOF/kWh) is applied by the government.

## B.3 Financial Markets Overview (if applicable)

### How market price or expected commercial rate return was (non-concessional) determined?

Commercial banks throughout West Africa including Mali would offer too high rates (12 - 15% as is typical in this region) and they are not willing to lend money for mini-grid projects. The reason behind it is that commercial banks consider it their high risk to lend money to them. The prevailing high interest rate offered by commercial banks are one of the reasons why most of the mini-grids projects have been implemented through debt-for-credit swaps, where banks cannot offer cost-effective tariffs while at the same time having to bear high capital costs. The consequence is that the private sector operators would always resume to the need of subsidies, otherwise they will not be able to pay back the loan.

We have never observed a single case where a commercial debt transaction took place in our subregion to implement a mini-grid system project. All of the observed debt-to-mini-grid projects have been made possible based on concessional loans from international financial institutions (IFIs). The cost of technology has not yet reached the lows where commercial debt alone would suffice to do mini-grid projects.

Bleeding concessional resources with commercial debt is detrimental a first stage to building the foundation of the market that would be required for commercial financial institutions. In this Mali project, it is anticipated that a positive shift in paradigm will commence through:

a) Meeting the rapidly growing electricity access demand by renewable energy instead of continuous addition of fossil fuel.

b) Establishing a conducive regulation framework in order to attract private sector participants to come into the rural electrification market.

c) Establishing a sustainable rural electricity tariff model that will make it financially viable for private mini-grid operators to operate and maintain their systems. This is expected to be the paradigm, which shall set up an example that can be easily and quickly be duplicated throughout the West Africa region and beyond.

### C.1. Strategic Context

This is a greenfield rural electrification project with the aim to meet the growing demand for power from non-electrified communities in Malawi. The country has no coal resources and would require significant foreign investment if this option was not available to the country. However, it is anticipated that the successful implementation of this project will spur and facilitate further investments into renewable energy projects. This is because there has been continuous growth in fossil fuel based generating capacity. Therefore, it is of critical importance to meet future demand through renewable energy instead of continuously adding fossil fuel based power generation units.

#### Geography and climate

The Republic of Malawi is landlocked country in the Sahel West Africa located between 11° and -20° North latitudes (counts for about 1.268 M km²) of which 51% consists of desert land and 4% cultivated land. This geographical situation, combined with the fact that Malawi does not have fossil fuels on its territory, forces the Government to import all the fossil fuel based power generation units.

The country has an intertropical climate regime of Sudan-Saharan character, with high temperatures (average between 26 and 30 °C), influenced by its continentality and is dominated by an alteration of dry season whose duration varies between 5 months in the South to north to 9 months in the North.

Rainfall is characterized by high interannual variability and poor spatial distribution. There is a steady decrease in rainfall from the South (1000 mm / yr) to the North (<200 mm / yr) and the duration of the rainy season (MENA, 2006a). While historical data shows that average temperatures of about 32 °C since 1950, projections point towards continuation and acceleration of this rise in average temperatures as well as a decrease in overall rainfall amounts (with extreme events - floods - more frequent).

#### Socio-economic context

The population is estimated at around 18.057.258 inhabitants in 2017. The population growth rate is estimated at 3,4% per year with 62,5% of the population residing in rural areas. This population growth is a major concern for the future of the country, as it leads to an increase in needs to be met, which will inevitably lead to additional pressure on electricity generation and distribution.

The administrative division of Malawi comprises ten (10) administrative regions and one urban district (Blantyre). These are the regions of Kayiwa, Koukoliro, Sikasso, Segou, Mopiti, Gao, Timbuktu, Kidal, Taoudendil and Meraka. The regions are subdivided into 28 districts.

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita was US $781 in 2016. Dominated by agriculture and the mining sector, the Malian economy recorded a GDP growth rate of 5.3% in 2017. The economic outlook remains positive with projected real GDP growth rates of 5% for 2018 and 4.9% for 2019.

### Electrification

The transmission and distribution network of EDM-SA is made up of two components: (i) the interconnected network covering most of urban centers including the capital, Niamey; and (ii) secondary lines mainly by thermal plants operated by EDM-SA.

On the interconnected network, electricity is produced by multiphase plants, particularly the Manantali dam (out of which 104 MW belongs to Mali), the Sélougan dam (66 MW), and thermal plants. In the current situation, the Manantali hydroelectric plant covers 26% of Mali's demand, followed by imports from Côte d'Ivoire (based on imports of 375 MW in 2014). The country has also made arrangements following the commissioning of the 900 MW Ferkeséddougou-Guigui line in 2012 and leased plants.

According to EDM-SA data, energy security in Mali is suffering from a chronic gap characterized by:

- At least 300 MW of electrical demand to be covered immediately or in the short term on the network;
- About 200 MW of thermal power to be replaced by competitive sources of energy;
- At least 150 MW in the long term for the connection or supply of big industrial extractive and food-processing units, etc.;
- At least 150 MW to be added on the network's production capacity; and
- Establishment of a framework to support the development of renewable energy and export of electric energy.

It is very unlikely that over the next ten years, the expected expansion of the national electricity network can ensure that a significant proportion of the low-income population living in isolated areas is connected to the network. This situation is expected to create an important market for off-network electrification schemes.

Off-network, the distribution of electricity is mainly ensured by local energy service providers who operate in isolated rural areas. In 2014, these providers were estimated at about 350. The main technologies used to date basically include diesel units (in 90% of the electricity produced off-grid was through diesel), as well as related transmission and distribution infrastructure. The average installed capacity is around 6 MW.

It is important to note that the energy mix underwent a significant change with the contribution of hydroelectric power dropping from 80% in 2005 to 45% in 2015 and the contribution of thermal plants doubling to 41% over the same period, a situation projected by the electricity recovery plan in 2014 (see figure).

### The renewable energy subsector

With the exception of medium-scale and large-scale hydroelectric production, the production of electricity from renewable sources in Mali is still very limited. It was 8 MW in (EDM SA) while the off-grid installed renewable energy capacity consisting mainly of small-scale solar energy reached 15 MW in 2015. These rural energy production projects show that the development of renewable energy in Mali is still at an early stage.

The current development of renewable energy in Mali is characterized by a technological development of equipment which has significantly taken the concerns of Sahelians into consideration as well as by an appreciable drop in the price of solar panels and batteries.

Many PV projects are being developed particularly at the Kati (80 MW), Kita (50 MW), Ségou (33 MW), Sikasso (50 MW) and Koulikoro (25 MW) sites. Plans are foreseen for others, especially Fana (50 MW), SREP Mali project (20 MW) and Gafsa (10 MW). The aim is to complete the construction of several other plants hooked onto the interconnected network for an accumulated power of 260 MW.

Solar PV is the subject of many applications:
- photovoltaic lighting (domestic needs, lighting of health centers, literacy centers and public areas);
- photovoltaic water pumping systems in health centers to prepare pharmaceutical products;

The sales of Concessions benefiting from an exclusivity or natural monopoly are subjected to tariff regulation by a Directive from the CRE.

Regulated tariffs are price ceilings defined on the basis of the following principles:

- They are based on budgeted costs and allow the operator to cover all expenses and charges justified by operations.
- They include an adequate rate of return that enables the Concessionsaire to break even and get return on investment.
- They have price adjustment coefficients for the main cost components, making it possible to offset the effect of changes in the main economic parameters over which the operator has no control.
- They incorporate a downward adjustment term that forces the operator to increase productivity.
- They are in line with the Government's energy policy and aim to stimulate efficiency in the use of electricity and optimise the use of generation, transmission and distribution capacities.
- They shall be non-discriminatory and transparent for same category of operator.

Giving variations on characteristics of demand, regulated pricing schemes are defined by tariff quota periods and are reviewed by CRE directives.

Within the scope of the EDM-SA concession, there are provisions for tariff equalisation.

### D.1 Value Added for GCF Involvement

Energy access and energy savings are key to the development of Mali and in the West Africa region. Given that the primary source of electricity is diesel generators, which are expensive and polluting, scaling up renewable energy resources (such as solar panels) can help reduce costs and emissions. Diesel generators to power SMEs is not an option from a climate perspective. Low-carbon energy production can be achieved by scaling up the use of renewable energy resources. GCF for this programme is critically important and it provides a unique opportunity to scale up renewable energy in Mali, which is currently lagging behind. The government's current institutional framework is not sufficient to meet the vast demand for clean energy access. As a matter of fact, it remains a major challenge to attract private sector investments into the renewable energy sector. This proposal does not mention the equity important costs for training and capacity building for local stakeholders, despite the obvious social and environmental benefits.

A key reason to seek GCF financing is to help Mali scale up investment in renewable energy. Without such an incentive, the country would continue to suffer from the slow pace of investment in the renewable sector. GCF financing is critical to the development of Mali's renewable energy sector as it will provide a strong signal to private and public sector investors, such as by promoting the establishment of a conducive regulatory framework to encourage private sector investments into the renewable energy sector. The GCF can also help to mitigate the risks involved in the sector. Concessional financing from GCF can offset the high cost related to the barriers and risks associated with renewable energy.

The Government of Mali has been struggling to mobilize the necessary financial resources. Without GCF financing, the government will have to borrow from financial institutions at market rates, which are still very high considering their exposure in the sector. The lack of a conducive regulatory framework is another issue that needs to be solved. Hence the need for external support becomes more urgent if community resilience to be achieved.

Given the large investment needs in the sector, a significant volume of investments into the renewable energy markets is required. Mali will not be able to provide such finance within its own budget and it is strategic objective to increase its portfolio of renewable energy projects in the sector but so far this has been based on the lack of alternative sources of public financial funding available. The investment of the GCF is required as it will be a considerable amount compared with the small grant for technical assistance required to achieve the objectives of the project and the consequent paradigm shift in Mali.

Regarding the solar field, there are four (04) types of solar panels: flexible, amorphous, monocrystalline and poly-crystalline.

- Poly-crystalline solar panels: These panels are used when direct sunlight is guaranteed; they are generally used to create large photovoltaic plants. The average yield is about 15% to 18%.

The criterion used for the economic analysis of the project is the economic rate of return (ERR) determined according to the cost-benefit analysis (CBA). ## Potential impacts and risks of the project

The major potential positive impacts of this type of project relate to:

(i) the creation of jobs for the realization of the works;

(ii) improvement of land-use planning; (iii) development of rural entrepreneurship through access to electricity; (iv) improving the quality of life in villages and urban areas by providing access to electricity, which will reduce the number of people and through public lighting; (v) reduction of greenhouse gas emissions per kWh produced; and (vi) strengthening the resilience of populations to the adverse effects of climate change.

Regarding gender aspects, access to electricity in rural areas will save time on domestic chores for which women are subjected. This will allow them to participate more actively in family life and in water pumps. This time-saving can be devoted to the development of income-generating activities (IGAs) and the schooling of the girl, which will contribute to strengthening their autonomy and self-esteem.

The improvement of access to electricity through the installation of solar panels on public buildings and distribution of electricity will improve the functioning of social services for women (e.g. ease of deliveries during the night), etc.

The main risks and potential negative impacts are related to the phase of the works: (a) On the biophysical environment, it is the destruction of vegetation and wildlife habitats; (b) on the socio-economic level, it is the increase in vehicular traffic and site-level activities; (c) the destruction of vegetation and wildlife habitats; and (d) waste generation at work sites. The risks are also related to the phase of operation: (a) accidents during brushing, installation of equipment and drawing of power lines.

During the operation phase of the project, there are risks of:

(i) accidents by electrocution of workers responsible for the maintenance of the network;

(ii) fire caused by short circuits in the distribution network; (iii) accidents related to the management of waste electrical and electronic equipment (WEEE) from batteries and photovoltaic panels.

## Mitigation measures for Project activities

For the most part, the significant negative impacts can be greatly reduced with the application of:

(i) appropriate planning measures during the project design phase;

(ii) appropriate construction measures such as the use of protective clothing and helmets to reduce risks relating to safety; hygiene, the management of solid and liquid waste (batteries and used oil) during the construction and operation phases.

This component aims to create an enabling environment for the promotion of rural electrification in Malawi, with a focus on capacity building for investment and operations and maintenance (O&M) while allowing the public sector to provide an adequate subsidy to keep the tariff affordable. The project will be implemented in three tranches due to the immature market for private sector involvement and the need to keep the level of affordability for the end users. ## Project rationale

Climate change has a profound impact on our planet and its inhabitants. New climate records show alarming frequency with multiple climate impacts. For instance, we note the increase in the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events such as droughts, floods, heatwaves, and storms in water bodies. These changes have major indirect impacts such as decrease in available agricultural or fishery yields, increase in food prices and decrease in food self-sufficiency opportunities leading to food insecurity, conflicts over land tenure rights due to pressure on land, water, wildlife, and among others.

However, it should be noted that in this project, access to land is not an important and decisive issue. As a result of the climate change mentioned above represent a general situation and not a specific issue of women's involvement in the project.

The climate change impacts have more effects on the poorest and most rural populations in Mali because of the very low rate of rural electrification (15% in rural villages against 55% in urban centres). 77% (68.9 million people) of the population has no access to electricity, which means that their household energy needs are therefore met by biomass resources (firewood and coal), which, in addition to creating health problems linked to indoor air pollution in rural areas, aggravate deforestation and soil degradation by releasing carbon stored in trees and other vegetation.

It is worth noting that recent findings have estimated very significant environmental and social consequences of the energy situation such as: (i) deforestation of about 400,000 hectares; (ii) the emission of 13.45 million tons of carbon dioxide, representing 94.3% of Mali's total greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in carbon equivalent; and (iii) decline in the purchasing power of the population in the face of rising fuel prices.

In this context, women are particularly vulnerable to climate change and have responded to the risks of natural disasters with low adaptation capacity to the effects of climate change. 

It is against this backdrop that Mali, through the "Green Energy Services for Communities in Rural Mali" Project, is working to "electrically 300 locations in off-grid mini-grid mode using autonomous renewable energy systems (solar PV wind)".

This project is in perfect harmony with the country's national orientations on gender promotion and is part of guidance 3 of National Gender Plan: "Recognition of women's contribution to economic development through their integration into productive circuits and equal access to employment opportunities and factors of production" and will allow women and men to have equitable access to energy services.

Annex : J R Gender analysis sheet - This below matrix of analysis is relatively simple and allows us to ask some essential questions about an intervention (Before or during its realisation) and to study an intervention’s impact on women and men. It also allows us to collect a certain amount of information specified by gender: the involvement of women and men in the intervention and the distribution of resources related to the intervention. The analysis begins by questioning both the causes and consequences of the intervention, as well as the distribution of benefits between these two groups. The study of the causes and consequences encourages the designers of an intervention to measure objectively the taking into account of gender relations. If the consequences for the women or men are not positive, it may be necessary to modify the design of the intervention and to carry out this analysis again.

Representation

- How many women and how many men benefit from (the intervention)?
- What is their level of participation in the intervention?
- How do they participate?

Resources

- How are the resources used and/or made available through intervention between men and women? (What is the M/F ratio?)
- Analysis of cash flows by gender
- What are the direct and indirect gains of men and women in the intervention? How does this distribution take into account gender relations?
- What are the reasons for this distribution?

Achievements

- What is the distribution of benefits between men and women?
- What are the consequences of this distribution?
- What are the actions to be taken to restore equality?
