Climate change is one of the biggest challenges being faced by India. Between 1901-2018, India’s average temperature has increased at a rate of 0.4°C per decade, and its annual greenhouse gas emissions, which have more than doubled from 1.2 GtCO₂eq in 2005 to 2.6 GtCO₂eq in 2018. The aim of this Programme is to facilitate the reduction of greenhouse gases and other emissions from the road transport sector in India through the accelerated adoption of electric vehicles. Climate context

How has India's climate already changed?

India has varied weather and climatic patterns across the country, with an average temperature of 25°C in Jammu and Kashmir and 30°C in parts of northeastern India. The amount and frequency of rainfall is equally diverse, with parts of Meghalaya receiving more than 4,000 mm of precipitation annually, compared to parts of Thar Desert in Rajasthan which receive less than 100 mm per year.

India's climate has been significantly affected by two weather systems: South-West Monsoon (June to September) which brings the majority of the rainfall in the country, and North-East Monsoon (October to December) which plays an important role in southern India. The monsoons are crucial for agriculture as they provide most of the annual rainfall.

India's climate has been changing over time. According to a recent Indian government report, the average temperature across the country increased 0.5°C over the last century. Even though the temperature increase is lower than the global average, severe impacts are already being felt.

Between 1985 and 2009, eastern and southern India experienced 50% more heatwaves than in the previous 25 years. The frequency of heavy rainfall has also increased by about 30%, leading to flooding in many parts of the country, leading to death of thousands and displacement of millions.18 This is in contrast to its decline in average precipitation during this period.

The Himalayan glaciers are retreating at an accelerated rate, with some estimates suggesting that the Gangotri glacier has retreated by 20 km since 1954. The Kedarnath glacier, which has retreated at an average 18 metres per year over the last four decades, is further, with rising temperatures leading to more frequent and intense monsoons, and variations in rainfall patterns across the country, water becoming more scarce with each passing day. Today, many people in India experience severe water scarcity for at least one month each year, with 180 million facing water scarcity all year round.20

How will India's climate continue to change?

As climatic conditions become more extreme, they are likely to be more proliﬁc. For India, the intensity of impact of climate change will rise due to rising levels of greenhouse gases, led by rapid population growth, urbanisation, strong industrial growth and a growing middle class.

According to one recent report, the level of precipitation, especially during monsoons, and glaciers in the Hindu Kush region will signiﬁcantly reduce over the next few decades. Consequently, the ﬂow of water in some of the main river systems such as the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra is likely to decline by 2050 compared with their levels today compared to the 2000s. This is expected to result in severe water scarcity and displacement of more than 270 million people in the India and Brahmaputra basins. ## The role of transport sector in emission generation

### Present status

According to an Asian Development Bank (ADB) report, the transport sector accounts for 23% of all energy-related CO2 emissions and is the second largest GHG emitter after the power sector. India's transport sector is the third largest GHG emitting sector in the country. Further, as per IEA, carbon dioxide emissions from India's transport sector have increased by about 10% between 2005-06 and 2018-19.

### Key points

* a significant increase in passenger and freight transport activity - Passengers and freight traffic by inland mode have both tripled between 2000 to 2010 to 3.1 billion passenger kms and 4.1 billion tonne km respectively.
* the sector's high reliance on fossil fuels - The Indian transport sector has grown 50% since 2010, and now accounts for about 60% of total energy use in India. This is mainly due to a significant increase in the amount of energy used in the transport sector, with natural gas, biofuels, etc., comprising of the remaining portion.
* Further, as the energy use has increased over the last two decades, GHG emissions too have increased, implying a strong correlation between energy use and carbon dioxide emissions.

## Going forward

To limit the impact of growing GHG emissions from India's road sector, a multi-pronged approach is required. According to the National Climate Action Tracker, the electrification of vehicles or "electricity essential" in India is to achieve net zero emissions from its road sector by 2050. ## Air quality and reduced emissions (PSS: Resource efficiency)

EVs do not have any emission from its operations as only electricity is consumed. This significant advantage over the petrol/diesel driven vehicles as vehicle emission contributes to a significant proportion of pollution in all major cities, including India.

India has a large installed base of solar power plants which can be used for charging of batteries in the EVs, given India's grid still has reliance on conventional energy sources. However, this will not be sufficient to meet the demand for charging infrastructure and hence there is a need for the integration more renewables, which will support the decarbonisation of the transport sector over time.

The Platform will also work with governments and other stakeholders in India to encourage green-only charging points will also be explored.

The Platform will seek best efforts to investigate and pursue opportunities for fiscal incentives to reduce emissions from electric vehicles. This may include installation of solar renewables (e.g., rooftop solar), disposable storage, procurement of renewable electricity etc.

The Platform will incorporate into its policies and stakeholder management program the national policy on electric mobility in India as well as other relevant policies and regulations.

## Hazardous waste (including e-waste) (PSS: Resource efficiency)

EVs do not require handling of hazardous materials because they do not contain engine oil and conventional batteries are not used for energy storage in conventional vehicles.

However, EV batteries that include other components such as cobalt, graphite, and nickel are an important component of an EV. At the end of the battery lifespan, there is a battery scrap which contains valuable metals such as lithium, cobalt, nickel, manganese oxide and nickel. These substances, if not recycled or treated adequately can be hazardous to human health and the environment.

The Platform will work with governments and other stakeholders in India to ensure that these materials are handled safely and disposed of properly. This includes ensuring that they do not enter into landfills where they may leach toxic chemicals into soil and moisture and can lead to major landfill explosions.

As a result, there is currently no regulatory frameworks for the end-of-life treatment or recycling of e-waste related to EVs, however these are rapidly evolving and options for use, recycling and disposal of EV batteries in India will be explored. The Platform will work with governments and other stakeholders in India to ensure that these materials are handled safely and disposed of properly.

The Platform will also develop a battery scrapage policy aligned with government guidelines and will require counterparties to comply with the same. Government approved scrapage facilities will be identified and shared with the Platform's members.

Gender mainstreaming, as defined by the United Nations Economic and Social Council, refers to a strategy for making women’s, as well as men’s, concerns and experiences integral dimensions of policies and programmes in all areas and at all levels. Gender equality is essential to ensure that women and men benefit equally from a certain system, and inequality is not perpetuated.

## Need for Gender Assessment and Gender Action Plan

Gender mainstreaming is central to the GCF’s objectives and guiding principles. The aim is to address a continuing gender gap in climate change policy, finance, and action by ensuring that the design, development and implementation of strategies and activities to be financed: The GCF Governing Instrument states that “The GCF shall promote the integration of gender equality into all its policies, programs, operations promoting environmental, social, economic, and development benefits, taking not only a gender-sensitive but also a gender-responsive approach.”

Gender mainstreaming, as defined by the United Nations Economic and Social Council, refers to a strategy for making women’s, as well as men’s, concerns and experiences integral dimensions of policies and programs in all areas and at all levels. Gender equality is essential to ensure that women and men benefit equally from a certain system, and inequality is not perpetuated.

There is often a heavy linking of the term “gender” with “women’s issues”; however, the evolved understanding on gender and gender mainstreaming is that Gender represents equality of both the sexes.

However, GCF has established on the basis of prevalent practices and past experiences that women and girls are disproportionately affected by the impacts of climate change and associated severe weather events. Women and girls are more vulnerable to the adverse effects of climate change and climate-induced impacts due to natural disasters like floods and earthquakes exacerbate women’s poverty and unpaid domestic and care work burdens. Gender inequality, exacerbated by climate change, is a major barrier to women’s empowerment. The lack of access to resources and the vulnerability of women to climate change stems from social norms and discrimination that result in the intersectionality of gender with other forms of discrimination such as race, ethnicity, age, class, and control over land and other productive assets; fewer legal rights; lesser mobility and lesser political and professional opportunities.

The updated policy emphasizes gender responsive approaches as essential to this process, including the avoidance of the risk of only identifying gender issues or ensuring a “do no harm” approach, a process will substantially help to overcome historical gender biases. Gender responsive approaches are essential to this, including balanced and actions by women and men.

2.1.2 Definition of Sub-Projects and Counterparties

The Platform will provide financing to counterparties in the e-mobility space (also referred to as a "transactional partner") for the acquisition or lease of electric buses, charging stations, and three-wheelers, changing infrastructure and others. Key characteristics of the sub-projects include:

* **physical assets in the e-mobility space are involved (e.g., financing a set number of electric buses).**
* **vehicles or charging points will be operated by the counterparty.**
* **the vehicle acquisition or lease contract and the counterparty will be governed by a financing agreement that will place certain obligations on both parties.**
* **vehicles financed may not be dedicated to a specific route but rather used by the counterparty generally.** This is subject to specific diligence.

For the avoidance of doubt:

* The Platform is designed to be a financing intervention. Sub-projects will not involve the design or development of vehicles, charging infrastructure, or other physical assets in the e-mobility space on the e-mobility sector development from a financing perspective (outside the scope of a sub-project).
* The Platform will not be involved in the day-to-day operation of vehicles or charging infrastructure.
* The Platform will not be involved in the design or manufacturing of vehicles.

Given this definition, the Platform will not provide funding for the design and/or development and implementation of each sub-project. Instead, the Platform will provide financing (governed by financing documentation such as lease or loan contracts) to Counterparties defined as entities who will be responsible for the day-to-day operation of electric buses.

Examples of counterparties include:

* A bus operating company which is bidding for a new electric bus tender issued by a state government for the purpose of providing service on urban areas.
* A private bus company operating a route between two major cities.
* A company constructing a number of new charging points to support the increased penetration of e-vehicles in a new area.
* A supply chain company providing a fleet of cars to their employees.


2.1.3 Limitations

* **Baseline data:** There is no baseline provided as most activities require the Platform to be established. For other activities, there is limited data available in the sector and the mandate of the program is broad enough that it would not be possible to provide a baseline for all activities. Some activities are context-specific. There are activities in the action plan which relate to increasing data availability and, where applicable, setting sex-disaggregated baselines and targets. For the avoidance of doubt, all data collection, storage, and utilization of sex-disaggregated data will be compliant with local data privacy laws.
