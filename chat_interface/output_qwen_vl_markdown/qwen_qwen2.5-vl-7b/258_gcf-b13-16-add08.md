### A.2 Project (Agriculture and Water Management) (Total: 30 MUSD)

1. The proposed project supports Government of Sri Lanka (GoSL) to strengthen the resilience of vulnerable communities in the Dry Zone through integrated water management, including climate-resilient crop production, erratic rainfall, and extreme events attributable to climate change. It will address technical, financial and institutional barriers related to achieving integrated water management to improve agriculture-based livelihoods of smallholder farmers, particularly women, in the Dry Zone. The project will also contribute towards increasing access to safe drinking water for 179,800 people by strengthening the community irrigation water infrastructure and associated agricultural practices, scaling-up decentralized drinking water systems, and promoting climate-resilient crop production.

2. The objective of the project is to strengthen the resilience of smallholder farmers, particularly women, in the Dry Zone through enhanced integrated water management and climate-resilient crop production. This will result in increased resilience of health and well-being, and food and water security and increased resilience and empowerment of women. The project will also contribute towards increasing access to safe drinking water for 179,800 people by strengthening the community irrigation water infrastructure and associated agricultural practices, scaling-up decentralized drinking water systems, and promoting climate-resilient crop production.

3. Project benefits include about 770,500 direct and 1,179,800 indirect beneficiaries who will gain from improved water management and climate-resilient crop production in the Dry Zone of Sri Lanka.

The project targets women-headed female-headed households and unemployed youth. Post-conflict investments in roads, schools, hospitals and housing have failed to address the needs of these communities due to the continued livelihood vulnerability and heightened exposure to impact of climate variability and extreme events.

### 4. Adaptation interventions in this project target largely public goods – village level irrigation systems, decentralized drinking water supply and early warning systems. As the project targets the very vulnerable and poor, there is a low likelihood that private sector investors will be willing or able to provide financing for these projects (with some repairs that are partly borne by the community-based organizations). There is no short or medium term prospect of private sector investment in this area. The government has already committed to providing the bulk of the capital investment required to build resilience to climate change in rural Dry Zone villages is prohibitive for a government that is constrained by its own fiscal space and an unfavourable balance of payments. Domestic financing is inadequate to meet recurrent costs and debt repayments.

### 5. GCF resources, are, therefore, critical to overcome these barriers that constrain GCF’s ability to advance integrated adaptation solutions in the Dry Zone. The project will address the following key barriers to scaling up from pilot to climate change impacts. These barriers (detailed in Section C2) include: limited community capacities to design integrated adaptive solutions; lack of institutional capacity and coordination among government agencies and communities; low level of awareness and climate change risks, impacts, and adaptation solutions related to water management; limited technical capacity for the implementation of climate-resilient irrigation systems; limited institutional capacity for decentralized drinking water supply; weak institutional coordination to implement a river basin approach in village irrigation cascade systems; and Limited financial capacities of community and agencies and government entities to sustainably meet the incremental costs of adaptation.

### 6. Current financing gaps are significant, as financing is hampering GCF’s ability to replicate successful projects and overcome these barriers that constrain GCF resources from vulnerable communities’ farmers in Sri Lanka’s Dry Zone will continue to face adverse impacts of climate change on their lives and livelihoods, steepening them further into poverty and food insecurity. The project will address the following key financing gaps: limited institutional capacity and financial capacity to deal with current challenges for food, income and household water in a region that has above-normal number of river basins; limited institutional capacity for decentralized drinking water supply and generating meteorological information and advisories for flood and drought. Combinations of these factors have led to significant allocations to enhance water security and agricultural productivity for the vulnerable communities in three river basins of the Dry Zone, including: upgrading and enhancing resilience of village irrigation systems (small scale rainwater storage reservoirs and groundwater recharge structures); enhancing climate-resilient decentralized water supply and management solutions to provide safe, year round drinking water; strengthening climate and hydrological observing and forecasting systems to enhance water management and adaptive capacity of small farmers in droughts and floods.

In addition, the income accrued to CBSOs from water management activities will be used to cover operational costs of CBSOs. The project will also seek to generate cover operation and maintenance costs for the infrastructure, support staff resources, and maintain a reserve fund that is used for contingency purposes. # Detailed Project / Programme Description

## CL1: Strategic Context

### Country Overview

10. Sri Lanka’s recent economic gains, following the end of a debilitating 30-year war and the aftermath of the 2004 Indian Ocean Tsunami, are being threatened due to its increasing vulnerability to climate change which is characterized by increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events such as droughts, floods, heatwaves, and cyclones. The country’s economy heavily depends on agriculture for food income. IPCC’s fifth assessment report predicts that South Asia, including Sri Lanka, is likely to experience more frequent and intense flooding and droughts in the future due to climate change. These shifts in its seasonal rainfall patterns accompanied by increased food and drought in the last decade directly impacting rural livelihoods.

11. While categorized as a middle-income country, Sri Lanka carries a complicated situation with deep regional disparities in wealth and wellbeing. About 5.2 million people—equivalent to a quarter of the population—are estimated to be living below the poverty line (30% of the total population). The unemployment rate is high: youth unemployment at 19%, low participation of women in the labour force and large-scale migration in search of employment all indicate a high level of unevenness in growth and opportunity across the provinces and districts. Poverty and social exclusion are most pronounced among rural communities, particularly those living in remote areas. The districts affected by conflicts in the Northern and Eastern Provinces and peripheral districts are most deprived where many years of economic stagnation have left them with limited access to basic services.

### Dry Zone Context – agricultural livelihoods, poverty, and conflict

12. The Dry Zone is one of three climatic zones (dry, intermediate and wet) which divide Sri Lanka on the basis of its precipitation patterns. It covers 30% of the country’s land area and is the country’s agricultural heartland and the main area where the staple rice is grown. Small-scale farmers cultivate a wide range of crops in this zone including paddy, vegetables, fruits, pulses, oilseeds, and cash crops. The livelihoods of people living in the Dry Zone are also related to agriculture – e.g. agricultural marketing, transport, and financial services. About two-thirds of the population live on less than 1 hectare of land per household. Most small-scale farmers cultivate rainfed rice and other crops which are vulnerable to climate change impacts such as droughts and floods, collectively referred to as irrigation schemes. A number of studies confirm that smaller farmers cultivating rainfed rice are more vulnerable to climate change impacts compared with larger farmers who have access to major irrigation schemes. Such farmers are much more vulnerable to impacts of climate change than farmers cultivating under larger irrigation systems. As productivity and crop yields decline due with water availability and unreliable rains resulting from climate change, small-scale farmers in the Dry Zone will be forced to sell their land or migrate which have to be met by buying food for consumption, increasing the level of indebtedness and further eroding their capacity to adapt.

13. While the impacts of the conflict were experienced throughout the country (e.g. suicide bombing and attacks on public places, economic downturn, social issues with war casualties), several districts in the Dry Zone were directly affected by the conflict. The most severely affected areas are the Northern Province, Eastern Province, and parts of the Eastern Province and peripheral districts such as Puttalam, Anuradhapura, Monaragala and Polonnaruwa were deeply divided during the conflict. These districts have been isolated from the rest of Sri Lanka for over two decades by the country. The end of the war in 2009 has allowed many of these districts to re-enter the economic mainstream. However, several socio-economic challenges remain. The most significant is that the livelihoods and food security of people living in conflict-affected areas are still highly vulnerable due to the lack of access to basic services and infrastructure. Recurrent droughts and floods have also been a major challenge for these communities. The frequency of conflict with severe impacts on food security, income, and water for drinking and sanitation of displaced/resettled communities as well as those living in remote border districts

**Visceral Leishmaniasis (VL):**
- One of the biggest water-related health issues in the Dry Zone is the high incidence and geographical spread of Chronic Kidney Disease of Unknown Aetiology (CKDu).
- The exact cause of the disease has not been identified, but stress related dehydration may be a result of a combination of factors including poor access to clean drinking water.
- Water is a contributing factor. The disease has affected 81 Districts in 11 Dry Zone districts with the number of patients being highest in Anuradhapura and Trincomalee Districts.

**Healthcare:**
- CKDu is more prevalent among male farmers between the ages of 35–50, which has left women widowed and children orphaned in many areas of the Dry Zone.
- The disease is also linked to poor sanitation, lack of access to clean water, inadequate hygiene practices such as livestock drinking and other domestic uses. There are a higher percentage of women-headed households in the project targets districts compared to the national average of 23% and many women are caring for sick and disabled family members.

**Water Security:**
- Water security in the dry zone is also hampered by lack of weather, climate and hydrological information which can be used for agriculture and water management. Farmers do not currently receive seasonal forecasts on which to base decisions about planting or harvesting.
- The project aims to address this issue through early warning systems using early warnings of extreme rainfall, which would allow them to plan discharges and flood mitigation measures to limit damage from flooding.

**Baseline Investments:**
- The government of Sri Lanka invests close to a USD 150 million in its annual capital budget for rural development programmes.
- Around USD 68.7 million of underlying and parallel financing is invested by GOS, to meet regular needs.
- Total investments in the Dry Zone are USD 234.9 million (USD 169.2 million from GOS and USD 65.7 million damaged V/S USD 27.3 million).
- Investments in developing and improved seeds and planting material (7 million).
- New rural water supply schemes (10,000) with new water supply infrastructure (48,000).
- CKDu control (salt drinking water and disease treatment): USD 13 million.
- Improved water safety infrastructure (0.5 million).
- Improved forecasting and disaster preparedness (2 billion).
- National Food Security Drive and Safe Agriculture Promotion Programme (USD 67.9 million).

**Other Investments:**
- There have also been many other investments in the past in water management in the Dry Zone including through donor support.

170.
