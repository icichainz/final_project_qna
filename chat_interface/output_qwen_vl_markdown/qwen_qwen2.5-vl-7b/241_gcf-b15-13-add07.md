### B.1. Description of Financial Elements of the Project / Programme

The proposed project is a grant-based proposal to scale-up restoration and sustainable management of critical wetlands in Uganda, a low-income country. A grant-based project makes sense in this case for a number of reasons. First and foremost, the proposed interventions are not commercially viable without significant public sector investment. Second, implementation of solutions that have long been recognized as necessary for improved wetlands management, especially in the context of rural livelihood opportunities, is often constrained by lack of political will (and sometimes even by human) among relevant national, sub-national and local level actors. Other significant political and institutional barriers also exist to scaling up these interventions at a national scale. Third, there are no private sector actors willing or able to make any required investment in the majority of the required solutions. The potential for private sector engagement is nascent in the country. Third, as such, public finance is necessary.

Consequently, public financing in particular by the GCF will be necessary. Without this grant support, the market will not result in any finance that can meaningfully lead to the requisite changes. That said, the use of grant financing for revenue based solutions (e.g., carbon credits) is not appropriate and could have a negative impact on private sector generation, especially in the context of rural livelihood opportunities. While such revenue generating opportunities are not conducive for servicing a loan (an alternative albeit unusual) way of financing the interventions—especially in the context of Uganda’s low-income status—the GCF has been generous enough to allow this project to be co-financed by the wetlands at the local level. The co-finance for this project has been revised according to the GCF guidance, the new figure stands at USD 12.5 million (of which USD 7.85 million is in local currency). This will cover all costs and outputs of this project. The Govt of Uganda co-financing is also now additional and would not have existed without this project.

The limited public financing available is hindering Uganda’s ability to implement adaptation measures to restore critical ecosystem services for resilient livelihoods in the country. The Government of Uganda (GoU), as a Low Income Economy (as per World Bank classification) has access to concessional funding from the GCF (100% grant resources). At USD 5 billion, seeks maximum concessionality from the GCF (100% grant resources) to underpin the proposed adaptation investments.

The project targets highly vulnerable populations—more than half of whom are women, living in disaster prone and food insecure districts dependent on climate sensitive and marginal livelihoods. Through this project, Uganda would be able to achieve the following:

- Restore critical wetlands to improve ecosystem services such as ground water recharge, flood control, fishing and biodiversity conservation.
- Diversify livelihoods and agriculture to make it more resilient to climate shocks, by enhancing the skillset of beneficiaries for employability and adaptation.
- Empower communities that are most at risk in order to be better prepared to climate related disasters. This will be done through participatory and decentralized early warning systems and capacity development for involvement in disaster risk reduction.

The GoU seeks UNDP’s support as the accredited entity to access the grant resources, as direct access mechanisms are not established in the country. While Uganda is working on developing direct access capacity, including through support from the GCF, it will be in a position to use this option for some years to come.

### C.1: Strategic Context

Uganda has shown great economic improvements in the past few years, with growth rates averaging 5.5 percent over the last five years and poverty rate falling from 60% to 38%. Only 18.0% of the population is employed. The remaining 72% are largely engaged in subsistence agriculture. Agriculture remains the backbone of Uganda's economy, employing about 90% of the rural population. Women make up a large proportion of this workforce, informally, 77% of whom are women and, 65% of whom are youth. As of 2013, 19.7% of the population was living below the poverty line (World Bank, 2014). Uganda has a total land area of about 241,000 km², out of a total population of 28 million in the country. wetlands provide subsistence employment for over 2.4 million people.

As outlined in more detail in the first section of the feasibility study, several climate changes will lead to specific impacts on Uganda's ecosystems and livelihoods. These include increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events (these stresses, both human and environmental, are further examined in section C.2 below). The 2.4 million people dependent on wetlands for their livelihoods, as well as countless more who are indirectly dependent on ecosystem services provided by wetlands, will be affected by these changes. According to the National Adaptation Programme of Action (NAPA) (2007), projected drought conditions could lead to disappearance of wetland areas, which will have a significant impact on livelihoods and food security for millions of people who depend on wetlands for livelihoods. Further, when rain-fed agriculture fails due to drought conditions, farmers encroach on wetlands, further destroying them. The NAPA also notes that climate change will lead to increased frequency and intensity of floods, which will have a significant impact on the management of wetland ecosystems (which have their own climate change adaptation services), and enhancing resilience in these areas is critical for ensuring food security and livelihoods.

The policy context for this project is largely tied for its comprehensive impact in this area. Recent national policies and plans related to climate change have put in place a robust enabling environment for efforts on wetland restoration and resilient livelihoods. Sustainable wetland management is promoted in the Vision 2040, which also recognizes that Uganda's development will be based on the principles of sustainable development. The NAPA outlines adaptation strategies across all sectors to ensure the country is resilient to impacts of climate change. This is further substantiated by the National Development Plan (NDP) for 2015-2019 and the National Climate Change Strategy for the period 2014/2015 to 2030 and serves as a guide for investment planning, budget allocation and social mainstreaming of climate change. The NDP is aligned with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and supports the overall theme of the "Growth, Employment and Prosperity for Socio-Economic Transformation." Specifically, in the area of environment, the NDP aims to achieve the following: "Reduce greenhouse gas emissions by 20% and reduce degradation," "increase the functionality and usage of meteorological information systems," and "increase the country's resilience to impacts of climate change. Overall, the NAPA recognizes that addressing the challenges of climate change is critical for enhancing Uganda’s development trajectory and supports this project in line with the National Climate Change Policy (NCCP), and ensures the development strategy is aligned with implementation of the NCCP.

The NCPF of Uganda (2014) aims to help meet Vision 2040's goals through strategies and actions that address both sustainable development and climate change. The NCCP supports the integration of climate change issues into planning, decision-making processes, and policies at all levels. In line with the East African Community (EAC) regional policy, this national policy emphasizes climate change adaptation as its top priority. Uganda's National Adaptation Programme of Action (NAPA), 2007, is specifically targeted at wetlands: "To promote long-term wetland conservation and restoration of degraded wetlands so that they can continue to provide ecosystem services for the benefit of present and future generations, enhancing development needs of communities and the country." In addition, the policy also prioritizes the need to ensure climate resilience in Uganda's ecosystems. The NAPA further emphasizes the importance of sustainable land management and ensuring sustainable management of water resources. These are all supported under this proposed project. In fact, the NCCP has developed a costed implementation strategy, which provides more detailed action plan and roadmap.

The projected targeted sites (the eastern and southwestern Wetlands Basin) indicate that most of the freshwater inflows pass through these areas during the dry season, with little or no water over the rivers. However, over the last three decades, climate change impacts, as well as other baseline (non- climatic) issues such as excessive sedimentation and non-native species invasions, have resulted in substantial water quality degradation.

As a result, the water table in both eastern and southwestern Wetlands Basin have experienced and are expected to experience significant seasonal drops. Land-use activities such as overexploitation of fish and agricultural lands (ploughing, and extraction of reeds) in the catchment are already proving unsustainable for the wetland areas. These human-induced changes have resulted in a decline in water quality and quantity, which has negatively impacted on ecosystem services. For example, in Kabale District, one of the proposed project target areas in Southwest Uganda, about 58% of wetlands have been converted to agriculture (e.g., rice farming), livestock grazing, and aquaculture production for growing, tree farming, and fish farming). These communities are searching for this land, since soils along the hillsides are becoming increasingly eroded. In addition, these communities are also experiencing a decline in water quality as ecosystems have lost their capacity to retain nutrients and store and filter water. This impacts water access for farming activities such as livestock grazing, aquaculture production, and irrigation of crops. The wetlands in both areas of these wetland ecosystems to adapt to climate change and variability impacts are also grossly compromised, such a flood control, water storage and filtration, and maintaining the microclimate. The project proposes to address these issues by engaging communities in sustainable land management practices that will reduce soil erosion, improve livelihoods, and restore activities, including water harvesting and aquaculture rearing. Restored and reforested wetlands and their catchments will provide a range of ecosystem services such as flood control, water storage and filtration, and maintaining the microclimate. This is achieved by reducing the impacts of flash floods thanks to absorbing the excess water better. The proposed interventions will also reduce soil erosion through improved land management practices that include reforestation, agroforestry, and by regenerating grassland. A restored and improved ecosystem is insufficient on its own to address the impacts of climate change and variability. The structure of the plans will be developed according to Ramsar guidelines for the development of management plans. As a final result concerned management plans for each project area can be developed with the relevant local and regional stakeholders.

### Wetland Rehabilitation

In line with the objectives of the "Extending Wetland Protected Areas through Community Conservation Initiatives (CORWEBI)" was conducted in 2013. The evaluation investigated previous works that were undertaken that were similar since no baseline data on the composition and functioning of wetlands impacts associated this project.

The project will have a number of environmental impacts which will be temporarily restricted during rehabilitation, it will be necessary to undertake earth works to re-establish topsoil in some areas. The earth works will remove sediment that is currently impacting on the wetlands operating effectively. These earth works will be limited to within 10m of the edge of the wetland and will not extend into the wetland itself.

To ensure that the sediment is not mobilized through either wind or more specifically rain events, it will be necessary to prepare an erosion control sediment plan. The plan should consist of measures including but not limited to the installation of check dams to reduce runoff, the use of geotextiles and/or other materials to retain sediment for other works. There will be a cut and fill balance and therefore no additional soil etc. will need to be transported into or out of the wetland itself.

The rehabilitation works around the wetlands will also take place. All banks should be battered to ensure they are completely stable, this will reduce erosion and sedimentation in the water course. The topsoil should be used within the existing catchment as topsoil is likely to be richer in nutrients than the existing topsoil.

### H.2. Arrangements for Monitoring, Reporting and Evaluation

140. Project-level monitoring and evaluation will be undertaken in compliance with the UNDP POMR and the UNDP Evaluation Policy. The Project Manager will ensure that all project activities are monitored on a regular basis with the support of the UNDP Country Office. The Project Manager will develop annual work plans, which get approved by UNDP to ensure the efficient implementation of the project. The UNDP Country Office will be responsible for monitoring and evaluating the project in case of any delays or difficulties during implementation, including the implementation of the M&E plan, so that appropriate support and corrective measures can be adopted. The Project Manager will also ensure that all project staff maintain a high level of commitment to the project.

141. The UNDP Country Office will support the Project Manager as needed, including through annual supervision missions. The UNDP Country Office is responsible for complying with UNDP project-level M&E requirements as outlined in the POMR and the Evaluation Policy. The UNDP Country Office will provide technical guidance to the Project Manager by the UNDP Regional Technical Adviser as needed. The project target groups and stakeholders including the NDA Focal Point, the UNDP Country Office, and the UNDP Regional Technical Adviser will be involved in the M&E process.

142. A project inception workshop will be held after the UNDP project document has been signed by all relevant parties to: a) reorient project stakeholders to the project strategy and discuss any changes in the overall context that influence project implementation; b) review the project’s logical framework, including assumptions, risks, constraints, and communication lines and conflict resolution mechanisms; c) review the results framework and discuss reporting, monitoring and evaluation roles and responsibilities and finalize the M&E plan; d) review financial reporting procedures and discuss any changes in the overall context that influence project implementation. The Project Manager will lead these meetings and finalize the first annual work plan. The Project Manager will prepare the inception report no later than one month after the workshop is held, which will be reviewed by the UNDP Country Office and the UNDP Regional Technical Adviser, and will be approved by the Project Board.

143. A project implementation report will be prepared for each year of project implementation. The Project Manager has the responsibility to ensure that all project activities are monitored on a regular basis with the support of the UNDP Country Office. The Project Manager will ensure that the indicators included in the project results framework are monitored annually and will achieve the desired results as per the approved M&E plan. The UNDP Country Office will coordinate the input to the PIR. The Project Manager will prepare the annual PIR, which will be shared with the Project Board and other stakeholders. The UNDP Country Office will coordinate the input of the NDA Focal Point and other stakeholders to the PIR. The quality rating of the previous year’s PIR by the UNDP Regional Technical Adviser will be used as a basis for the current PIR, which will include corresponding management response, will serve as the final project report package. An independent mid-term review will be conducted after 18 months from the start date of the project and the results of this review will be incorporated as recommendations for enhanced implementation during the final half of the project’s duration. The terms of reference, the review process and the final TE report will follow the standard templates and guidelines developed by UNDP. The mid-term review will be conducted by an independent consultant appointed by the UNDP Country Office and the UNDP Regional Technical Adviser, and will be approved by the Project Board.
