# A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

## 1. As a Small Island Developing State in the Caribbean hurricane belt, Belize is highly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. While the most extreme and visible effect is the increased intensity of tropical storms and hurricanes, other effects include more frequent and severe droughts and floods, and documented trends of increased droughts, flooding, and significant rainfall variations. Smallholder farmers are among the most vulnerable as they suffer devastating losses, most dramatically in the occurrence of an extensive dry spell or a prolonged period of heavy rain. The smallholder sector is also highly exposed to the high cost of such events; smallholders' limited investment capacity is fully focused on recovery and purchase of inputs for the next season. In addition, smallholder farmers are often unable to access credit due to their low income.

## 2. The project aims to introduce climate resilient agricultural practices that will allow smallholder farmers to have a sustainable production process and improved market access for their produce, even under the stress of climate change. This is expected to improve the overall resilience of smallholder farming systems in terms of economic resilience. In the context of smallholder farmers in Belize, improved resilience is defined as the capacity to minimize the impacts of climatic and economic shocks, enabling farmers to better control periodic variations and strengthen their ability to adapt to long-term changes. Improved resilience can be achieved through the meaningfully improved smallholders adaptive capacity, significant improvements across the entire value chain are required for this. Smallholder farmers' limited access to credit, lack of technical knowledge, low levels of education, and limited capacity to respond to market demands are all factors that contribute to farmers’ vulnerability. The complex interplay between these factors and their combined effect on smallholders’ adaptive capacity has led to increased resilience and directly addresses climate change threats.

## 3. The proposed Project has two complementary and mutually-reinforcing components: Climate Resilient Value Chain Development (Component 1) and Community Support for Public Climate Resilient Infrastructure (Component 2). These activities support both components. It will initially work in 20 communities clustered in five priority Districts and across the country, including the Belize City Corridor. The project will focus on a range of crops (e.g., corn, not peppers, cabbages, carrots, and onions), one fruit (pineapple), and beekeeping products (principally honey).

## 4. Component 1 will introduce/strengthen smallholder participation in select value chains through the promotion of climate resilient and environmentally sustainable production methods, product diversification, and related income-generating activities. The project will work with a network of trained and certified climate-resilient agriculture specialists working with Ministry of Agriculture (MoA) extension agents. A competitive matching grant fund (MGF), partially funded by the GCF loan, will be structured to support climate resilient production methods and diversification. Smallholder farmers who are members of the project’s community-based members receiving support from technical areas of the project will be eligible for funding. Additionally, this component will provide support for food security, soil conservation and feeding direct benefits through implementation of backyard gardens.

### B.1 Description of Financial Elements of the Project / Programme

The overall goal of the Project is to develop value chains of smallerholder farmers that are resilient and adapted to the effects of climate change. The total Project cost is estimated at USD 20.0 million, including price and physical contributions from the GCF, the Government of Ethiopia (GoE), and private sector partners. These contributions relate to improving climate resilience and marketability of the value chain as well as strengthening organizations and public institutions.

The Project will also cover costs related to investment in technical assistance to improve production, such as roads, drainage and information systems. In addition, there are crosscutting activities of institutional strengthening and research necessary for the development of both components. The table below summarizes the financial elements of the project.

Activities will be financed as follows:

(a) HAD will primarily finance costs related to investment and technical assistance to improve production, value-added activities and improving market access. (b) The GCF will finance the costs of activities related to incorporating innovations and improving knowledge on climate change adaptation and to increase the productive resilience of the beneficiary farmers. The GoE's total contribution will be US$2.2 million, which is allocated as follows: 41% of the total costs of Component 1 (US$5.8 million), 39% of the costs of Component 2 (US$5.5 million) and 5% of Project Management costs (US$0.2 million).

(d) The project participants and PIs will present proposals for funding under the Project to improve resilient production systems, value chains and market access. They are expected to cover counterpart contributions with at least in kind of at least 10%, totaling US$0.8 million of the total cost of Component 1 (for investment and technical assistance).

# Detailed Project / Programme Description

## C1 Strategic Context

There are three important factors that shape Belize’s development and are fundamental to contextualize this Project's proposal.

First is the overarching condition of economic and demographic “smallness” and a high level of public debt, which makes it difficult for Belize to meet its obligations under international agreements. The critical area of poverty reduction. Second, the agricultural sector contributes meaningfully to employment and foreign exchange earnings. Third, Belize has one of the highest levels of inequality in Central America. This is reflected by the fact that 50% of its population lives below or near the poverty line. Finally, the aggravation of climate change, especially in the shade of droughts and floods, is generating annual average losses 2.87% of GDP, pressing the need to implement adaptation measures for the environment.

## Economy and Demography

Belize is a small developing “upper-middle income” country, classified by the United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs as a "small developing state". It has a total land area of 8,867 square miles and an estimated population of about 350,766 inhabitants (2019), with 40% living in urban dwellers. The country is relatively sparsely populated, the total population density being about 16 people per km².

Nominal GDP is estimated in US$3.7 billion in 2018 (ranked 172 out of 194 in The World Bank Development Indicators). Belize’s per capita income is around US $5,400. Since 2006, per capita GDP has reflected at a standard deviation of 13% and the country has experienced significant declines in the last five years. Since the economic and financial crisis of 2008, the economy is characterized by low growth rates, high unemployment, persistent negative balance of payments, high public debt (around 100% GDP) and high levels of poverty, with more than 40% of the population below the poverty line.

Due to inward migration of unskilled labor and outward migration of skilled workers, poverty levels are high in rural areas. The majority of the poor live in rural areas, particularly migrant workers without access to land. Rural poverty rates are highest (more than 70%) in Toledo and Corozal districts. Unemployment remains high, especially among young people, women, and indigenous groups, including men, women, and children. When combined with unemployment, the employment situation is considered to be grim. Poverty most likely to have increased in the last few years due to population growth, decline in real per capita income and the lack of access to social services, education, health care, and other basic needs for people living under conditions.

As an open economy, Belize is highly vulnerable to external shocks and is also significantly affected by climatic and natural disasters. Over the last 15 years, the country has been severely affected by Hurricane both in 2002, which inflicted economic losses totaling approximately 30% of GDP; Hurricane Dan, which struck in 2007 and caused losses equivalent to 90 million dollars (7% of GDP), 64% of which corresponded to the agricultural sector. ### Agriculture Sector

**Agriculture is the main source of rural livelihoods in Belize and an important overall contributor to the national economy. The sector is key to ensuring food security for Belize, in addition to its wider role for rural socio-economic development.**

Belize has an excellent climate for agriculture and abundant water resources.

The climate additionality provided by GCF in the road works, generates a significant improvement in road stability in the face of predicted adverse weather events. Including these adjustments will allow for road investments to last longer than existing dirt roads, which are often washed away during heavy rainfall and storms, thereby reducing loss of products, and high expenditure of machines maintenance.

Therefore, every investment will have to be analyzed on technical parameters that specifically address climate proofing and include activities such as: sections of the road with a slope greater than 12% (i) raising the level of the road to reduce risk of road damage and improve accessibility during flood event. # C6. Regulation, Taxation and Insurance (If applicable)

## Regulation

According to the Department of Environment (DOE) of the Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries, Forestry, Environment & Sustainable Development (MOSA), the EIA system in Belize establishes three categories or schedules (I, II and III) for proposed projects or activities, which may or may not require an Environmental Assessment (EEA) of a Limited Level Environmental Study (LLES). Information submitted is reviewed by the Project Evaluation/EIA Unit of the DOE. Reviews take into consideration the Project Description, Schedule of Works, and other relevant documents as per the Environmental (Amendment) Regulations 2007 as well as the criteria of the Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) Procedure Manual (location, nature, magnitude, etc.). Our environmental and social due diligence process followed on the ground consultations, an internal IFAD screening process, and a review by the Project Evaluation/EIA Unit of the DOE. The final review concluded that none of the infrastructure proposed on the Project would be considered as Schedule I; therefore requiring full EIA.

Nevertheless, some of the proposed activities by the project could be considered as Schedule II such as the improvement of roads less than 2000 meters in length, canalization or flood relief works or packing/canning of vegetable products. In these cases, a Limited Level Environmental Study (LLES) is required; the nature and extent of such studies are determined by the DOE, in consultation with key agencies.

For DOE establishes that some Schedule II projects differ from Schedule I projects only on scale - which is the case of our project. Large-scale infrastructure projects are usually Schedule I while small-scale projects of the same type may fall into Schedule II. In a Schedule II project, the impact may not be as severe as a Schedule I project depending on size, location and other considerations.

All activities that qualify for Schedule III will be undertaken both internally and as accredited by the GCF; no activities qualifying above this categorization will be undertaken.

The Investment and Production Plans will inform the PMU which rural and urban infrastructure are subject to environmental assessment. The Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) documentation (project description, land titles, and others) will be submitted separately to the Department of Environment. It is expected that the first Environmental Clearance will be requested by the second semester of the Project and will be received in the third semester. Upon receipt of the Environmental Clearance and the Water Abstraction License, the Project will have to submit documentation of a Water Abstraction License.

E.4.1. Vulnerability (Adaptation only)

Several studies ([BNSN, 2008] and [GLO, 2002]) have noted that Belize, on the whole, is highly vulnerable to Climate Change. They note further, that the coastal zone of Belize, where a significant percentage of the population is located and where the bulk of economic activity takes place (tourism and agricultural production), is for the most part below the high tide level. As a result, this places the coastal zone in a very precarious position with regards to climate change impacts such as sea-level rise, increased frequency and intensity of storms, and droughts. These will also adversely affect economically important crops in Belize such as sugarcane and citrus. Furthermore, warmer weather from high temperatures and prolonged dry spells will reduce water availability for agriculture and other competing uses resources for water irrigation purposes. Sea level rise will cause flooding and saline intrusion and soil salinization. Rain-fed agricultural production will be affected, demanding improved management techniques and consequently increasing the need for additional investment in irrigation infrastructure to ensure food security and higher food prices. Hence, there is a clear need for additional investment resources to confront the expected impacts of climate change and the additional costs this entails.

The target population of the project is among the most vulnerable in the country. They are highly exposed to climate change and have low adaptive capacity. The limited government support this population receives is not geared towards increasing resilience; hence, they are constantly subject to social and economic losses due to climate induced disasters. This makes them extremely poor and vulnerable and, and painful, and does not allow them for prepare for future events. Without a significant overhaul to the country's approach to rural development, smallholder farmers will continue in a vicious cycle that reinforces their vulnerability.

IFAD has a long lasting history within Central America and the Caribbean dating back to 1978. The Caribbean region is one of the most vulnerable regions globally to climate change impacts, including increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events, as well as constant capacity building and policy support. ### Irrigation

The proposed irrigation investment will have a direct impact on reducing the variability of rainfall patterns caused by climate change. The benefits of this investment will be: (a) high over-all efficiencies of irrigation-dependent production systems; (b) high output and high value per c.h. of water; (c) adequate water supply for all crops, including those that are more sensitive to drought conditions; (d) improved management of water allocation over time and within the scheme; (e) limited losses, for the sake of flow capacity and scour control; (f) predictable and reliable water allocation; (g) good collaboration between the farmers: (i) good access to information on weather forecasts and weather; and (j) good knowledge about management options covering both cultivation and water management.

In the conditions of Belize, a climate resilient irrigation scheme would be characterized by the predictable availability of adequate water at the place and the time when the crops need it. Thus, the scheme must be (i) hydraulically efficient in terms of water allocation; (ii) have sufficient conveyance capacity to carry excess water and erosion; (iii) well operated (for example in terms of water allocation within the scheme); (iv) designed with a good drainage system that can handle the runoff from the fields. There would be a need to consider efficient drainage systems to cope with excess water and erosion. The availability of sufficient water at the place and time when crops need it is also dependent on the conveyance capacity, required conveyance and regulation structures, as well as the system operation set-up designed by the support of an International Technical Assistant.

### Roads

Some sections of the road surface level will be raised to an elevation of about 0.5 m higher than expected flood level to reduce risk of road damage and to prevent an inaccessible road during flood event. In terms of drainage, it is important that the road structure has sufficient capacity to convey water away from the road and erosion from road surface runoff. A good road drainage system is vital for the type of roads under consideration. It shall have capacity to convey water from the surface of the road, as well from the different layers of the road structure. The design should be such that it prevents water from running down the road surface and causes water flowing towards the road and conveys water across the road in a controlled fashion. Furthermore, water must not be allowed to accumulate on the road surface or in culverts. This can be achieved by providing drainage at the culverts or along exposed running surfaces, cut-offs etc. This will prevent excess water within the roadway that will adversely affect the structural integrity of the road and cause damage to other parts of the road structure. Erosion and weakened subgrades followed by a mass failure are all products of inadequate or poorly designed drainage. Different types of drainage structures that could be used in the case of Belize include cross drainage including ditches, culverts, swales, gullies, trenches and drains, rock checkers, cut-off ditches as well as retaining walls, gabions, rip-rap to protect embankments.

### Summary of the Secretariat’s assessment

#### 2.1 Project background

The project aims to increase resilience of smallholder farmers in Belize to climate change impacts, mainly increased droughts, flooding, and significant rainfall pattern variations between wet and dry seasons, which are expected to become more frequent for important commodities for the country. In response to these climate impacts, the project will develop and climate-smart technologies and services that will increase the productivity of smallholder farmers’ ability and resilience.

The project will also increase access to markets through rehabilitation of critical infrastructure. It will deploy grant award and co-lending mechanisms and work with producers' organizations and local communities to meet their needs in addressing the impacts of climate change. Through this project, around 50,000 people in 23 communities in five districts will be supported.

(b) Climate resilient rural infrastructure and assets development (CRRID):

(c) Strengthening organizations and public institutions.

The Program will increase the resilience to climate change of poor rural households to minimize the impacts of climate and economic events on small farmers in 23 communities in five Districts of Belize, while supporting sustainable market access for their products.
