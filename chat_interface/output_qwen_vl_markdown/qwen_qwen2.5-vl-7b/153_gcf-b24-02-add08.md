### A.21 Executive summary (max. 750 words, approximately 1.5 pages)

Oceans and their coastal regions are most severely exposed to the impacts of climate change. Around 50% of the world's population lives in coastal areas, which are also home to many vulnerable communities dependent on marine ecosystems, particularly in developing countries. Mangrove forests and coral reefs protect coastlines against the effects of rising sea levels and storms. They provide critical habitat for millions of species, including those spawning grounds for fish, which is an essential source of protein and income for the local population. In addition to their role as buffers against coastal erosion and shore huge amounts of CO2 and their protection and restoration contributes to climate mitigation.

This GCF Funded Activity (hereafter referred to as the "Programme" or "GCF Programme") will be established as a logical follow-up to the Ecosystem-based Adaptation for Climate Change Mitigation in Coastal Areas (EBA) based Adaptation approaches (EBA) in one of the most vulnerable regions affected by sea level rise, enhanced frequency and intensity of extreme weather events, and increased coastal erosion. The GCF Funded Activity (GCF), World Country Selection (Madagascar, Mozambique, Tanzania and South Africa) is guided by criteria such as vulnerability to climate change, exposure to climate-related risks, the potential for adaptation actions to reduce vulnerability to coastal populations of ecosystem services from coasts and freshwater ecosystems. Special attention is given to the linkages between the ocean approach and the land-based approach in order to ensure synergies and avoided policy trade-offs.

The Programme's objective is to enhance through a coastal zone management based on the conservation and sustainable use of coastal ecosystems, ecosystem services that contribute to reducing climate-related changes and enhancing resilience of vulnerable communities and their livelihoods, particularly those related populations to climate change.

The GCF Funded Activity is an independent non-profit foundation and currently the only global institution exclusively funding local level initiatives in the mainstreamed conservation and sustainable fisheries sectors. It funds NGOs to strengthen its specific knowledge and execution capacities of the BAP in order to fund NGOs with suitable and promising sub-projests, based on EBA for climate change in the Programme region. The GCF Funded Activity will be implemented through a series of sub-projects, each with a sub-project preparation and implementation.

The BAP is designed as a set of guidelines and tools for proposals to be financed with GCF funding as within the scope of the Programmes. These calls will be aligned with GCF result areas and have a specific focus on EBA and related requirements. The BAP will be implemented in accordance with the following principles: (i) alignment with quality of climate rationale, coherence with national and/or regional adaptation plans; see section 8.1 for compliance with the GCF Funded Activity's guidelines and procedures as outlined in the GCF Funded Activity Manual (Annex 2). Every single sub-project will have to demonstrate the location-specific adaptation impact of the intervention and its contribution to climate change mitigation.

- Vulnerable coastal populations will be able to reduce or avoid negative impacts of climate change through a stabilized provision of ecosystem services.
- Important coastal ecosystems will be protected and sustainably managed to ensure adaptation-relevant ecosystem services for vulnerable coastal communities.
- Enhanced knowledge, expertise and capacity of relevant national agencies in using Ecosystem-based Adaptation approaches.

Total costs of the GCF Funded Activity will amount to EUR 50 million, comprised of EUR 30 million applied for from the GCF Trust Fund (GCF TF) and EUR 20 million from the World Bank. # PROJECT/PROGRAMME INFORMATION

## B.1. Context and contact (max: 1000 words, approximately 2 pages)

Coastal regions of the Mozambique Channel / West Indian Ocean (WIO) are facing severe impacts from extreme weather events such as storm surges and coastal erosion. These events cause significant damage to coastal ecosystems which lead to the loss of valuable land and infrastructure. These damages are not only the consequence of extreme weather events but also due to climate change, which is causing sea level rise and increasing the likelihood of people living in coastal regions (in Madagascar 68 % of the population, in Mozambique 50 %) and their high vulnerability to these impacts.

The region is one of the world's most significant marine biodiversity areas and a biological gem pool for the country. It also has the highest value of the island coasts as well as the marine coast. At the same time, the coastal population, including the coastal livelihoods (fisheries, tourism, etc.), is highly vulnerable to these impacts.

The Green Climate Fund (GCF) funded Programme is based on the vulnerability of their coastal population to climate change and their specific productive activities (e.g., fisheries, tourism). The GCF-funded Programme will focus on coastal areas with a high coastal/land area ratio. For this purpose, an internationally widely accepted approach - the University of Notre Dame Global Adaptation Index (GAIN) - has been used (see Annex 8 for more details). GAIN summarizes a country's climate vulnerability and readiness.

Some coastal ecosystems, such as coral reefs, mangroves and sea grass beds, reduce the force of wave action on the coast and subsequent damages to coastal populations (Ecosystem-based Adaptation - EBA). In addition to their relevance in terms of biodiversity conservation, they are also important for human well-being and livelihoods in the region. Mangroves are an important source of firewood and timber; mangroves, coral reefs and sea grass beds are important sources of food and income (e.g., fish, shellfish, etc.). Coral reefs provide a habitat for many species.

In addition, coral reefs are increasingly important for the tourism sector. Last but not least, mangroves, coral reefs, sea grass beds and other coastal ecosystems also play an important role in reducing the impacts of climate change.

In the WIO region, these adaptation-relevant ecosystems are still quite abundant, but rapidly degrading and diminishing in size. The main drivers for this degradation are climate-related pressures such as increasing storm surges or even destroying coral reefs, mangroves and sea grass beds.

The Programme aims to protect and restore adaptation-relevant coastal ecosystems that enhance and enrich their resilience against the impacts of climate change and extreme weather events (e.g., storm surges) on coastal erosion. In order to minimize the pressure on these ecosystems and to ensure the sustainability of the interventions, maintenance activities will be included in the project design.

The intervention not only focuses on individual EBA measures, but also intends to make EBA an integral part of the overall development strategy for the region. This is done by integrating coastal ecosystem conservation and restoration with other climate change adaptation measures such as green and grey adaptation measures into account. This transformative approach goes beyond single projects and contributes to a more integrated and sustainable development.

Without the proposed interventions, it is very likely that damages from storm surges and coastal erosion will increase significantly, as the above-mentioned ecosystems are degrading rapidly. Alternative measures, such as the construction of seawalls or other hard engineering solutions, would not be cost-effective in this region and would not directly support the livelihoods of the vulnerable coastal population. Therefore, we consider the full integration of EBA into development strategies to be a more sustainable approach that is both environmentally sound and economically efficient.

The thematic focus on coastal ecosystems also means that the Programme will have a particular need for adaptation measures given the respective ranks in the ND-Gain Index. At the same time, they face high vulnerability due to climate change and extreme weather events such as storm surges, which rapidly degrading and diminishing in size. The threats to these ecosystems must be addressed by targeted interventions.

#### 2.1 International Context and Priorities

Rising concerns about deteriorating living conditions of coastal populations due to climate change and the degradation of marine ecosystems have led to increased pressure on these habitats. Recognizing the importance of oceans and coasts for sustainable human development, the international community has committed itself through various agreements and initiatives aimed at protecting biodiversity in the oceans, seas and marine resources. ### a) Madagascar

The Republic of Madagascar ratified the UNFCCC and became a Party in 1998. Malagasy states adopted a substantial number of laws on coastal and marine resources, including those related to the adverse effects of climate change. Between 2015 and 2030, losses and damages associated with floods and cyclone events are estimated at about USD 4 billion.

The National Adaptation Plan (NAP) is being developed in four priority sectors: agriculture, fisheries, coastal regions and sectors, Madagascar has set up an ambitious policy framework (National Adaptation Programme of Action - NAPA) to address climate change. The UNFCCC-funded NDC turned into NDC 2015, and three National Communications (NCs) to the UNFCCC.

The National Coastal Zone Management Act and the NCAs are still based on the actions of the NAPA related to El Niño in coastal and marine areas implemented in 2006:

* **Restoration of natural protection and reduction of the vulnerability of coasts, inland, and marine areas most affected by climate change:** This includes shoreline stabilization.
* **Appropriate land use planning and integrated management in major agricultural centers, cash crop zones, intensive livestock farming and aquaculture areas for sustainable development.**
* **Restoration of natural habitats (forests and mangroves): 45,000 ha; takes 13 years.**
* **Establishment of protected areas:** This includes the creation of new protected areas.
* **Early warning systems that monitor for cyclones, floods and drought, and public health surveillance.**

In Madagascar's Third National Communication to UNFCCC (from Oct 2017), the protection of coastal areas through EIA is a priority. The NAP also aims at reducing vulnerability in coastal communities by improving livelihoods over time, improving the livelihoods of poor coastal populations.

The national adaptation plan, which includes the NAP, also addresses Madagascar's GHG emissions. According to its Intended Nationally Determined Contribution (INDC), Madagascar aims to increase absorption in the Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry sector from 61 MTCO2e in 2015 to 93 MTCO2e by 2030 (an increase of 57% or 32 Gt of GHG absorption, compared to the business-as-usual scenario). However, as mangroves currently represent only 2.4% of the forest cover in Madagascar, the absorption of GHG through better conservation of mangroves is only a co-benefit.

## South Africa

South Africa submitted its INDC to UNFCCC in August 1997. Its INDC (2014), National Development Plan (2012) and National Climate Change Response White Paper (Dept. of Environmental Affairs, 2011) together provide the strategy for South Africa's climate change adaptation.

In South Africa, changes are being observed in the marine and coastal environment and have already had significant impacts on the fisheries sector and on the local economy. Therefore, the National Climate Change Adaptation Strategy (NCCAS) was developed to address these issues. The NCCAS is a comprehensive strategy that includes agriculture including fisheries). With the concept of ECA, "biodiversity and ecosystem services will be used as part of adaptation strategies in order to increase resilience of ecosystems and communities". This includes coastal management guidelines on climate adaptation resilience actions for municipalities, as well as investment in disaster risk reduction and climate change adaptation.

ECA in fisheries management is designed to build greater resilience in the fisheries sector, assisting the recovery of overfished stocks. The ECA approach will be used to develop a national strategy for sustainable fishing and offshore fishery resources, to increase the resilience of natural fish populations to climate change, and to introduce reduced catch limits on some species. This will also include the development of marine protected areas along the coast to ensure nursery habitats essential for prawns and estuarine fish are maintained and coastal ecosystems are protected from overfishing.

The destruction of coastal ecosystems such as mangroves does not seriously affect South Africa's GHG emissions, as its mangrove area is only marginal (1.74 ha).

## Baselines for Climate Vulnerabilities and Impact on the Programme Region

Coastal ecosystems are highly vulnerable to climate change impacts due to their sensitivity to the impacts of climate change. Since the 1970s, more than 53% of the temperature increase due to the greenhouse effect and other human activities has been absorbed by the oceans. The rate at which this absorption is occurring is increasing with ocean warming. In addition, it is projected that sea levels will rise significantly in the future, and there will be an increase in extreme weather events such as hurricanes and typhoons. These factors are expected to have a significant impact on coastal activities, ocean acidification has increased by -26% since pre-industrial times, and will decrease to an average pH of 7.8 by 2100. The projected rise in sea levels is likely to be between 35-40 cm by the end of this century. At rates of temperature rise, oceans will become too warm for coral reefs by 2050, resulting in the loss of the world's most biologically diverse ecosystems and a significant reduction in fish stocks. The projected increase in sea levels will also affect the ecology of coastal ecosystems: over the last 100 years, the global mangrove area has lost 32 million ha while the current rate is estimated at -5% per year (Source: Global Mangrove Action Project (GMAP) and International Agricultural Organization). Saline waters such as freshwater lakes are also highly affected by climate change.

Population densities in coastal areas are high. The majority of the world's population lives within 100 km of the ocean. Coastal floods, cyclones, sea-level rise, and salt-water intrusion in coastal aquifers all have devastating effects on local communities and their livelihoods.

## Mangroves

Mangrove forests are one of the Earth's most climate-resilient, productive and biodiverse ecosystems. Mangroves are self-sustaining plants and the forests create one of the most species-rich habitats worldwide. Their high nutrient supply and ability to retain sediment make them a critical component in coastal protection against erosion by waves and storm surges. Through the exchange of nutrients and organic matter with rivers, mangroves are strongly intertwined with other terrestrial ecosystems such as wetlands and savannahs.

Intact mangrove ecosystems have remarkable climate change adaptation as well as mitigation capacities. Mangroves can store large amounts of carbon in their biomass and sediments, which makes them important ecosystem services. Mangrove resources are used directly by processing mangrove wood to charcoal, while a large share of the global population depends on mangroves for food security through fishing and other livelihoods.

In addition, these trees play a vital role in the sequestration of blue carbon. The carbon storage capacity of mangrove forests is estimated at 100-250 tons per hectare. Mangroves are also important sinks for CO₂, responsible for about 10% of global emissions caused by deforestation – around 240 million tC of CO₂ per year.11 Soil organic carbon is an important component in the resilience and recovery capacity of mangrove ecosystems. The total amount of soil organic carbon stored in mangroves is approximately nearly 1,600 GtCO₂ for oceanic mangroves.11

The unsustainable exploitation of mangroves and development pressures on mangroves has led to an alarming loss of mangrove cover. Major causes are the logging of mangroves for timber and charcoal production and the extension of agriculture into mangrove areas.

Moambique harbours the largest mangrove area in the Programme region, and the second longest extension of mangroves in Africa.12 It ranks 18th amongst countries globally in terms of mangrove coverage. Mangroves occur in nearly all coastal provinces of Mozambique. The total mangrove cover is estimated at around 305,000 ha. This area size has shrunk over the past three decades and a nationwide decrease from 408,000 ha in 1972 to 357,000 ha was noted for the period of 1996-2006.

Agricultural (landscapes) encroachment with slash-and-burn practices, urban development and infrastructure projects, and coastal development are major threats to mangrove ecosystems. The expansion of shrimp farming and aquaculture production have been responsible for most of the mangrove degradation in the past. The threats to mangroves and other coastal ecosystems are expected to worsen with increased coastal economic development, exacerbated by the effects of climate change.

### G.2. Gender consideration and action plan (max. 500 words, approximately 1 page)

Within one of the target countries, issues relating to gender equality remain an on-going challenge, specifically in traditional fishing communities, often living in remote locations, with low access to education, employment, and modern infrastructure. Women are disproportionately affected by poverty, food insecurity, limited access to resources and vulnerability to climate change, with women often being the most vulnerable and bearing the brunt of these challenges.

Women, as key agents responsible for the resilience of small-scale fishing communities, as well as for the reproductive work, have a leading role to play in climate adaptation, ensuring climate justice, and disaster risk management. Women are also more exposed to climate change impacts than men due to their greater dependence on natural resources and vulnerability related to this and are differently impacted by changes in their environment due to climate change and cultural and global trends.

Therefore, there is a strong rationale to build in gender considerations in the overall Programme strategy and design. The Programme will therefore include a Gender Mainstreaming Guide that will provide guidance for the development and evaluation at the Programme, as well as the project and community level and in the overall policy dialogue with institutions and stakeholders.

A Guide to Gender Mainstreaming that will be developed prior to the official launch of the Programme, will support further gender mainstreaming efforts. The Guide will include a gender action plan. Country level assessments and action plans will be completed as part of the Programme Guide.

The Guide will be developed by a qualified team of consultants, to be assigned by the BAF with funding from the KFW. It will draw on lessons learned from previous Gender Mainstreaming Guides (e.g., the Green Climate Fund Projects-Manual) and to the KfW’s Gender Strategy (as accredited entity), relevant safeguard requirements of the BAF’s Environmental and Social Management System (ESMS).

As a key instrument of the Programme, the Guide will support project developers to ensure an overall gender-sensitive approach in their projects. The Guide will be developed based on the lessons learned from the BAF’s ESMS which governs the Programme’s environmental and social safeguard requirements. The acquisition of gender mainstreaming knowledge is also one of the main objectives of the Programme. It will provide guidance considerations in the development of project frameworks to mitigate impacts from access restrictions to protected areas, as well as other gender-sensitive issues that may arise during the implementation phase of this Guide. Other important topics will comprise gender sensitive stakeholder engagement, as well as guidance for the development of gender action plans and gender mainstreaming strategies at the project level, including in their overall decision making levels such as in management structures for protected areas or in community based management structures.

The Programme’s Guide to Gender Mainstreaming will inform gender considerations for the overall global BAF funding portfolio with the overall intention to apply the provisions of the Guide for the entire BAF portfolio. Applicants will be encouraged to use this Guide and its recommendations throughout their project development process, through the project cycle and to provide evidence that they transferred recommendations from the Programme level into their project.

In their project proposals, project applicants will provide information on how they ensure that relevant institutions, groups or individuals are involved in the project design and implementation. The BAF will assess whether this will be able to change through successful implementation of the project. In addition, a stakeholder analysis will identify key stakeholders at different levels (national, regional, local) who may have an interest in the project, as well as the extent to which communities depend on coastal and marine resources for their livelihoods. As these are often women’s livelihoods, this information is critical to ensure that gender considerations are included in disaggregated information. Detailed approaches and plans regarding involvement of women on the sub-project level will be outlined in the gender action plan.

During the project selection process, the BAF will ensure that both men and women equally benefit from sustainable and climate-smart livelihood options in fisheries, aquaculture, tourism, ecosystem restoration and sustainable management of coastal resources. The BAF will also ensure that gender considerations are included at all levels under gender sensitivity in the general project concept. Proposed activities will be gender-sensitive and promote the participation of women in decision-making processes.

In addition, women will be particularly encouraged to take their role in project implementation as well as being part of various committees and working groups. The BAF will ensure that the technical assistance provided by the implementing bodies can provide the necessary support and training to women in the technical, organizational and leadership domains.

Gender Mainstreaming in Management of Coastal Ecosystems: Common Challenges

Historically, the contributions of women in onshore fisheries, aquaculture, processing and trading of marine products, in managing waste from urban and tourist growth, and their important role in conservation and disaster-risk reduction initiatives in marine and coastal

areas have been routinely ignored or underestimated in research, management and policy.

A variety of inter-related factors have led to women’s work and contributions in fisheries, marine and coastal conservation and resources management being grossly understated and undocumented. These include top-down and linear planning, lack of gender-disaggregated data, focus on large-scale commercial fishing activities at the expense of small-scale fishing communities and techno-environmental bias of marine and coastal development policies and projects.

The key messages from the Global Gender and Environment Outlook (2016)[^1] regarding marine and coastal communities and ecosystems are:

- Women and men have common but differentiated responsibilities in the fishing sector. While women make up 50% of the global population, when only the whole fishing cycle is taken into account, actually some 47% of the workforce is female.
- Fishing activities are often gendered. In many countries, fishers are traditionally defined as “fishermen”, while women’s activities in the sector are too often overlooked in official programmes and policies.
- Environmental change and damage to marine systems have gendered impacts, and women are disproportionately affected by overexploitation and degradation. Climate change is especially threatening to coastal communities and fishing livelihoods. The long-term effects on fishing sector activities such as post-harvest work are often not taken into account.
- Health impacts are gender-differentiated. For example, many marine contaminants are persistent organic pollutants (POPs) that accumulate in the food chain and affect women’s bodies more than men’s because of their higher levels of body fat. POPs also bioaccumulate, threatening human health and the health of marine organisms.
- As fisheries collapse globally and fish become scarce locally, many women have to turn to other livelihood activities.
- Illegal, unreported and unregulated (IUU) fishing relies on trafficked, indentured and slave labour, mostly by men.
- Evidence shows that fisheries management improves when women are actively involved.

The Programme's overall objective is to enhance, through the conservation and sustainable use of coastal ecosystems, the capacity of vulnerable communities to adapt to climate change related risks for vulnerable coastal communities. # Gender Assessment and Gender Mainstreaming at the Project Level

Applicants will be required to apply the recommendations and provisions of the Programme Guide to Gender Mainstreaming throughout the project cycle and to provide evidence that they translate these recommendations into the Project level meaningfully to the specific project.

In their project proposals, applicants should provide information on how they ensure the relevant target groups and local communities are involved in planning and implementation, as well as detailing what stakeholders will be able to change through successful implementation of the project. Applicants are encouraged to use a gender-sensitive approach/methodology and by acquiring gender disaggregated information.

Detailed approaches and plans regarding involvement of women on the project level will be identified and developed in a participatory manner on the level of the individual projects, based on the findings from the Gender Assessment/Gender Action Plan.
