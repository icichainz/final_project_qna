# A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

## Background:

The challenge of climate change calls for extraordinary vision, leadership, compassion and wisdom as the cumulative accumulation of greenhouse gases (GHGs) historically since industrial revolutions has resulted in the current problem of global warming. The per capita emissions of many developed countries vary between 7 to 15 metric tonnes, while the per capita emissions in India were only about 1.56 metric tonnes in 2010. Climate change is a major challenge for developing countries like India which are highly vulnerable due to their high population density and low adaptive capacity to climate change.

Few countries in the world are as vulnerable to the effects of climate change as India because of its vast population that is dependent on the growth of its agrarian economy, its expansive coastal areas and the Himalayan region and islands. The country has already started experiencing the adverse impacts of climate change. In 2015, India set a target to reduce the emissions intensity of its GDP by 20–25%, over five years, 2005 levels, despite having no binding mitigation obligations as per the Convention. A slew of policy measures were launched to achieve this goal. As a result, the emission intensity of its GDP has reduced from about 17% between 2005 and 2013.

The United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) in its Emissions Gap Report 2014 has recognized India as one of the countries on course to achieve its voluntary goal. The largest renewable capacity expansion program in the world is being taken up by India to reduce CO₂ emissions. The Government of India is aiming to increase share of clean energy through massive thrust on renewables. A capacity addition of 31.9 GW from solar power, 20.5 GW from wind power, 4.8 GW from Small Hydro Power and 6 GW from Biomass Power, which include 5.8 GW from Solar Power, 7.04 GW from Wind Power, 0.53 GW from Small Hydropower and 0.93 GW from Biomass Power is proposed to be added by the end of 2017.

The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) has recognized India as one of the countries that will achieve 40% cumulative Electric power capacity from non fossil fuel based energy resources by 2020. The Government of India aims at a target of 40 GW of rooftop solar power by 2022 which will require substantial financial resources, estimated at USD 34 billion overall, availability of long-term debt financing remains a major constraint for expansion of rooftop solar power in India.

Indian Solar Rooftop: installed capacity stood at 1.4 GW on March 2017 with Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra and Rajasthan being the leading states in terms of installed Rooftop Solar capacity. The solar rooftop market has grown at a CAGR of over 50% since 2013. The Indian solar rooftop market is expected to grow at an annual rate of about 40%. With 65% of total installed capacity, Commercial & Industrial remains the biggest market segment. These consumers account for more than 50% of India's total power demand and make savings of up to 50% through rooftop solar systems

## C1 Strategic Context

The largest renewable capacity expansion program in the world is being taken up by India. The Government of India is aiming to increase share of clean energy through massive thrust in renewables. In order to achieve the renewable energy target of 175 GW by the year 2022, the major program/schemes on implementation of Solar Park, Solar Defence Scheme, Solar schemes for CPCL, Solar PV power plants on Canal Bank and Canal Top, Solar Power Generation from Waste Water Treatment Plants have been initiated.

### Business Models

Currently major part of the rooftop capacity in India is based on the CAPEX model, where the user has invested in the capital intensive system. The alternate model is the OPEX model where the developer builds a solar energy system on the customer's property for no significant upfront charges. The generation from the solar energy system offsets the customer’s electricity consumption and hence reduces their power bill. As per the data available with NABARD, around 80% of the installed solar rooftop capacity was under the CAPEX model. Despite concerns around bankability of OPEX projects in the Indian context, the OPEX model has been gaining traction in recent years. The share of OPEX projects developed by developers is expected to be around 25-30%. As per the current policy framework and priorities of various departments and their more urgent priorities for deployment of capital, the government segment is expected to play a very important role in scaling up of the OPEX market in India. Developers are understandably satisfied with government entities as they have a higher credit rating compared to private sector entities. Solar rooftop projects are riskier than conventional ground-mounted solar PV projects due to additional factors such as shadow risk, rooftop structure analysis, etc. As a result, banks usually charge a high risk premium over their funds in order to mitigate these risks and ensure profitability of the project.

It is difficult to indicate a firm lending rate as the landed cost of GCF funds for TCLC itself will largely depend on the hedge prevalent at the time of drawdown. As such, any saving in landed cost of funds on account of concessionality will be passed on to the customer.

Senior Loans from GCF to TCLC shall be priced on concessional terms. The concessionally received by TCLC from GCF shall be passed on to the project developers in the form of interest charged on the project loan.

**Outcome analysis**

Monitoring, reporting, and evaluation arrangements will comply with requirements established by GCF in the FAA and the AMA. Besides, every project supported by the programme will undergo detailed evaluation including identification, eligibility review, due diligence evaluation, credit review, approval, pre-closing review, financial closing, disbursement, supervisory monitoring, and post-implementation evaluation. The projects that are funded under the programme shall be subject to an ex-ante assessment on the rationale for the project and its expected development impact. This will be followed by a detailed implementation plan which will be aligned with each specific project. The outcome of such assessments may lay down certain operational and financial parameters which the underlying project is expected to meet even before the loan.

Post sanction and effectiveness of FAA for the project, MABARD shall monitor the project reporting and evaluation process in accordance with the requirements of the FAA and AMA or when TCCL shall provide all the requisite information and reports to MABARD. The supervision of the projects financed by the GCF will be based on a detailed Monitoring Plan which will be prepared as per the guidelines issued from time to time by the GCF and/or its agents. Considering that the projects funded under the programme shall be spread across multiple locations across India, all projects will be supervised through a combination of field visits, desk reviews, and remote monitoring tools set up in TCCL. In addition to the reporting requirements under the FAA and/or AMA, the Credit Monitoring Team shall continue to monitor the projects in line with the TCCL – Credit Monitoring Policy. On a quarterly basis, the Credit Monitoring Team will prepare an Annual Report on the status of the project based on the information provided by the project developer as assessed at the time of granting the loans. Further, every year, the Credit Monitoring Team of TCCL will prepare an Annual Report on the status of the projects in line with the TCCL – Credit Monitoring Policy. The report shall include the project evaluation and any additional financial indicators included in the loan agreement. Project level results will be aggregated to assess the impact of the GCF’s programme in addition to the GCF’s project-specific indicators.
