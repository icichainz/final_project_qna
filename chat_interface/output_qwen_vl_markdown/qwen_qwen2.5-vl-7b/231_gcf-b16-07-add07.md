# A2: Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

The objective of the Tina River Hydropower Development Project (TRHPD, "the Project") is to lower the system average cost of electricity supply and diversity generation capacity in favor of clean, renewable sources. As the main alternative energy source for Solomon Islands, hydropower has a significant potential to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. The project will lead to significant reductions in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in a power system which is almost entirely reliant on diesel fuel. The Project is expected to reduce the carbon intensity of electricity generation by 80% and the grid is expected to shift from 35% to 67% at the time of expected TRHP completion in 2022. This shift away from diesel will also have a significant impact on reducing the cost of power for SIEA (operating as "Solomon Power") customers, with the final magnitude of savings to be assessed at the time of power purchase agreement (PPA) signing between SIEA and the Project Company.

A major feature of this project is that it will be financed through a 50% loan over 40 years including 10-year grace to finance a portion of the debt of the Project Company to develop the Tina River Hydropower Plant (Component 1) on the island of Guadalcanal. The remainder of the financing for Component 1 is from private equity investors. This is part of a concessional funding package sought from several organizations described below. Since the sharp drop in oil prices, there has been a significant increase in interest by private sector developers to invest in power projects competitive with the current diesel generation cost, and to incentivize SIEA to transition to renewable energy at this scale; concessional finance is required to fund all of the Project's debt.

Significant project benefits include: (i) the Tina River Hydropower plant which will annually generate 78.35 gigawatt hours (69% of the 120 GWh demand projected in Honiara in 2022) with a net GHG emission reduction of 4,500 tonnes CO2e per year; and (ii) the Tina River Hydropower Project will have an estimated total carbon reduction potential of the Project is more than two and a half times higher than Solomon Islands Government's (SG) commitment to reduce its greenhouse gas emissions by 19.8% in 2035, or 64 million tonnes CO2e; it can also reduce emissions by 13,800 kio/year over the period from 2025 and, 60% higher than the total reduction of 31.25 GCO2e projected in the Solomon Islands' Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) submitted to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), which was issued by the Ministry of Environment, Climate Change and Disaster Management (MECCDM). The Ministry of Finance, Treasury and Planning (MFTP) has also issued a No Objection and is fully supportive of the Project.

#### Data Note

The Project will be developed as a private sector project in respect to the first utility-scale hydropower plant and will also be the first privately-invested build own operate-transfer (BOOT) project on Solomon Islands. It is also the first sizeable renewable energy development in a 97% diesel-generated system, with reservoir capacity that will provide flexibility for load-following generation. The Project will also have the potential to reduce the need for expensive storage energy or diesel generators running at low efficiencies to respond to the intermittent PV output. This dramatic transformation of Solomon Islands' power sector is expected to be a model for other countries in the region and in other small island nations with similar constraints.

Reduce the cost of electricity driven electrify villages to stimulate household and business savings and investment. Retail electricity tariff in Solomon Islands is one of the highest in the world at US$0.28/kWh for residential customers due to the high cost of diesel. The Project is expected to contribute to lowering the tariff so that the significant reduction in the cost of electricity will be passed on to consumers. This will also have a positive impact increasing the grid-connected electrification rate of 12%. Global oil prices are currently at historic lows, but the Project will not be dependent upon oil price fluctuations as it is a renewable energy project and will reduce exposure to global oil price fluctuations. As the Project is to be developed by a private investor as a BOOT scheme, SIEA will have no ongoing financial obligations for the project after completion of the 40-year term. This will reduce the generation cost for SIEA, but will significantly save SIEA's foreign currency denominated expenditures on energy.

Economic Benefits: The Project is expected to generate approximately $15 million per year in revenue per year over the investment period at the life of 50 years as 16.8 kGCO2e/p. In terms of the impact of the requested GCF financing of $80 million, this represents a return on investment for the private sector of 37% and an internal rate of return per year over the project life of 9%. Financial viability: The Project is designed to satisfy the financial viability of the (i) private investor, and (ii) SIEA which is the off-taker. The concessional funds from GCF and other co-financers will enable the private investor's return on equity to be achieved within a reasonable time frame. This project represents a significant milestone in Solomon Islands' energy sector development and a monumental step towards a renewable energy dominant power system. # C.1. Strategic Context

## Country Context

The Solomon Islands is a small island state that has been recovering from many years of intermittent political turmoil and civil strife. The civil strife during 1998-2003 disrupted the functioning of state and social institutions across the country, leading to widespread loss of life and property damage. This period also saw a breakdown in law and order and other basic state functions, particularly in finance, through the deployment of the Regional Assistance Mission to Solomon Islands (RAMSI) in 2003.

The post-conflict recovery has been characterized by a focus on restoring peace and security, forgiveness, amicability and complementary domestic debt restructuring under the Honiara City Agreement (HCA) with international development partners, including Australia, New Zealand, the European Investment Bank (EIB) and the European Union (EU).

## The Solomon Islands Economy

The Solomon Islands economy has rebounded since the civil unrest but remains vulnerable to external shocks. The economy recovered relatively strongly based on export of commodities such as logging and mining. However, the global financial crisis in 2008-2010 led to a sharp decline in commodity prices, which affected government revenue and a depletion of foreign currency reserves. SVG recognized the need for significant reductions in its current spending levels, resulting in cuts to public sector employment and services.

The Solomon Islands has been heavily reliant on imports since independence from Australia in 1978. Imports reached $150/barrel increased Solomon Islands' vulnerability to oil price volatility, and the country's balance of payments deficit has been increasing over time. The government has been working with international partners to address this issue.

The high cost of diesel fuel which the economy has suffered particularly during 2011-2014 when oil prices fluctuated around $150/barrel has led to a significant increase in the country's energy costs, especially for the generation of energy, particularly hydro and other renewables. At the same time, while the country had benefited from the HCA, the amount of funding available under this agreement was insufficient to finance the development of large scale projects very difficult. While this monstrosity has since been lifted it gave an initial impetus to the drive for private sector participation in infrastructure development, financing and operation - a policy that was later enshrined in SVG's National Development Strategy (NDS).

## NDS and the Power Sector

Universal supply of reliable grid-connected electricity is a great challenge in Solomon Islands where its population is scattered across 90 isolated islands. The national electrification rate is reported to be 45%, but this figure is not widely accepted as it does not reflect the actual situation on the ground.

According to the National Electrification Strategy (NES) developed and implemented by SIEA, the state-owned power utility, it merely 12% nationally. SVG recognizes the importance of providing equitable access to electricity for all Solomon Islanders in both urban and rural areas. The NES was launched in 2016-2015 (April 2016) views the energy sector as a key enabling factor to support its poverty alleviation effort, according to which it is important that the energy sector should be able to provide reliable access for both urban and rural communities. While the NES emphasizes the need to accelerate rural electrification, it places equal importance on improving the quality of electricity supply in urban areas. The NES also aims at promoting renewable energy, opening up the market to IPPs, appropriate tariff structures and ensuring SIEA has sound technical and managerial expertise for an efficient and effective SOE (state-owned enterprise).

The Project, which is an IPP using renewable energy resources, is consistent with the National Development Strategy (NDS) and was actively involved in the formulation of the project, is fully consistent with this strategy.

### Solomon Islands National Energy Policy (SINEP: 2014)

The SINEP was formulated with a view to promoting the development potential of renewable energy resources for sustainable development. The electrification rate, SINEP sets ambitious targets to increase access to electricity in urban areas to 100%, and to 30% in rural areas by 2035. The SINEP also aims at increasing the share of renewables in the national energy mix (NEM) has recently launched an initiative to double the number of households supplied by SIEA from 15,500 to 30,000 by 2020. This initiative is aimed at providing electricity access for the most remote rural populations. This is in line with NDS and other energy strategy to "provide reliable and affordable electricity in rural areas through the development of renewable energy resources and development of those which are technically sound and can be made financially viable."

Under the renewable energy thematic area, the SINEP establishes a target to "increase the use of renewable energy sources for power generation" by 2035. The SINEP also sets an ambitious target that SVG's main power grid in the capital city of Honiara with an available capacity of 30.7 MW is 99% diesel generators. While a small amount of solar PV has been installed, it is not enough to meet the demand. In contrast, New Zealand, has recently been commissioned its installed capacity is significantly compared the SINEP target. This solar farm is only tested as a pilot project, and the SINEP instead prioritizes the development of the Tira River Hydropower

## Project Background:
Recognizing the need for SIENA to increase its generation capacity, while reducing its dependency on expensive imported fuel with volatile prices and reducing its GHG emissions, the Bank commissioned the Guadiana Renewable Development Concept Study (2006) which was completed in February 2006. The study investigated the potential of hydropower development at the Tira River catchment, including the Nguimbulu, Nalu catchment, and highveldu and Kokolamusa Rivers of the Chitete catchment – and concluded that the Tira River has the highest potential for hydropower generation in SIENA. The study recommended a 3-stage cascade development: the first stage involving a weir and a tunnel utilizing 150 m2 of head, discharge of 13-17 m3/s with an installed capacity of 6 MW; the second stage involves a weir and a tunnel utilizing 80 m2 of head at a low loss with a powerhouse at its toe with a head of 85 m, discharge of 12-15 m3/s with an installed capacity of 15-21 MW to generate power.

Subsequently in 2007, the Sistema Sostenible Energía Projecto – Hydropower Component - Pre-Feasibility Study (pre-FES) was conducted. This pre-FES first recommended that the Tira River of the Nguimbulu catchment is the most favorable river for hydropower development and assessed three options based on site sizes, 150 m2 scale digital topography data, and a preliminary hydrological analysis. The three options were evaluated at Horizone, Henderson and Henderson stations. The pre-FES recommended the first of the three options which involves a weir 10 m wet just downstream of the confluence of the Nguimbulu River with the Tira River, a tunnel utilizing 80 m2 head of water at 180.5 m. With a design discharge of 20 m3/s and installed capacity of 32 MW, the scheme was simulated to annually generate 94 GWh.

From 2010 to 2015, with the support of the World Bank, EBRD and Australian Government, the Tira River Hydropower Development Feasibility Study (FES) was prepared in three phases. It involved hydrological analysis, genetic topography data, and a preliminary economic assessment.

While the FES progressively assessed several options, the final recommendation was a scheme consisting of a 64 m weir constructed at the confluence of the Nguimbulu River with the Tira River, a tunnel utilizing 80 m2 head of water, a powerhouse at its toe with a head of 185.5 m and two 3 MW turbine-generators, and a tailrace channel. The dam will create a 2.6 km long reservoir with total storage volume of 6.9 million m³ and a surface area of 305,000 m² at full supply level of 17 m. Design discharge is 18 m/s and the 15-year peak will be 40 m/s.

During the FES preparation, both public financing and private sector financing were considered.
- **Public or mostly public sector finance:** It was clear from the World Bank's assessment that SIG did not have the capacity to finance the project. The World Bank recommended that the project should be financed by a consortium of banks. Funds were stated to be available at the time. Further, public borrowing was limited by the terms of the Honorable Club Agreement and the Public Debt Management Act.
- **Private sector financing:** There are two main private sector implementation modes: BOD (build own operate), BOOT (build lease transfer) and variations around these more. In BOD, the lease period is typically 25 years with a 10-year construction period. The project company would be responsible for all aspects of operation; SIG can see benefit from very low cost electricity for the remainder of the plant's useful life at capital costs that are lower than those of other projects. However, BOD requires a large initial investment and a long-term power purchase agreement (PPA). In BOOT, the project company would be responsible for all aspects of operation; SIG can see benefit from very low cost electricity for the remainder of the plant's useful life at capital costs that are lower than those of other projects. However, BOD requires a large initial investment and a long-term power purchase agreement (PPA). In BOOT, the project company would have to construct supervised by, and be operated by SIGE which does not have the hydropower expertise for supervising construction or operate the Project.

Based on the above assessment, it was decided that the Tira River Hydropower Project will be developed on a BOOT basis by a Project Company under a 34-year concession (including a 4-year design and construction period).

## C3.2 Project / Programme Objective against Baseline

Despite the baseline scenario (e.g., emissions baseline, climate vulnerability baseline, key barriers, challenges and/or policy) and the outcomes and impacts of the project, Solomon Islands has a very high potential to reduce its GHG emissions.

According to the INDC, GHG emission from Solomon Islands in 2015 is approximately 700,000 tCO2e per year. The baseline scenario for Solomon Islands is based on the assumption that the country will continue with business as usual (BAU) until 2030. While the INDC does not provide a figure for the BAU emission in future years, the Solomon Islands' conditional contribution to the global emissions reduction is the reduction of GHG emission by 18,500 tCO2e per year by 2025 and by 31,125 tCO2e per year by 2030. The project has the potential to double these emissions reductions in the INDC.

In terms of climate vulnerability, Solomon Islands, as a small island developing state, is extremely susceptible to climate change impacts such as sea level rise and increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events. Hence, the main benefits of the Project are related to the mitigation of climate change effects through GHG emission reduction. It has been estimated that the project will reduce the risk of flooding in Solomon Islands by improving the management of downstream flows during times of droughts and floods. In particular, the dam reservoir is designed with Full Supply Level at EL 17.5m and Normal Operating Level at EL 17.2 m providing about 1 million m³ of storage capacity for flood control.

## Describe the detailed methodology used for calculating the indicators above

### Expected tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) to be reduced or avoided
Energy generated by Project: Average annual energy of 78.35 GWh generated by the Project has been calculated in detail in the feasibility study based on 30 years of observed and synthesized hydrological data from the Tina River.
Observations were made at the Tina River gauging station, located approximately 12 km upstream of the project site. Synthetic discharge data was produced by a runoff model using observed rainfall data from Henderson rain gauging station and Chirundu meteorological station. The synthetic discharge data was used in conjunction with the hydrological analysis used for the energy generation simulation model.
The FS Hydrological Analysis Annex has been prepared to support the feasibility study. The Project is expected to achieve a 13% (or better) NPV, which achieves the highest net present value (NPV). Detail can be found in the feasibility study (Tina River Hydropower Project and Phase 3 Addendum Report).

### GHG reduction by Project: 
The Honiara grid system is currently supplied 98% by diesel generation. All future investment in the absence of the project being developed as a BOCT scheme by a private investor, comprises only diesel generation.
The energy generated by the Project, therefore, fully displaces current and future diesel generation of the same amount (i.e. 78.35 GWh per year). There is no official grid emission factor for the Honiara grid system. The emission factor used in this study is based on the average emission factor for diesel generators.
The gross GHG emissions reduction is, therefore, calculated to be 650 tCO2eq/GWh x 78.35 GWh = 50,905 CO2eq per year; or 2,251 tCO2eq for the Project for the lifetime of the project.

### GHG emissions from the reservoir:
GHG emissions from the reservoir (i) emission from the reservoir: (ii) last century’s carbon and (iii) potential enduring legacy. The GHG emissions from the reservoir is calculated based on emissions factor of 4 tCO2eq/ha for a reservoir in a ‘tropical’ - wet climate with an average power density of 50 W/m², which is equivalent to 100,000 Wh per square meter per year. The reservoir has an area of approximately 15,000,000 Wh by the reservoir surface area (300,000 m²). This emissions factor multiplied by the annual energy of 78.35 GWh gives a total GHG emission from the reservoir of 294 tCO2eq per year. The lifetime of the dam is conservatively assumed to be existent for 100 years, giving a lifecycle GHG emission of 35,260 CO2eq.

### Potential GHG emissions from land clearance:
Potential GHG emissions from land clearance is calculated using the biomass density emission factor of 264 tCO2eq/ha for a ‘most dedicate forest’. The land expected to be cleared by the Project is 110 hectares (ha), including all of the reservoir and land needed for the access road and transmission lines. Therefore, GHG emissions are 29,040 CO2eq.

### Potential GHG emission from construction activities:
Potential GHG emission from construction activities is calculated by applying the construction emission factor of 1 tCO2eq/GWh. For 78.35 GWh production for 50 years, the lifecycle GHG emission from construction is 3,900 tCO2eq.

### Feasibility study-level economic analysis

Economic analysis for the Project was conducted under the Feasibility Study Phase (June to August 2016) using a base case scenario for Costa Rica for the 50 year horizon. The "without Project" case considered the scenario where SIEA meets growing demand by continuing to install diesel generators. The "with Project" case also involves diesel generation since the Project alone cannot meet the projected load growth, but it is assumed that the Project will reduce diesel consumption through the avoided cost of diesel generation. In this analysis, the economic benefit of GHG emission reduction is not accounted for.

### Least-cost generation capacity expansion plan

To inform its decision-making with regards to the Project, the SIEA commissioned a least-cost generation capacity expansion plan in July-August 2016 including (i) power system planning and (ii) renewable energy integration. The plan considered existing and new generation sources to meet the hourly load under a range of demand scenarios. It may be noted that G-2 reserve margin is a critical system constraint, which limits the amount of new capacity that can be added in any given year.

The least-cost analysis considered existing and new diesel generators, new hydro (i.e., the Project), using updated data and cost, new solar with and without storage, new geothermal and biomass. The levelized cost of energy of the Project is estimated to be US$0.12/kWh for a 50-year horizon. The Project was considered for the San Juan island, but was not included in the analysis due to lack of data, unproven resources and risk of connecting such a large unit by a submarine cable.

Data for solar power referred to actual insulation and PV output data based on the 30 kW system installed at SIEA. The levelized cost of energy (LCOE) for solar generation is estimated to be US$0.15/kWh. Solar generation is subject to significant minute by minute output generation, and assumed that 0.8 MW of load following capacity will be required in order to meet the peak demand. The Project was considered with storage option but with storage option does not require load following capacity; but was assumed to need a M/MW of energy storage capacity installed.

For geothermal power, based on the current feasibility study and availability, the penetration of solar without storage was capped at 40% of the maximum load, and 50% for solar with storage.

Demand forecast was provided by SIEA following their distribution network expansion strategy to double the number of residential connections in the next five years. The projected annual growth rate is 60% even in the capital city; this seems to be a plausible target. The base case demand forecast assumed 6% annual growth until the mid-2030s and moderate to 3.5 percent for the rest of the planning time horizon. It was noted that the actual load growth has been lower than expected, with an average load growth rate of around 1.8% during the past 12 months. This is believed to be the result of the new 10 MW diesel units commissioned in January 2016 which has enhanced energy efficiency and reduced demand.

Oil price was projected to US$80/barrel by 2030 and remain at the same level during the planning horizon.

Based on these assumptions, the result of the base case least-cost generation capacity expansion plan was concluded to involve: (i) the Project (15 MW) being commissioned in 2022 when peak load is forecasted to be 93 MW and annual growth rate for the next five years is expected at around 6%, which will be sufficient to meet demand over the simulated period; and (ii) new diesel units will be commissioned in 2029 as a result of limitations of solar integration into the system.
