# A21. Executive summary (max. 750 words, approximately 1.5 pages)

This project will take place in the semi-arid region of Northeast Brazil (NEB) (annex 16), a region with a population of 21% and 34% of the Brazilian population. The NEB is home to the poorest population in the country; IFAD has been supporting rural development there for the past 25 years. The Northeast region has experienced periods of drought, which have become more frequent and severe. Noteworthy, the 2011–2016 drought that affected this region is considered the worst in the past 100 years; it has exacerbated poverty significantly through famines' indigence, migration, disease, and malnutrition. Economic losses from the drought event in the Northeast are estimated at US$3 billion.

It is possible to identify an increase in temperature from 1981 to 2000 of about 0.8°C in NEB. An analysis of drought events in NEB from 1981 to 2016 reveals that drought intensity has been increasing over the last 36 years, and that recent droughts have had a greater impact on population than previous ones. The increase in temperature is expected to have an effect on population as well as economic activities. NEB is expected to experience a further temperature increase of 0.5–2.9°C in the period 2011-2040 compared to a baseline period of 1981–1990. Despite increased precipitation in the Northeast region, the frequency and intensity of droughts are likely to increase due to climate change. Factors such as an increase in number of consecutive dry days and wide variable climates are common features in these and other similar regions.

Family farmers are the most affected by climate change. There is a significant correlation between average precipitation and agricultural production, but statistically, the effect is significantly higher for crops produced by family farmers than average agricultural production. The average crop area lost due to drought from 1990 to 2016 was 221,973 hectares.

It is estimated that due to climate change, substitute foods such as cassava, beans and corn can suffer productivity losses up to 5% by 2030 in the Northeast, and some scenarios project that cassava could even disappear from the region. The average annual loss of production for family farmers would be US$14 million if rainfall is reduced by 20%, while a 10% reduction in rainfall would result in an economic loss to family farmers' agricultural production value of US $193.15 million in family farmer's agriculture production value.

The Planting Climate Resilience in Communities (the Northeast-PCR) project will work towards paradigm shift: it is expected that family farmers will be the main beneficiaries by increasing their productivity while simultaneously improving farmers’ capacity to face the challenges posed by ongoing climate change. The project will result in a more resilient agricultural sector, which will contribute to food security and nutrition for the region and stabilize family income and food security when incentivizing young generations to stay active in rural activities. The project will be implemented through three components: (1) Climate Resilient Productive Systems (CRPS); (2) Water access; and (3) Knowledge Management and Scaling-up.

Implementation of diversified agroforestry systems that aim to increase local water availability in the production system – CRPS, as proposed under Component 1, will influence hydrological conditions across the landscape. The Caatinga is a rich productive area, with fertile soils, where temporary streams and wetlands exist, making up a biome with good

#### Climate change problem
The project will take place in the semi-arid region of Northeast Brazil (NEB) (areas 16), a region with a population of 21 million people (37% of the national population). This state in the region are home to the poorest population in the country (average HDI 0.587) where IFAD has been supporting rural development for the past 25 years.

Family farms account for almost all of agricultural profits in the drylands of Brazil (generally over 90%). These family farms are characterized by a mixed farming system, which combines crop production with livestock rearing and/or dryland agriculture (harvesting food crops—mainly maize, beans and cassava—for home consumption and sale) with raising livestock. Families often have backyard gardens, a few fruit trees, and poultry. A few also have small irrigated plots of land. The average farm size is 10 hectares, but there are large farms (up to 25 million hectares); these farms account for 52% of the population value and 87% of the total labour in the sector.

In the Semiarid, brackish or salty groundwater is common. Approximately 52% of wells have freshwater (<500 mg/l TDS) while 43% are saline (1-500 mg/l TDS), and 4% salty (>1,500 mg/l TDS). Hence, an estimated 75% of the wells in the Semiarid are used for human consumption.

In Brazil, drought is the natural disaster that affected approximately 50% of all population, and most of this population is in the Northeast of Brazil - NEB. About 60% of the drought events were registered in the Northeast. The Northeast region has experienced a significant increase in temperature over the last century (1920-2000), with an average increase of about 0.8°C per decade. An analysis of drought events in NEB from 1950 to 2000 shows that there have been more frequent, more severe and have affected a larger area with significant impact on agriculture as well as the socio-economic development of the region. The Northeast region is one of the most vulnerable regions in Brazil over the past 100 years; it has exacerbated many social problems through farmers' indebtedness, migration disease, and malnutrition.

# Emission Sources and Mitigation Potential

Brazil has the world’s sixth largest greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, releasing 2.3 billion tons of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) in 2016, compared with 2.1 billion in 2015. In 2016, emissions were 8.9% higher than 2005, jeopardizing the country’s Paris goal of reducing its carbon emission 37% by 2025 compared to 2005 levels. GHG emissions are mainly driven by deforestation and land use change (45%), fossil fuel combustion (18%) and biomass and fossil fuel combustion. Land-use change and agriculture accounted for 73% of all carbon emitted in 2016.

Total emissions in the states of the Northeast in 2016 accounted for one quarter of Brazil’s total emissions (591.4 Mt CO2e). The largest share of these emissions was from deforestation and land use change, followed by fossil fuel emissions, followed by agriculture (with 108.9 MCO2e or 18%) and energy (with 102.7 MCO2e or 17%). Land-use change, specifically deforestation in the Caatinga biome, accounted for almost 5% of the carbon emissions (28.2 Mt CO2e). The Northeast is also responsible for 34% of Brazil’s total GHG emissions and 16% of the emissions in the Northeast. PCCB will contribute to the national accounting method for GHGs.

Finally, with 107 MCO2e emitted, energy-sector emissions in the Northeast are largely caused by fuel production (mainly from coal), followed by electricity generation (with 35.4 MCO2e or 33%) and industrial processes (with 28.9 MCO2e). The use of biomass for energy production (wood, sugarcane bagasse, and other agricultural residues) could be vital to helping these states move towards more renewable sources of energy and help decrease their emissions.

## B.2. Theory of change (max 1000 words; approximately 2 pages plus diagram)

The PCPR aims to achieve the following goal: If family farmers in the semiarid NEB transition to productive systems that are more resilient to climate change, they will be better able to face the challenges posed by ongoing climate change. Because the adoption of climate-resilient practices will result in farming systems that are more sustainable and productive, it is expected that these families will experience increased income, increasing and stabilizing family income and food security while incentivizing young generations to stay active in rural activities. The PCPR will work with the most marginalized and vulnerable groups of the region poor in Brazil. The project will also seek to ensure that the knowledge generated through this project mobilize resources and disseminate lessons to many levels of government in other regions in Brazil and abroad.

The climate stresses and vulnerability sources (described in section B.1) result in a number of climate change impacts, including: (a) increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events; (b) reduced water availability for non-resilient to these impacts, as presented in B.1); and are a driver of deforestation and land degradation. Historically, the government's response to climate stress in NEB has mainly focused on conventional solutions such as dams, wells, and irrigation systems. Such approaches have not been effective at reducing vulnerability or increasing resilience for family farmers, and do not increase farmer's autonomy capacity to face future climate shocks. Hence, the PCPR proposes to invest in a new approach that will provide family farmers with the tools they need to adapt their livelihoods and build resilience at the family and producer's organizations level, allowing the most vulnerable to absorb climatic shocks while increasing their adaptive capacity. The PCPR will do this by providing family farmers with climate-smart practices are the following: (a) know-how; given the limited capital farmers have to spend and make investments, (b) can be implemented by workforce available to family farmers; (c) provide a guarantee of acceptable production and productivity levels; (d) can be scaled up in a cost-effective manner; (e) can be integrated into Category B or C projects; (f) promote the inclusion of women, youth and traditional communities, and (g) can be scaled up.

### Sub-activity 3.1.2. Facilitate discussions to unlock policy barriers

The National Forest Code requires farmers in the Northeast to preserve 20% of their land as legal reserve. Family farmers are often unable to comply with this requirement because they do not have enough land or because it is located on steep slopes, which makes it difficult for them to use it productively.

The Forest Code anticipates that States could implement a legal reserve quota (CRA) market, where farmers that preserve their reserves can sell the right to other farmers who need to comply with the law. The first CRA's were identified during project design. The most notable include: i) lack of an Environmental Reserve Quota (ERQ in Portuguese); and ii) norms and regulations preventing family farmers from accessing markets.

As recommended by the World Bank,18 establishing the CRA market could provide additional income for family farmers who are unable to comply with the legal reserve requirement. This would be achieved through a CRA: a CRA produced on a beneficiary's property could be used to offset a legal reserve (RL) debt in another property within the same state. The RL debt can be generated by the farmer himself or by other farmers, and the RL debt will be efficiently offset by environmental improvements produced by smallholder farmers with CRPs, thereby generating a transfer payment from the RL offered to the smallholders. Implementing a State CRA could create a market for credits that would allow family farmers who are unable to comply with the law to generate income.

B.5. Justification for GCF funding request (max. 1000 words, approximately 2 pages)

PCPR is an acronym in the Brazilian NDCs goal to establish sustainable and climate change-resilient agriculture. It works in alignment with other initiatives for sustainable and low carbon emission agricultural development (ABC Plan).

GCF resources contribute to the economic sustainability of PCPR and leverage additional sources of funding towards climate finance.

The high risks perceived for both climate change and highly vulnerable groups funding in the targeted region are consistent with the World Bank’s 2015-2016 Global Economic Prospects report, which projects a worst-case scenario. The share of resources that commercial banks and institutional investors allocate to project financing in the region has remained limited and concentrated on refinancing projects already underway. Although private capital could be mobilized for PCPR, the current level of public investment is insufficient to attract private investors.

Availability of climate change and development investment is facing huge challenges in the country. The most recent fiscal year (2015-16) saw a 3% increase in public debt as a share of GDP (to 74% from 71%), with a projected increase to 89% by 2019, where 2015–16 economic recession deepened fiscal deficits and the country’s current exposure to debt sustainability risks (due to the increased Nonfinancial Public Sector Gross Debt of 60% in GDP in 2013 vs. 48% of GDP in 2015). The government has also been unable to meet its public expenditure commitments, with a projected decline in real terms of GDP annually in federal government expenditure in 2019–24, which could reduce the current public budget available for climate change in the upcoming years.

The fiscal situation is characterized by “exceptional” high debt levels (that represents around 30% of total public sector net debt), liquidity pressures and accumulation of large payment arrears and (in several Northeastern states) the inability to pay public wages are some of the critical financial problems justifying the level of concessional credit requested.

Brazil implemented an important tool to finance climate change: the Amazon Fund. A global initiative managed by BNDES to fight deforestation in the Amazon, the Amazon Fund has received donations of over US$ 1 billion from foreign governments and private donors since its creation in 2007. The Amazon Fund is expected to generate a return on investment of around 1% of the annual GDP, significantly above the current investment level! The Amazon Fund will cover 3.5% of the climate change financing required by Brazil for other purposes or regions, but few additional funds are available.

The Brazilian government has been following products for climate change adaptation and mitigation from public funding (Social Fund and Amazon Fund), and its reimbursable funding (National Climate Fund, PRONAF and FINEM Environmental Fund) and private sources. The concessional credit requested is expected to generate a higher internal rate of return (IRR) than the proposed project. The concessionality of GCF grant and loan financing provides an important opportunity for Brazil’s public sector to access concessional funding at lower interest rates, which are more vulnerable to climate change in Brazil, supporting them to transition to a climate-resilient production system. Within this group, smallholder farmers (including indigenous communities [indigenous, quilombola and freed slaves of Piauí]), these groups are the most vulnerable to climate-related risk, endure extreme conditions of poverty, lack access to the labour market, and have no permanent income sources.

The GCF grant of USD 34 million will focus on activities to increase awareness among farmers in Brazil as well as among Ministry of Agriculture officials. The project addresses a key constraint: the adoption and emission reduction in agricultural production in Brazil: the lack of information and technical assistance for promoting climate-resilient practices, such as crop diversification, soil conservation, water management, agroforestry systems, systematization and public awareness of CRPS. ### Brazil: National Adaptation Plan (NAP)

The project is included in GCF’s national strategy and it adheres to these strategic foundations and investment areas: adaptation based on ecosystems and water security; resilience and sustainability of indigenous and traditional communities; sustainable land management, including carbon sequestration, land carbon agriculture, and adaptation of the productive process. Another of the strategy's key priorities is water security.

Brazil has an overarching national climate-change policy, which paves the way to implementation of adaptation and mitigation measures by the three federal government layers: federal, state and municipal. The National Policy for Sustainable Development (NPD) was launched in 2013 with the aim of reducing greenhouse gas emissions and increasing carbon sinks. It sets out a series of actions to reduce emissions up to 2050. Regarding specific REDD+ actions, Brazil has the commitment to stabilize emissions at 2005 levels by 2020.

In the Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), Brazil emphasizes that adaptation is considered a fundamental element to tackle the effects of climate change. Social considerations are at the core of Brazil’s adaptation strategy, based on the need to protect vulnerable populations from the negative effects of climate change and enhance resilience.

In this context, the country developed a National Adaptation Plan (NAP) and put forward cross-sector adaptation strategies to address the wide range of risks caused by climate change. The NAP has also set guidelines to implement actions in the following sectors: energy; transport; water resources; agriculture; forests and land use; and urban areas.

The NDC supports strengthening the Low Carbon Agriculture Program (ABC) as a key strategy for sustainable agriculture development and commits to restoring 15 million hectares of degraded pastures, enhancing 5 million hectares of native vegetation, and increasing the share of organic farming in Brazil’s agricultural production by 2030. The ABC plan is one of the sectoral plans devised under the national climate-change policy. Its overall objective is to contribute to the achievement of the NDC targets through four main components: 1) increase resilience of production systems and rural communities; and 4) promote adaptation to climate change in the sector.

Brazil has also been renewing environmental legislation of private rural areas to reduce deforestation and promote sustainable land use practices. The Forest Code was updated and revised in 2012, and Law No. 12.651/2012 reinforces Brazil’s commitment to protect native vegetation and the integrity of the climate system by setting restrictions on forest conversion and deforestation. According to this law, all landowners must maintain a minimum area (RAF), as defined by the law, must be maintained by landholders with native vegetation at a proportion of 20% at Caatinga Biomes. The Forest Code also sets out the obligation for landowners to obtain a certificate of regularity and environmental registration on the Rural Environmental Registry (CAR) for all rural lands (Article 29, No. 13, Law No. 12.651/2012).

Beyond the Forest Code revision in 2012, a series of advances in environmental policies and laws has occurred over the last 20 years. Recently the National Policy for the Recovery of Native Vegetation (PROVEG) was launched with the objective to recover degraded areas and promote sustainable land use practices.

The additional Recovery of Native Vegetation Plan (PLANAVEG), launched through International Ordinance No. 230, dated November 14, 2017 will support the continuation of the implementation of the Forest Code, providing incentives to smallholders, indigenous peoples and traditional communities, having as a basis the information from the National System of Information on Forests (SINIF).

## II. Summary of the Secretariat's assessment

### 2.1 Project background

The semi-arid region of Northeast Brazil (NEB) has been experiencing periodic droughts with chronic water scarcity. Average temperature increased by 0.8 degrees Celsius between 1951 and 2013, while average annual rainfall was reduced by 4% over the same period. Severe droughts occurred in 1993 and 2012, with the overall drought frequency, intensity, and severity increasing since 1976. These climate drivers have had significant impacts on agriculture; for example, by these climate-driven impacts, with the average crop area lost being 221,973 hectares per year from 1985 to 2014. The average annual rainfall in Northeast Brazil has decreased between 0.5 and 2.0 degrees Celsius in the period 2011–2040, compared with the baseline period 1961–1990.

### The project proposes to address these observed and projected climate impacts and to build the resilience of the most vulnerable and the poorest farmers in NEB. At the same time, the project will promote low-emission agriculture and restore forests, thereby generating emissions reductions and increasing carbon sequestration.

The project will be implemented for eight years, with a requested GCF funding of USD 99.5 million (USD 65 million from the Global Environment Facility (GEF) and USD 34.5 million from other sources), including from the International Fund for Agricultural Development (IFAD) of USD 30 million, the Brazilian Development Bank (BNDES) of USD 59.3 million and participating States of USD 1.3 million.

At the same time, the project will directly benefit one million farmers in at least three States of NEB through the introduction of climate resilient productive systems (CRPS) and increasing their resilience to climate change.

- Implement CRPS for family farmers and traditional communities in their family farming and household gardens.
- Water access for production; and
- Knowledge management and scaling up.

### Component-by-component analysis

#### Component 1: Climate resilient productive systems (Total cost: USD 76.5 million, GCF cost: USD 54 million, or 71% of the total project cost)

Output 1 will implement CRPS for family farmers and traditional communities in their family farming and household gardens. It will also implement sustainable land management practices in larger areas of the Cerrado region to reduce deforestation and restore degraded lands. It will develop territorial resilience investment plans (TRIPs), which will be used as a basis for planning and implementing climate-resilient agricultural activities, including setting up resilient backyard gardens, reducing land degradation in areas of collective farming, promoting agroforestry systems, diversifying crops, improving water use efficiency, adopting biofertilizers, and local entrepreneurship for family farming.

One of the key challenges this output aims to address is the increase in soil salinity and brackishness due to overgrazing and poor land management practices. The proposed solution - basinal agriculture with halophyte plants combined with collective desalination systems - is assessed as being adequate and reasonable for managing the baseline situation in the regional context. The CRPS is also deemed to be the optimal solution in semi-arid NEB, given its effect on recharging soil moisture, soil carbon, retaining water and improving microclimate.

The other challenge of increasing land degradation due to overgrazing and timber extraction can be addressed by promoting agroforestry systems that provide more efficient stoves and biodigesters that will reduce the demand for timber extraction. It is anticipated that under this scenario, farmers would also adopt practices such as crop rotation, intercropping, mixed farming, and agroecology to reduce land degradation, aggravating their livelihoods by reducing agricultural production. Overall, the proposed solutions are adequate and reasonable for managing the baseline situation in the target region.

### 3. Guiding concepts on gender in the context of socio-environmental projects

To guarantee transformations in gender balance in social relations, in addition to increasing empowerment of women, a concomitant treatment of gender is needed in social projects, programs, and policies. The notion of gender mainstreaming in social projects, programs, and policies implies that gender equality should be considered throughout the cycle—from design to implementation and monitoring. The assumption is that gender must be intrinsic to the project, program, or policy. This means that it is necessary to establish a dialogue with beneficiaries to the monitoring and evaluation system. From this perspective, the project is committed to a concomitant approach to gender, based on the understanding that it is not a mere tool for achieving other objectives but an essential element of the project. The goal depends on this gender-centric perspective. Also, gender should ideally be tackled with an inter-sectoral approach to social inequalities through a deep understanding of its connection with environmental issues.

Some lessons learned from a gender diagnosis conducted with six IAFI-supported projects in rural areas show that there is a need for a concomitant approach to gender and that this project can incorporate the gender approach transversally in its goals, strategies, and actions. In most projects analyzed, the importance of prioritizing rural women as beneficiaries was not considered. This has led to low levels of participation by rural women in decision-making processes, both in the public benefited by the actions and in the technical team. Although specification of minimum participation numbers for segments considered to be more prone to illustrating gender inequality is a good practice, it should be noted that this approach cannot be confused with an effective and cohesive implementation strategy that aims to overcome social inequalities.
