### A2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

The project "Building climate resilience of vulnerable and food insecure communities through capacity strengthening and livelihood diversification in mountainous regions of Tajikistan" will increase the adaptive capacities of vulnerable households, particularly women-headed households, living in Khujand, Khatlon, and Badakhshan Autonomous Regions (GBRA) domains. Due to their location, these communities are vulnerable to the negative impacts of climate change such as increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events, increasing temperatures with glacial melting and sudden onset extreme events causing loss and damage, in addition to high levels of malnutrition and poverty (See Annex 1). The proposed project intervention takes a transformative approach to ensure food security and support communities to adapt to climate change through two key components:

i. Capacity strengthening and awareness raising of food insecure climate vulnerable communities and households for enhanced rural resilience and food security; and
ii. Resilience building at household level through livelihood diversification of livelihoods and improved market access.

A key element of this approach will be addressing institutional capacity needs, at both national and sub-national levels to better enable the support vulnerable communities develop effective responses to prepare for and manage climate risks. Interventions of the project will reach 50,000 direct beneficiaries among the estimated 270,000 vulnerable individuals residing in climate vulnerable areas; and up to 70,000 indirect beneficiaries who live in the same communities.

The project intervention is designed to create a sustainable pathway for livelihood diversification pathways in Tajikistan through the sustained impact of project measures that have high potential for replicability and scale-up. The project will also contribute to the development of an enabling environment which stimulates a demand-based model for climate information and use of ICT/mobile platforms to enable public and private sector participation, innovation, and market development. It contributes to key policies in the country and supports the implementation of the National Adaptation Plan (NAP) and other national strategies on institutions/ways knowledge generation and learning through the incorporation of climate change and food security into the education system at all levels. The project will also promote new technologies such as renewables, rehabilitates dilapidated assets that take into account the diverse needs and capacities of women and men and that strengthens community resilience through tailored social and behavioral change interventions.

Tajikistan is a food-deficit country and the poorest in the Commonwealth of Independent States. Poverty headcount ratio at national level was recorded at the rate of 31.3 % in 2015. ### Economic context - Tajikistan is a low income country with USD 2,675 per capita GDP purchasing power parity (PPP) and a population of 8.3 million in 2014. Its economy grew strongly and the poverty level substantially fell from about 80% of poverty rate in 1997 to 31.3% in 2014. Agriculture is the largest sector of the economy, which accounts for approximately 56% of GDP and employs more than half of the population. The agricultural output has been down by 4.2% in 2015 due largely to the economic downturn in the Russian Federation and weak global demand, despite a significant increase in the export volume of cotton (from 378 thousand tons in 2014 to 690 thousand tons in 2015). ### C.2 Project / Programme Objective against Baseline

#### Background

Tajikistan has been identified as one of the countries that are highly vulnerable to the adverse impacts of global climate change, particularly susceptible to low agricultural productivity, water stress and high losses from natural disasters. The country is located in a mountainous region with an average altitude above sea level of 1300 meters. Its cold climate has always been an important factor determining the incomes and lives of the rural population. Variation in precipitation patterns, glacial and permafrost degradation, forest fires, outbreaks of pests and diseases are also expected to increase as a result of global warming and climate change.

#### Project/Programme Objectives

The project aims to reduce vulnerability of households to adverse effects of climate change. Recurrent natural disasters including droughts and floods are already impacting low-income rural households, slowly eroding their capacity to cope with and recover from each event.

During the period of 1940-2010, temperature rise was recorded at an average rate of 0.18°C per decade, annual precipitation by -30% in mountainous areas and +5% in lowland areas. In 2009, droughts over the period also affected the large parts of the country. Current climate change projections appear to have followed that trend.

By 2030, the average temperature is projected to increase by 2.3°C. Average rainfall is likely to increase by 8% in the territories up to 2500 m above sea level and decrease by 3% in the mountainous areas. Climate change could affect all sectors of Tajikistan's economy.

The country is likely to experience considerable additional economic losses, humanitarian stress and environmental degradation unless measures are taken to reduce vulnerability and promote adaptation are not put in place. More frequent drought-induced extreme weather events may adversely impact food security and stability of both human and natural systems and further exacerbate climate-induced losses and damages.

#### Distributional Effects

The distributional effects are more likely to fall upon those in subsistence agriculture, particularly in Vatshkent District. The main causes for this are the increased frequency of droughts, soil erosion and droughts, which effectively decreases water availability for agriculture. Overall, land degradation, soil erosion and loss of biodiversity and ecosystems are driven primarily by inadequate environmental management, dilapidated agricultural infrastructure, poor land use planning, changed drainage systems, as well as lack of education and awareness of the impacts of land degradation.

Looking towards the future, three direct main channels exist through which adverse climate conditions can affect people's livelihoods from agriculture: (1) decline in agricultural productivity/yields; (2) reduction in agricultural water supply; (3) increase in food prices. The World Bank (2012) estimates that a 5% decrease in rainfall results that a 20% decline in agricultural productivity due to climate change could increase the national poverty rate by 8%, and the rural poverty rate by 9%. A similar increase of relative food prices can lead to a rise in poverty rate by 16% and poverty gauge by 25%.

In terms of projections, climatic models (CCSM3, ECHAM4 and CSIRO) used for the preparation of the Third National Communication under UNFCCC (2014), highlight that the amount of precipitation in Tajikistan is likely to decrease by 16% and increase by 8%.

# Detailed Project / Programme Description

## Limited Awareness of Climate Change and its Impacts on Livelihoods and on Nutrition

During community consultations, most village heads mentioned that although the impacts of climate change are very much felt, the phenomenon itself has not been understood by most villagers. Communities mostly rely on traditional practices for food production and consumption, which often lead to overexploitation of natural resources and can result in making inaccurate decisions regarding allocation and use of natural resources at their disposal.

## Limited Adaptive Capacities of Communities

Communities in rural Tajikistan often lack livelihoods due to the impact of climate change and variability. Recurrent droughts in the past decade have led to low (nearly zero) to no migration from rural areas, which has left women and families with no other source of income other than remittances and donations (from charities). During the current drought, many households tend to sell off their productive assets (livestock) when there is a shortage of grain. Another coping mechanism for these communities is that women only let it all lie due to lack of planning and awareness.

## Limited Knowledge and Capacities at Government Level

Despite increased investments done in recent years to support climate change adaptation, the level of awareness with regards to climate change especially at the regional and district levels is high. Whilst the government has submitted three national communications and is currently drafting its National Climate Change Adaptation Strategy, there are no adaptation plans at the District Development Plans (DDPs) or at the Village Development Plans (VDPs). There is also a lack of capacity at the local level to forecast weather based on available information and data. Lack of inter-ministerial communication is another key challenge facing the country.

## Reliance on Climate Sensitive Sources of Income

The population of rural Tajikistan mostly relies on agriculture-based livelihoods, and even though it is an underdeveloped sector in the country, it forms a significant part of the economy. This reliance on climate-sensitive sources of income has led to significant economic impacts for many people, driving significant levels of external labour migration (mainly to Russia) leaving behind children and women who take multiple responsibilities in their roles as sole household heads.

## Increased Losses Due to Droughts and Heatwaves

In addition to the lack of access to climate proof storage to protect their harvest from disasters like floods and extreme temperatures, many communities also have limited access to irrigation systems. These factors often lead to increased losses due to droughts and heatwaves.

## Lack of Strong User Associations at Community Levels to Manage Common Assets

Farmers in communities often plan to produce crops based on what fetches them the highest price from the middlemen. This fluctuates with the market, which is defined by predominantly rural women who are not well informed about the losses borne by farmers during the production process. ## Approach and targeting

In order to enable highly vulnerable and poor communities to engage in assets creation under Component 2, farmers receive a vital conditional cash transfer from WFP. The transfer is conditional to the completion (building or rehabilitation) of the community level adaptation assets identified during community consultations. In the short term, these transfers help people to meet their immediate food needs by providing them with high-yield seeds, low stocks, and high prices; and helps them meet immediate food needs. In the long-term, assets increase communities' ability to adapt to climate change and improve land productivity.

In this context of the green transfer, transfers are a crucial element for those who are most vulnerable populations to engage in climate adaptation activities, based on the following elements:

- Compared to many other organizations, WFP targets the most vulnerable rural households - both farmers and pastoralists. The transfers are conditional upon completion of community level adaptation assets that address the impacts of climate change. Transfers are therefore a necessary enabling condition for these populations to participate in the project.
- Without this transfer, the most vulnerable people would not be able to feed themselves; in practical terms, without conditional transfers, the most vulnerable people would need to use their time and efforts to provide food for their families to meet their current needs. This means that they will have no capacity or resources left over to invest in assets to build resilience against shocks in the future. This means that without the enabling element of an initial transfer, those who are in greatest need may not be able to participate in the project and will not be able to build up their own climate change adaptation assets. This, in turn, would undermine the sustainability of the project.
- By establishing these vulnerable households to participate in climate change adaptation activities, transfers associated with asset creation can help them adapt to climate change. As bring some of the most vulnerable people from a subsistence to a sustainable livelihood level by (1) reducing pressure on landscapes and the natural environment; (2) increasing their adaptive capacity through training, creation and management of climate adaptation assets; and (3) by improving productivity and building economic protection from shocks, thereby preventing relapse into poverty.

### RATIONALE FOR GCF INVOLVEMENT

#### D1.Value Added for GCF Involvement

Gaps in food security related adaptation interventions: While climate change adaptation at the national levels has been a priority under the recent climate change related projects, many gaps still exist in introducing adaptation measures to address these issues. The majority of the population is engaged in smallholder rain-fed agriculture and livestock production, which are highly vulnerable to climate change impacts such as increased temperatures, reduced rainfall, and more frequent droughts. These changes have led to the displacement of communities, loss of life and assets, and caused an overall reduction of community resilience, outmigration - especially affecting women who are more vulnerable to climate change than men. The government has been working on introducing adaptation measures in agriculture through improved seed production services. To do that, private sector investment is limited due to the insolvency of banks to provide loans for smallholder farmers and lack of trust between the government and the private sector. In communities in rural Tajikistan, it is not expected to generate a revenue or profit for the government and beneficiaries involved. The project however, will enable the beneficiaries to diversify their livelihoods, build community and household resilience, prevent loss to property and make informed decisions on the use of available natural resources.

#### High vulnerability: Climate change poses significant threats to sustainable poverty reduction in Tajikistan. The country has been ranked as one of the most vulnerable of twenty-eight countries in the World Bank’s Europe and Central Asia region*2013. Tajikistan is highly dependent on agriculture for its economy, employment, and food security, especially for the rural poor. Climate change threatens livelihoods in Tajikistan mostly because of low adaptive capacity. Vulnerability to climate change is high due to a combination of factors including poverty, lack of education, and numerous institutional constraints, especially in the agricultural sector. At the local level, the relevant civil society organizations continue to employ traditional approaches focusing primarily on increasing production, improving water management and irrigation infrastructure, and promoting agroforestry systems as strategies for mitigating climate change.

Creating new enabling environments: Update of climate services by communities could potentially create conditions for investments by public and private sector actors to package and use this information to serve specialized needs across sectors and businesses. The project will enable producers and users of climate information to access, interpret, and use climate information in a more effective manner through initiatives such as ICT/mobile technologies weather advisories. The project will provide the private sector (small-scale farmers) with the necessary tools and knowledge to make informed decisions on how best they are required to make informed decisions to increase productivity and make medium and longterm investments.

A GCF funding will enable Tajikistan to expand its existing capacity to meet its targets. In addition, through partnerships with other ministries at the district level, the project will be able to further enhance capacities in climate change adaptation and disaster risk reduction (DRR) for all relevant stakeholders across the country. Without the proposed project, the government of Tajikistan cannot mobilize development of the national and regional systems to reduce mortality for its vulnerable populations and decreasing risks on climate sensitive businesses.

The increase in temperature and evaporation rates will lead to a higher demand for irrigation (driven by higher temperatures that push up evapotranspiration). Combined with increased heat extremes, this will result in reduced water availability for agriculture. In addition, more frequent droughts can be expected. Agricultural yields could drop by as much as 30% in some parts of Tajikistan by the turn of the century. At the same time, given the mountainous nature of the terrain in Tajikistan (97% of its country is over 1500m above sea level), increased precipitation will lead to more frequent and intense floods. Floods are likely to cause damage to infrastructure and agricultural land. The risk of landslides caused by intense rainfall, snowmelt during spring, and extreme floods of meltwater during spells of hot weather in the summer months is also expected to increase.

### H2. Arrangements for Monitoring, Reporting and Evaluation

Project monitoring and evaluation (M&E) will be carried out in accordance with WFP procedures, under WFP supervision. WFP will assume financial oversight of the project and provide information on a regular basis in conjunction with GCF operations. The project management team will be responsible for outcome monitoring and evaluation; project management team meetings will take place at least twice per year.

In addition to semi-annual performance reports, technical reports and a yearly performance report, specific M&E activities include:

* **A Project Inception Workshop (IW):** Will bring together all stakeholders for project implementation within the first two months after project approval. Through this workshop, stakeholders and local leaders will build project ownership and discuss priorities with community members, including women and elders. A supervision plan will be agreed upon by relevant stakeholders during this workshop.

* **An Annual Progress Report (APR):** Will be prepared by the project management team and evaluate yearly project progress, using identified M&E indicators. The APR will identify short-term objectives and targets, lessons learned and risk mitigation measures. It will also include a summary of project achievements, challenges faced, and any adjustments to planned activities and physical progress reports as required with compliance with the requirements of the environmental and social assessment and management plan (ESAMP) and the GCF investment criteria.

An independent and external Mid-Term Evaluation will take place at the end of two years after project implementation (Nov/Dec 2018). This evaluation will assess progress toward outcome achievements, assess financial, social and environmental risks and pinpoint corrective actions as required. It will present initial lessons learned on project implementation and management.

An independent and external final evaluation will also be submitted no later than nine months after the completion of the project. These reports will assess progress towards the project's outcomes and impacts defined in the logical framework as well as the overall project performance against the six GCF investment criteria. Final evaluations will include information on challenges and lessons learnt.
