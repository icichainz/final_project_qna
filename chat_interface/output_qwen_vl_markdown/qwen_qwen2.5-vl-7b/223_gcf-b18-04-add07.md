### A2. Project Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

Nauru is the world's smallest island nation, isolated deep in the Pacific. It depends almost entirely on its port for support of its economy and for access to markets. The economy needs to survive. Yet aging dysfunctional port facilities are completely exposed to the effects of climate change.

Awei Port’s century-old design leaves visiting ships totally unprotected from sea and climate conditions. The offshore leading edge is vulnerable to wave attack, with a 10% chance that waves will breach the breakwater. Shipyard shutdowns are chronic for 3 months a year, and shipping costs are climbing. Some ships already refuse to serve the port.

Western swells and waves that pose the main challenges at Awei are driven by the El Niño Southern Oscillation, the South Pacific Convergence Zone, the Western Pacific Monsoon, and distant tropical cyclonic activity. The great uncertainty about how climate change will affect these drivers raises the significant risk that the wave climate at the port will worsen in coming decades.

The alternative proposed for GCF’s support is construction of a climate-resilient port that can operate year-round. Of five designs approved, the one selected comprises: (i) a channel through which oceangoing ships can pass between the area where they anchor and the breakwater; (ii) a new breakwater to protect the port from waves; (iii) berths to shelter the wave and berth from winds; and (iv) port buildings, container terminal and port security provisions comprising a modern facility that will be climate-resilient. The project is estimated at US$105 million. The project will also help reform port governance and management with capacity building of Nauru Port Authority (NPA) to ensure financial sustainability and operational sustainability.

The new port facilities will mean the ships spend considerably less time at sea, leading to significant reduction in CO2 emissions from the shipping sector.

Impact potential: All Nauru’s 13,100 people depend in multiple ways on the country's sea link. Making the port fully functional and climate-resilient will benefit them all. Over the 50 years of its lifetime, estimated reductions in CO2 emissions are 535,400 tons.

Paradigm shift potential: Ports are at the forefront of infrastructure risk of climate change and the project can provide replicable solutions for many Pacific islands that face similar challenges. Reliable maritime connectivity can transform economies and improve access to markets and services.

Sustainable development potential, and needs of the recipient: Making the port a lifeline, rather than a choke point, will create new opportunities for development, employment, and poverty reduction; bring down high costs of such basics as food and medicine; and reduce vulnerability to climate change. The project will also improve supplies. Education will benefit. The gender action plan (GAP) promotes benefits for women.

Country ownership: Government leaders, including the National Designated Authority (NDA), consider the project a priority. They have been involved in the planning process through consultation. Note Annex 1A for a 'no objection' letter from the NDA and Annex 4 for commitment letters from the Government of Nauru and Development Partners.

Efficiency and effectiveness: The ratio of benefits returned for GCF’s support will be high for GCF investments in the Pacific region. Several development partners are contributing. GCF's contribution covers 41% of total project costs, or an estimated 75% of costs eligible for climate change adaptation.

### Nauru: Overview and Challenges

Nauru is one of the smallest and most remote countries in the world. It has only a limited human resource pool and little economic diversity. Like all the Pacific island states, it is not only extremely exposed to the effects of climate and other natural hazards but also vulnerable to the impacts of global environmental change.

#### Key Statistics

- **Overall unemployment rate** in 2011—22.9%
- **Youth unemployment rate** in 2011—one more than 45.0%
- **Life expectancy at birth** in 2011—60.3 years
- **Infant mortality rate** per 1,000 live births in 2011—38

Nauru's small size, location and remote total reliance on maritime transport makes it a focal point of this vulnerability. Barriers such as some passenger air traffic, its economic, social, and cultural exchanges with the rest of the Pacific region and beyond are due to sea. Its current economy, any future economic development, food security, energy supply, and other basic services are all heavily dependent on maritime transport.

### The Port Sector

The port sector is a critical component in Nauru's economy. It provides access for import and export trade, as well as the delivery of essential goods such as food and medicine. However, the current port facilities have four main components:

1. **A small, protected harbor on the Aliewa shore**: Nauru’s main port is located at Aliewa on the west coast, where a small, protected harbor was first built more than 150 years ago (Figure 2). Only small boats and flat barges can cross over the reef to access this harbor. The harbor has been upgraded several times since its construction in 1863; however, it is currently only used by small boats such as the barges and lighters that must currently be loaded and unloaded manually on the beach.

The port sector plays a crucial role in Nauru's economy, providing access to essential goods. However, the current facilities are limited and vulnerable to climate change impacts. The project aims to improve these facilities to enhance maritime transport and support economic development.

### Climatic challenges to offshore cargo handling.

Ships conditions dictate the pace at which the ship-barge interchanges can be undertaken, as well as when they can be risked at all. In rough weather, the process is slow and complex. When sea conditions become particularly difficult, cargo and container vessels cannot use the mooring system and must be set to sail. This means that ships are unable to load or unload their cargoes (Figure 5). The ship-to-ship transfer of containers currently inapplicable.) The transits to and from the ships and the small harbour are also difficult and dangerous in heavy seas, and when conditions deteriorate, they become impossible.

### Phosphate exports using mooring system.

Overlapping bulk phosphate carriers are moored at sea to the offshore mooring system prior to loading. The phosphate is transported by barge along the cantilever arms and deposited into a vessel's hold. After loading, the ships are repositioned and pulled away from shore whenever the cantilever loaders must be moved for proper loading.

### Fuel imports using mooring system.

After a tanker is secured at sea using the port’s mooring system, fuel is pumped through the port’s flexible hose equipment to facilities on land.

### Climatic challenges to use of mooring system for phosphate and fuel shipments.

Phosphate bulk carrier and fuel tankers are often unable to berth in rough weather. Loading and offloading in poor conditions often requires the rental of specialized and expensive tugs from Solomon Islands to control this drift. With the rising cost of these tugs, these tugs are now needed to hold all such vessels in place while loading and discharging their cargoes (Figure 6).

### Shifts to fishing harbour during Alevu’s shutdowns.

When ocean conditions are extreme at Alevu but calm at the small Anare fishing harbour on the island's opposite coast, some limited loading and offloading operations are undertaken there.

### Climate Hazards Facing Awo Port Facilities and Operations

Because Nauru's main port of Awo is on the island's western coast, it is largely protected from easterly winds and waves but can be affected by westerly winds and waves. This section examines the climate factors that either alone or in combination affect the facilities and operations at Awo port: westerly waves; westerly winds; sea level; and, potentially, rainfall and temperature.

Westerly waves. There are two sources of westerly waves: the westerly winds driven by the Western Pacific Monsoon (WPMM), and tropical cyclones. Nauru does not experience tropical cyclones directly, since it lies close to the equator, but they regularly pass to the southwest. Even a distant cyclone can create ocean swells that cause strong, westerly swell waves at Awo port. These waves can be generated from swells causing extreme waves and flooding in the Pacific several thousand kilometers distant from the actual storm.

The rose diagrams in Figure 7 illustrate the major role played by westerly winds and tropical cyclones in the wave climate of Nauru's main port. The diagram on the left shows that the dear majority of waves at Awo are easterly and small, below 1.75 m in height, except for the night, which does not show any significant waves. This clearly shows that the clear majority of high waves are westerly. This is due to the WPMM and tropical cyclones. A full analysis of wind and wave climate at Nauru's main port can be found in Chapter 20 of IPCC (2013).

## Facility Damage

The damage to the port’s still unrepaired mooring system occurred during an attempt by an ocean-going ship to manoeuvre away from the mooring due to rough weather.

### Additional Operational Costs

The costs of rerouting ships via Solomon Islands to avoid congestion for cargo transfer on the Awo route are high. When Aewa cannot operate due to weather and the unloading and loading of cargo is attempted at the ill-suited, ill-equipped Anibang fishing harbour on the far side of the island, the process is slow and inefficient.

### Diminished Sea Link

The delays, technical challenges, added expenses, and risks caused by weather and the species of fish caught at sea cargo handling at Aewo port have affected the willingness of shipping companies to continue maritime transport routes to and from Nauru. Due in part to this, Matson Shipping suspended services to Nauru in 2018, which has had a significant impact on the country’s trade with the Mariana route.

### Planned Infrastructure Improvements

The baseline scenario is a government plan to address the current infrastructure damage and operational challenges at Aewo port. The following improvements are planned:

- **Fix and retain mooring system**: Nauru would maintain and repair the currently damaged offshore mooring platform, which will be either refurbished or replaced once this task was completed.
- **Employ tugs during rough weather**: The rental and use of specialised tugs to assist these operations when the sea is too rough for ships to operate.
- **Upgrade dilapidated shore facilities**: New onshore facilities and buildings would be installed to replace those that are no longer functional.

### Current and Future Climate Hazards

These baseline interventions would merely mitigate current operational risks and fail to address the growing risks posed by climate hazards. The projected impacts of climate change will have a significant effect on Nauru’s port operations and facilities, as discussed in this section.

## Projected Climate Change and Impacts on Awo Port Operations and Facilities

This section looks at how climate change will alter the main climatic factors currently affecting Nauru's port operations and facilities. As discussed, these are sea level; westerly winds; westerly waves; and, potentially, rainfall and temperature.

### Wave Climate

The wave climate is currently contributing to the effective closure of Awo operations for about 3 months each year. The two forces directly driving westerly waves are westerly winds and distant tropical cyclones.

- **Westerly Winds**: Westerly winds prevail when the WPM prevails.
- **Westerly Waves**: The details of the factors determining the duration and intensity of westerly waves are complex, but they are influenced by the Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and the location of South Pacific Convergence Zone (SPCZ). Confidence is very high that El Niño and La Niña events will continue to occur under climate change, but there is little consensus on whether climate change will cause El Niño or La Niña to change in intensity or frequency. ## Tropical cyclones

Tropical cyclones are a direct cause of high swell waves at Nauru. The factors influencing the origin of tropical cyclones include their frequency and distance from Nauru. Hence, should climate change affect the frequency, path, or intensity of tropical cyclones, this could have a major impact on swell levels at Nauru.

The factors that influence the frequency, path, and intensity of tropical cyclones are complex. Climate change will also affect the path, intensity, and frequency of tropical cyclones in the South Pacific; some climate models project more frequent and intense tropical cyclones (IPCC 2013). However, other climate models do not indicate that climate change may influence these cyclones, but it may also threaten to possibly cause more intense or more frequent swell waves at Nauru's Avo port.

There is some scientific certainty that climate change will render the baseline project (a) more vulnerable to adverse effects of climate change than it would be without such effects. ## Value Added for GCF Involvement

Under the baseline scenario, the port facilities and operations will continue to deteriorate and be threatened by climate change. This is not a climate-resilient scenario. There is a credible risk that under the baseline scenario the port will reach its operational limits before 2035 and major investments are not affordable or appropriate. The estimated cost of improvements and upgrades to baseline scenario is $28.4 million.

Nauru is a SIDS and is highly vulnerable to external economic shock and to climate change. Its small size, remoteness from ports and markets, and lack of access to international financial institutions make it difficult for Nauru to obtain concessional credit on favourable terms. As a SIDS with high debt levels and as a nation highly vulnerable to climate change, Nauru requests grant financing.

GCF funding will add value by supporting the construction of a climate-resilient alternative to the baseline scenario that is not only resilient to the real risk of future operational degradation and breakdown due to climate change. The alternative will deliver real lasting benefits to the population of Nauru and secure the maritime transport link and sea connections.

The GCF-supported climate resilient alternative has relatively high (in cost) first costs of $65.20 million. This is $38.80 million more than the baseline cost. Although the baseline will deliver benefits through some upgrades by restoring the port prior to 2035, it does not provide a long-term solution and requires additional investment in the future.

For this reason, the $38.80 million in additional investment represent climate change adaptation costs. The small size of Nauru makes it unlikely to cover these costs or to borrow this level of funding. GCF is therefore requested to contribute $26.90 million to help finance a long-term climate-resilient alternative to the baseline scenario project.

The requested to support of $26.90 million will represent:

- 41% of the overall $65.20 million project costs, or
- 73% of the additional investment required for climate change adaptation funding.


The other financing partners are the Asian Development Bank, the Government of Australia, and counterpart financing by the Government of Nauru. Together, these partners will ensure that Nauru and its people will have a climate-resilient port that can provide safe, reliable, efficient marine transport services all year round.

The GCF involvement will add value to the baseline scenario, which is not climate-resilient, by making the port climate resilient.

### Mitigation Potential

In both with and without project scenarios, all ships use equal time and fuel to reach Nauru waters. However, in the with project scenario, the port will be operational for 365 days a year, compared to only 200 days currently being unloaded/loaded, thereby leading to significant reduction in CO₂ emissions from the shipping sector. In addition, the pusher barges make many trips from the container ship to assist loading/unloading operations. In the conceptualization and design of this project, an extensive assessment of climate, meteorological, and ocean conditions was undertaken using data from Nauru and similar sites and hindcast methodology (see Annex 2). This context is important because it provides a realistic basis for assessing the vulnerability to climate change. Although not entirely complete due to incomplete data, inadequate understanding of climate-meteorology-ocean interactions, and other limitations, this assessment provided an initial framework for the application of such techniques and tools in Nauru and possibly for small ports in the South Pacific.

The port options assessment and design process was undertaken using a combination of physical modeling and computer simulation. The use of such techniques and tools in Nauru is possible but limited by the availability of resources (PorPhos 200 excavation) to provide the best possible solution within the financial and logistical constraints of a remote island.

In addition, a Full Mission Bridge Ship Simulator was used to assess the navigation aspects of options under different meteorological conditions. This was used to develop a realistic assessment of the constructability of the options, given the constraints and conditions in Nauru, using experienced mariners and port engineers in the simulations. These simulations were undertaken with the assistance of the Australian Maritime Safety Authority (AMSA) and examined the challenges of entering the berth, docking, and exiting the berth for every option.

The simulations allowed the project team to assess the constructability of each option and to identify the most suitable option from a technical perspective. This is a technological best practice and not often used by small port developments.

Finally, component 2 reform of the NPA is best practice to ensure the institutional and financial sustainability of the project.

## Selected Risk Factor 4: Unexpected rough weather during construction

As with any marine construction project, the weather can have a significant adverse impact on progress. The works have been designed to avoid the need for a floating crane, which would be vulnerable to weather risks. This risk will be largely carried by the contractor. Use of an experienced contractor, preferably with local knowledge and experience, will minimize this risk. The works should be scheduled and planned to minimize exposure to unexpected or inclement weather. Construction is not recommended between mid-October and mid-April through to November to avoid the likely periods of highest wave activity on the west coast of Nauru.
