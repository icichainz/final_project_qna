### A.2 Project Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

The project addresses climate change adaptation and mitigation to improve water security and implement climate resilience throughout Grenada's entire national water sector. The project's holistic approach addresses two main climate risks and vulnerabilities of Grenada: freshwater availability and disaster preparedness. Other Caribbean communities share these vulnerabilities, making the lessons learned from this project applicable regionally.

Climate change poses a severe threat to Grenada's water supply because the small island developing state (SIDS) relies on surface water for 90% of its drinking water needs. The country has already begun to aggravate with the problem with an increasing average temperature and more erratic rainfall. More frequent heavy rainfall events make water supply outages more common due to high turbidity in the raw water supply, Saline intrusion is also a concern as sea levels rise.

The Vulnerability Assessment (VA) undertaken as part of the G-CREWS project preparation also indicates the water sector's vulnerability to climate change and its impacts on freshwater availability. The VA identified several key areas for intervention that will be addressed by this project.

The Government of Grenada (GoG) has demonstrated strong leadership in preparing the way for a paradigm shift in the water sector through clearly formulated national climate policy priorities, National climate change documents and strategies, including the recently adopted National Climate Change Strategy (NCCS), the National Poverty Reduction Strategy (NPRS), the Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), the recently finalized National Adaptation Plan (NAP) and the currently updated National Climate Change Action Plan (NCCAP). The GoG's 2015-2024 National Poverty Reduction Strategy highlights the impacts of climate change, including on the water sector, for Grenada's economy.

In order to avoid critical climate-induced water shortages in the future, this project supports Grenada's water sector in both risk reduction and adaptation. The project will address the current challenges associated with rainfall variability and expected future climate change until 2050. The main objective of the G-CREWS project is to increase systemic climate change resilience in Grenada's water sector. The entire population of Grenada will benefit from the project.

To achieve its objective, the project supports the water sector's comprehensive transformation on multiple levels, which represents a paradigm shift for the country and region. These levels include: households and private sector (e.g., hotels and businesses as water users), the public sector as provider of potable water and infrastructure, and behavioral changes triggered through appropriate governance, regulation, economic incentives and raising awareness.

This transformation will be achieved by:

- Climate-Resilient Water Governance
- Climate-Resilient Water Infrastructure
- Climate-Resilient Water Supply System
- Additional Contributions of the Water Sector to Grenada's NDC (financed by BMUJ, Germany)

The project is funded through a grant from BMUJ (Germany).

# B.1 Description of Financial Elements of the Project/Program

The GCF is seeking grant funding to alleviate severe climate-induced risks related to drinking water availability for almost the entire population in Grenada, particularly vulnerable groups, and the two largest and highly vulnerable economic sectors: tourism and agriculture. The GCF grant is used to overcome the Government's recent constraints to source public funding or assume additional debt to invest in urgently needed adaptation measures for the water sector. These measures are critical to protect the island’s drinking water supply from climate change impacts on the economy and the people of Grenada, with an estimated damage equivalent to 200% of GDP in 2031, leading to a lengthy debt restructuring process assisted by the Extended Credit Facility Support Programme of the International Monetary Fund (IMF). According to the IMF, Grenada’s public debt is projected to reach 85.7% of GDP in 2024 and 96.5% in 2031, despite recent achievements in fiscal consolidation; the IMF noted a number of outstanding challenges: public debt is still considered highly vulnerable, and the country has not yet demonstrated sufficient capacity to manage its debt effectively with due prudence until various reforms yield expected results. This means Grenada must continue avoiding any burden on public debt.

The GCF has formulated a clear commitment to continue reducing its debt to 60% of GDP in the coming years. The links between climate change and water security are well established, and the lack of reliable data is one of the main obstacles that are hindering the country’s implementation of long-term adaptation measures in its water sector. The Government of Grenada, with a current budget of around 903 million USD, seeks maximum concessionally from the GCF (100% grant) to support the proposed adaptation measures.

The expected total costs of the G-CREWS project are EUR 42,057 million. This includes the GCF financing of EUR 35,209 million and co-financing contributions by the Government of Grenada and other partners (e.g., BMU) for the Environment, Nature Conservation, Building and Nuclear Safety (BMUB) of EUR 6,500 million. This demonstrates the high level of Government ownership in this project especially considering the Government’s public debt constraints (see C1). The G-CREWS project is expected to generate a total cost of EUR 38,297 million.

In-kind contribution of the GCF amounting to EUR 3,338 million for e.g., staff costs, office space, the planned new Water Resources Management Unit, and the value of land acquisitions. In-kind contributions from other partners amounting to EUR 105 million for e.g., staff costs, office costs and smaller construction works, including the distribution pipelines on Carriacou island.

In-kind contribution of GDS and other organisations amounting to EUR 2,236 million.

The aid provided by the BMUB amounts to EUR 48.7 million (see supporting document 03).

The BMUB contributes EUR 5,500 million for regional learning and scaling-up activities, as well as activities to increase water and energy efficiency in the water sector, building on its existing commitment to the island over the past four years through the Climate Change Resilient Commercial Water Users project.

Furthermore, the project is expected to leverage EUR 2,89 million in private sector contributions, mainly within the Challenge Fund for Climate Resilient Commercial Water Users. However, the private sector funding is not included in table B-1.

On behalf of the GCF, GIZ seeks EUR 35,290 million in grant funding from the GCF; since the country urgently needs to adapt to the negative impacts of climate change that particularly affect Grenada’s water availability, putting additional stress on a highly vulnerable and indebted Caribbean small-island developing state.

### C1: Strategic Context

**Grenada**, located between Saint Vincent and the Grenadines to the north and Trinidad and Tobago to the south, is a small island developing state consisting of three islands: Grenada, Carriacou and Petit Martinique. The Island of Grenada consists of three main islands: St George's, St David’s and Carriacou. The total land area of these three islands is 345 square kilometres (133 square miles). In 2017, the World Bank gave the country’s population as 107,317, of which approx. 46.41% were women and girls.* The vast majority of the population lives on the biggest island Grenada. Grenada is a constitutional monarchy with Queen Elizabeth II as its head of state represented by Prime Minister. According to the Human Development Index (HDI) of 2016, Grenada’s HDI value is 0.754.* This score puts Grenada in the top 79th out of 188 countries and territories and thus places it in the high human development category.

#### Grenada's Economy

Grenada has a small open economy that is highly vulnerable not only to adverse international economic developments and shocks but also to natural disasters. The country’s economy is heavily dependent on tourism, agriculture and fishing. Due to Hurricanes and spill over effects of the recent financial crisis, Grenada built up a public debt of 114 % of GDP*, which the Government was able to reduce to 83.6% in 2014.

Tourism contributes approximately 57% of the country’s GDP* and employment is estimated at around 39%. In 2014, tourism contributed about EUR 50 million to the country’s economy*. In 2016, approximately 9 % of the workforce was employed directly in the tourism sector.

The agricultural sector has played a major role as a macroeconomic shock absorber during the adjustment period. Between 2009 and 2011, its growth rate was approximately 10 per year. Agricultural production accounted for roughly 5% of GDP in 2014. Continued strong performance in 2015, with approximately 45 % growth*, followed by a decline of 37%, in 2016. The agricultural sector is dominated by the banana and citrus industries which together employ approximately 13 % of Grenada’s workforce and also provides subsistence support to a much larger number of small-scale farmers.

Grenada's rate of unemployment has varied only slightly in the last few years (no more than 5%). In 2014, unemployment was 32.5%, dropping to 26.9% in 2014 before rising slightly to 30.4 % in 2015*. In 2015*, the highest unemployment rate was approximately 38%. The latest Labour Force Survey of Government of Grenada’s 2016 Labour Force survey found that unemployment is at 31.3% for women and 25.5 % for men.

#### National Debt

The two hurricanes Ivan (2004) and Emily (2005) were economic catastrophes. Damage costs of more than 200 % of GDP had tremendous effects on Grenada’s debt*. In addition, the economic and financial crisis hit the country hard just as it began to rebound from the hurricanes.* As a result, the GoG announced in March 2013 that it would undertake a comprehensive review of its public finances. The Government of Grenada has since then been able to reduce its debt by USD 21.7 million over a three year period.* Nonetheless, Grenada remains one of the most heavily indebted small island

#### Poverty

In spite of development progress, poverty is still very present across Grenada. The most recent Country Poverty Assessment, conducted for Grenada over the 2007-2008 period, revealed a poverty rate of 37.7 % and an indigence (extreme poverty) rate of 24 %. When considering only those living in households with income above the poverty line but vulnerable to being pushed into it, just over half of the population was classified as either poor or extremely poor.

Of people, 65% were employed at the time of the assessment, and 31.5 % of employed people were living below the poverty line. The unemployment rate for Grenada is 20%, which is significantly higher than the national average by 5.6%, in comparison with the poverty rate reported in the 1998 assessment. This increase can be explained by the successive natural and economic challenges, including severe storms, droughts, and economic crisis.

### Grenada's Water Sector

Grenada has a humid tropical climate, with an average annual temperature of 28°C. The country has two distinct seasons: a dry season (January to May) and a rainy season (June to December). On average, Grenada receives around 1,150 mm of rain per year, which is slightly below the national average.

The main rainy season delivers approximately 75 % of total annual rainfall. However, there is a considerable variation between the northern and southern parts of the island. The eastern part of the island (Carenage and Petit Martinique) are significantly drier than the main island due to their small size and relatively low elevations.

During the dry season, all three islands can experience severe drought conditions. For details on climate variability in Grenada, refer to the Climate Change section below.

#### Water Supply

Grenada is divided into 71 waterheds, which are well defined due to the steep, hilly topography. The 29 water supply facilities managed by NAWSA provide piped water to approximately 43,000 customer connections, covering 98 % of the island's population. There are two sewage collection disposal systems.

Water for irrigation is not supplied by NAWSA and is provided through private wells or from rainwater catchments. The majority of domestic water supply places either downstream of NAWSA’s dams and intake structures or in areas where there is no piped water. The responsibility for the supply of agricultural water rests primarily with the Irrigation Management Unit of the Ministry of Agriculture, Land, Forestry, Fisheries and Marine Resources (MFLFMR). There are 10 irrigation schemes on mainland Grenada.

These yield some 54,600 m³/day (12/10 day gal./day) in the rainy season and a maximum of 31,800 m³/day (7.9 day gal./day) during the dry season. The current gap between demand and yield is 22,800 m³/day (5.05 day gal./day). During the dry season clearly indicates the high risk of water shortages that Grenada already faces, in particular during a prolonged drought.

The total population served by NAWSA’s piped water supply system represents about 10 % of the present potable requirement. On the smaller islands (Carenage and Petit Martinique), domestic water supply is obtained exclusively from rainwater catchments, while for water for irrigation is supplied from groundwater or reservoirs.

## C3 Project Objective against Baseline

The main objective of this project is to increase system climate change resilience in Grenada's water sector. The project aims to achieve this by applying a multi-level approach addressing resilience at the level of water governance, households, businesses and the water supply system.

The proposed project will address the vulnerability that climate change poses to Grenada's water sector without district capacity to enhance the water sector's climate resilience, the people and ecosystems of Grenada will face increasingly severe consequences from climate change over the next three decades.

The main climate impacts affecting the water sector in Grenada are changes in temperature, precipitation, tropical storms and sea level rise. Based on recent climate impact projections, GIZ’s Vulnerability Assessment arrives at the following conclusions:

- Temperature projections provide robust guidance with clear signals for warming trends (i.e. +1.18°C to +2.72°C until 2050s).
- Projections point towards decreasing precipitation trends with a reasonable level of confidence, robust across all the scenarios (eratic and decreasing by up to -30% until 2050s with seasonal extremes of ~40% from June to August).
- Projections of future tropical storms provide limited guidance. Based on past trends and available regional climate change studies, however, a trend towards more intense and increasing storms can be expected.
- Grenada is located at sea level of less than 0.5m asl by the end of this century.

It is important to note that Grenada’s water sector is also extremely exposed and sensitive particularly to shock-like climate impacts linked to drought and heavy rainfall or tropical storms. These impacts are already a daunting reality in Grenada today and are likely to be aggravated further by climate change in the coming decades.


## Data

Confidence in climate projections and available data for Grenada are comparatively high. GIZ based its vulnerability assessment on data provided by Grenadian authorities and on recent (2017) climate model projections obtained from a Global Climate Model (GCM) ensemble, comprising five usable GCMs from the Coupled Model Inter-comparison Project CMIP5. The results of this analysis are robust and provide satisfactory results for the purposes of the assessment.

The GIZ assessment results are in agreement with publicly available vulnerability indices, for example:

- The 2015 Notre Dame Global Adaptation Country Index (ND-GAIN index) ranks Grenada at 61st among the world’s countries most at risk (down from 48th place in 1997).
- Standard & Poor’s Global Market Diversity Index ranked Grenada’s vulnerability as 96th among 116 nations.

The results are also in agreement with earlier vulnerability assessments of Grenada, such as UN ECLAC 2011, NASAP 2015, and Walingdorff 2017.

In general terms, the findings from projections and Grenadian authorities are deemed sufficient for the project to operate on the proposed baseline scenario or theory of change.


## Baseline Scenario

It is anticipated that decreasing precipitation in conjunction with rising average temperature will have the highest impact on water availability, because 90% of water on Grenada’s mainland is collected from rainwater.

Currently, under normal conditions, NAWASA provides approximately 2.41 billion imperial gallons or liters per year (11,030 million cubic meters) to customers in Grenada. This represents a supply of 586 imperial gallons (2,679 liters) per day per person. Under normal conditions, NAWASA with its current infrastructure can meet demand from 1.66 and 1.75 billion imperial gallons (7,546,000-7,955,000 m³) available for customers. Total water demand on average is approximately 2.38 billion imperial gallons or liters per year (10,600 million cubic meters). Under normal conditions, NAWASA supplies approximately 36 imperial gallons (165 liters) per day per person. During 2009-2010 drought conditions, annual supply dropped to approximately 1.42 billion imperial gallons (6,455,000 m³) while demand rose to 2.1 billion imperial gallons (9,270,000 m³). Under normal conditions, NAWASA can meet the current demand of 38 million cubic meters per year. These conditions are comparable to Grenada’s projected climate in 2050. If the current business-as-usual scenario remains unchanged, NAWASA is likely to be unable to provide sufficient water for domestic and public services would progressively decline over the next 30 years due to climate change impacts. The Vulnerability Assessment projected a gap of approximately 600 million imperial gallons (2,727,600 m³) by 2050 due to reduced rainfall.

## Barrier Assessment

To prevent a shortage scenario, Grenada needs to climate change resilient water sector. In its essence, a climate resilient water sector requires: (i) higher water efficiency stabilizing demand for potable water at a climate resilient level; and (ii) resilient water production and supply systems that reliably produce enough potable water to meet Grenada's demand at any time of the year.

### Barrier 1 - Governance

There is a lack of regulatory mechanisms. Grenada lacks a Water Resources Management authority that would manage water abstraction rights to remain under a resilient level. The current water tariff systems do not sufficiently incentivize efficient use and conservation of water resources, nor does it provide adequate financial incentives in policies with relevance for the water sector aggravate the water resource management problem, for example due to inappropriate farming practices along rivers or protection of forest resources.

### Barrier 2 - Infrastructure

There is a lack of infrastructure. The current water supply system is often used inefficiently and neglects potentials for rainwater harvesting. This puts unnecessary pressure on the scarce water resources, in particular during the dry season, when most tourists visit Grenada and farms intensify irrigation. Public instruments for promoting water efficiency are not sufficiently developed or enforced.

### Barrier 3 - Infrastructure

There is a lack of storage, both in terms of raw water and treated water capacity, which does not allow for the supply gap to be bridged during dry seasons. The current water distribution system leads to inefficiencies between different areas of Grenada's water distribution system leads to inefficiencies between supply schemes, which presents an optimal use of available water resources in certain regions of the island. This results in problems of water supply management and water resource allocation.

### Barrier 4 - Public Finance

Due to the current water tariff limit, revenue streams for NAWASA are low to finance long-term investments in climate resilient infrastructure. In addition, Grenada's public debt constraints prevent public investment in the sector.

# E.4 Needs of the Recipient

## Vulnerability and financing needs of the beneficiary country and population (Adaptation only)

### GIZ assesses Grenada's vulnerability as medium with a trend towards high.

This is in agreement with publicly available vulnerability indices: The Notre Dame Global Adaptation Index, for example, ranked Grenada at 61 with a declining trend since 2005. Negative climate change impacts are likely to become more pronounced in the coming decades—particularly due to projected declining trends in precipitation (up to -20% annual mean in the 2050s), more droughts and heavy rainfall events, and increased sea level rise.

If Grenada is not able to increase its resilience soon, negative climate change impacts will increasingly affect the entire population. The main impacts will include reduced availability of water, increasing heat stress and environmental hazards connected with extreme weather events such as hurricanes, floods, landslides, and droughts, which are already a reality today.

In 2009 and 2010, a prolonged drought caused widespread disruption in the water sector, resulting in substantial losses for the population. In addition to the direct effects of drought on food security, the lack of water also affected the hurricanes hit Grenada directly, Ivan in 2004 and Emily in 2005. The storms brought catastrophic destruction to Grenada's population, economy, and public sector. Over 40 lives were lost, 90% of the islands' homes destroyed and the damages exceeded $1 billion.

Among Grenada's population, low-income households, women-headed households, the elderly as well as women and girls in general are particularly vulnerable. One underlying reason is the segregation of roles for women, men, and children into different economic activities. Women are more likely to be responsible for water collection and other resources as well as increased burdens for women in managing the household especially in times of droughts or disasters.

Grenada's overall vulnerability also relies heavily on tourism and agriculture (see C1). Both sectors depend on weather variability, farm-related weather, infrastructure, and access to markets. In addition, Grenada's public finances are constrained by a severe debt burden and are unable to provide the necessary fiscal space for investments in enhancing the islands' resilience (see E1 for details).

### E.5 Economic and Social Development Goals in Grenada

#### Addressing of Economic and Social Development in Grenada

Grenada has a very high poverty level, as outlined in Chapter C1. Low-income groups are highly vulnerable to the water sector's impacts on their livelihoods. ## H.2 Arrangements for Monitoring, Reporting and Evaluation

Monitoring, reporting and evaluation arrangements will comply with the relevant GCF policies (as stipulated in the AMA or Project Agreements etc.). The project will introduce a results-based monitoring scheme, measuring activities (on a milestone basis) against the intended results defined in the project outline. Environmental and Social Monitoring will be done to verify whether predicted impacts have been achieved as well as to assess whether planned interventions in the project are implemented and to verify their effectiveness. The results-based monitoring will also identify any unforeseen risks that might affect the project implementation. The monitoring will be documented via Excel based monitoring sheets addressing the process over time and continuously.

- Assessing the adaptation context of the project.
  - Monitoring milestones to take stock of the progress made towards the intended results.
  - Monitoring output indicators.
  - Monitoring outcomes and impacts.

The monitoring instrument consists of three dimensions: Dimension 1 denotes the building of adaptive capacity, such as creating or strengthening the abilities of the relevant actors in the water sector to better respond to climate variability and change. Dimension 2 refers to adaptation actions that directly lead to adaptation while activities under Dimension 1 only create the potential for effective adaptation to take place but are key to achieve the paradigm shift. Dimension 3 ensures that the project contributes to sustainable development in spite of the adverse effects of climate change.

In order to achieve sustainable development goals, both adaptive capacity and adaptation actions are necessary. This is why all three dimensions complement each other.

The Steering Committee of the G-CREWS project - in collaboration with the National Climate Change Committee (NCCC) and the NDA - will provide overall strategic oversight for the project.

Monitoring schedule and context: GIZ undertakes semi-annual reviews to assess the progress of project implementation and completion. These reviews are carried out by the GIZ team to monitor progress in achieving project outputs and to monitor evidence of use of funds. A mid-term review is carried out in year three of the project or at any time that GIZ, the NDA and/or the Steering Committee deems necessary.

The mid-term review includes: (i) a review of the assumptions and risks included in the design and monitoring framework; (ii) an assessment of whether the project has been implemented as planned and whether any deviations have occurred due to altered conditions; (iii) a review of the achievement of planned impacts; (iv) an assessment of the need to restrict or expand the scope of the project, including the need for additional resources.

Upon completion of the G-CREWS project, GIZ carries out a project completion mission, in which the implementation of the project based on the project and financing agreements, the delivery of outputs and the achievement of project targets are monitored. The results will be reported to the NCCC and the NDA. Actual performance indicators are reported and compared to the expected results. Wherever necessary, adaptation of operational procedures or other measures may be required.

At the time of the project's physical completion and commissioning, and before the expiry of the guarantee period, MoF/PCU and GIZ will submit a final report on the measures carried out.

# Annex 2 Campaigns in the Context of the Communication Strategy in Activity 2.2

## Campaign 1: Enhancing knowledge about the water sector and the impacts of climate change

This includes issues like:
- the hydrological cycle in Grenada;
- water availability, demand, and water shortage;
- the predicted impacts of climate change on the water sector;
- links between water, climate and health; and
- the disaster risk of the water supply system.

## Campaign 2: Providing education and increasing awareness about efficient water use and rainwater harvesting

This includes, for example:
- information on the Challenge Fund;
- technical and financial information on options to reduce water consumption;
- technical and financial information on options to use alternative water sources such as rainwater; and
- information on relevant regional and international organizations, bodies and technologies to support implementation of water efficiency and rainwater harvesting approaches, if required.

## Campaign 3: Building trust in the new water governance structures, including the necessity and impacts of new water tariffs

This includes, for example:
- public consultation on and awareness of new policies, regulations and acts;
- public consultation on and awareness of the new tariff regime; and
- information on why a Water Resources Management Unit is needed, what its role and responsibilities are, and how the broader public can participate and obtain information from the WRMU.

## Campaign 4: Raising awareness about the G-COREN's project

This includes, for example:
- the objectives, activities and benefits of the project;
- progress reports of the project ;
- highlighting major implementation steps; and
- organizing outreach and learning events, in close cooperation with the OECS, CCCCC and/or CARICOM.

All your campaigns will make use of the three-step approach of a publication plan (brochures, flyers, etc.), public communication plan (press releases, fairs and school events, TV and radio appearances and spots etc.) and social media plan. In addition, in particular for the regional dimension of the Campaign 4, close and long-term links between GIZ, GoG and NAWASA with the OECS, CCCCC, Caribbean Water and Sewage Association (CWSA), CARICOM Secretariat, and other relevant organizations will be established from the beginning.
