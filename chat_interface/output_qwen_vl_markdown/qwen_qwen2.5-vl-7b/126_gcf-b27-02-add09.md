A.21. Executive summary (max. 400 words, approximately 1.5 pages)

Energy and land use are two of the main sources of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in many Latin American countries. The objective of the Programme “Green Climate Finance Facility for Local Financial Institutions (LFIs)” is to link small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), including financial institutions, with climate change projects in the renewable energy, energy efficiency and land use sectors.

The Programme will achieve this by helping local market actors from the agriculture, forestry, energy industry and transport sectors to access finance for their projects. The programme will also help SMEs, for example: small businesses do not have trusted relationships with mainstream commercial banks – as well known financial institutions are often too large and complex to provide the kind of support needed. Under this programme we will make available LFIs a Green Climate Credit Line; it will also implement ten grant-funded activities with the objective of building capacity in the LFI sector, including: technical assistance for project matchmaking between LFIs, Technology andendor Solution Providers (TSPs), and SMEs (including farmers); performance-based financing mechanisms for TSPs; technology support and capacity building for LFIs and TSPs; and knowledge learning.

For this first Programme roll out, four countries will be targeted (Colombia, Ecuador, Panama and Peru), which have a considerable share of the region’s GHG emissions. The programme is based on the use of its existing priority energy and land use market project sectors: uncrowded financial markets for local climate change projects, and the short-term nature of the projects themselves. If this Programme proves successful in these four countries, it could be easily replicated in other Latin American countries and in other regions.

This Programme will achieve 10.75 million tCO2e in lifetime GHG emission reductions while training personnel at all LFI levels on climate change finance. The programme will also increase the capacity of LFIs to access and increase other local market actors (e.g., SME employees, business owners connected from sub-loans, green TSPs, banks’ credit managers, public officials, among others) at a total cost of USD $143.50 per tCO2e avoided.

CAF is the overall executing entity for the programme.
GCF reimbursable proceeds will be channeled by CAF onto Local Financial Institutions (LFIs) for the purposes of funded activities:
Component 1 - Green finance credit line for LFIs
CAF will make available a credit line in conjunction with Local Financial Institutions (LFIs) and other local and national actors.
Component 2 - Technical Support and Awareness Building
CAF will execute and carry out the planned activities under this component in collaboration with local actors.

Approximately 30 people reached estimated per each project to be carried out over the 5-year project period

#### Baselines:
The energy and land sectors are responsible for majority of greenhouse gas emissions in many Latin American countries. **Policy Context:** The Panamanian Nationally Determined Contribution (INDC) to the UNFCCC highlights the importance of the energy and land use; land use change and forestry (LULUCF) sectors. The government has set a target of reducing GHG emissions by 54% in 2030 to contribute to the de-carbonization of the electric matrix; promoting renewable energies and supporting improvements in energy efficiency. ## Summary

The proposed Programme supports national level GHG savings activities in all the target countries based on their NDCs and targets. The Programme will tap into the growing international interest in scaling up investment in energy efficiency, renewables and land use projects. All the NDCs expressed the need for additional international financial support to scale-up these investments. The proposed Programme is designed to provide a flexible framework that allows for the private sector in dispersing investments to help these countries meet the ambitions they set forth in their NDCs.

The Programme will focus on three key areas: (i) energy efficiency, renewables and land use; (ii) scaling up large-scale, high-visibility projects. Globally it is estimated that only 1% of climate change project funding has actually reached these sectors. The proposed Programme aims to increase this share by providing financial support to populations in low-carbon and climate-resilient measures.

The proposed Programme thus aims to support the achievement of NDCs through a combination of financing, technical assistance and awareness building to boost local markets for climate change projects in key sectors, primarily for SMEs in Latin America. The participant countries are Panama, Ecuador, Peru and Colombia.


## Overview

The Programme is designed to accelerate the development and volume of local climate change projects in Latin American countries that can overcome key financial and knowledge barriers (Figure 13). It targets projects in the energy and land use sectors with strong mitigation benefits, supporting countries to reduce emissions from two sectors: (i) energy efficiency; and (ii) land use.

Using a combination of funding sources, including loans and grants, the Programme will conduct activities across two Programmes: (i) Financial Assistance (Financial); (ii) Technical Support and Awareness Building – to boost local markets for climate change projects.

## Barriers

Key barriers that currently inhibit investments in small-scale renewable energy, energy efficiency and land use sector-relevant climate mitigation projects include: (i) high and under-financed interest rates; (ii) unviable loan terms; (iii) lack of access to suitable finance for many SMEs; (iv) lack of visibility of LFs; and (v) limited knowledge of the benefits of sustainable practices. The proposed GCF programme will address these barriers by providing financial support for sustainable projects with climate change benefits in Latin America. - Review of the achievement of planned impacts and indicators (according to the Logframe)
- Assessment of the need for resources to reinforce the program

**MRV framework**

CAF will set up a monitoring framework at programme level that will apply to all participating LFs, TSPs and SMEs and will be based on the MRV actions and steps described below for clean energy and land use. The review will be undertaken by the UFs and SMEL/TSPs.

### Energy

- **Approach:** For energy efficiency and renewable energy projects, MRV and due diligence processes should be in proportion to the size of the project in order to avoid disproportionately increased costs. Differentiation is needed between small (small) and large (large) projects. Small projects are defined as a project with a total investment value of less than USD 1 million (small), and projects with a total investment value of more than USD 1 million (large). The MRV framework for small projects will be based on the fact that LFs are delivering the expected impacts; however, overly complex MRV requirements for small projects can create unnecessary costs. For large projects, the MRV framework should be proportionate to the size meaning that the projects lose some of their economic benefit for the LF and/or the client.
- **Methodology:** The methodology will follow the IFC’s approach to GHG Accounting for Energy Efficiency (IFC 2017). This includes the following: i) Scope: All energy efficiency measures in the project; ii) Renewable energy should be included in the documents. If/ approach to GHG Accounting for Energy Efficiency (IFC 2017).

### Land Use

- **Methodology:** Based on IPCC, the MRV system for all of the investments in land use will follow the same generic formula to calculate emission removals.
- **Approach:** The methodology will be based on carbon dioxide emissions removed from different activities in the land use sector. Each LF will apply the Ex-Ante Carbon-Balance Tool (EX-ACT) (FAO, 2017). This tool is a simple and user-friendly tool that allows LFs to estimate the amount of CO2 emissions or sinks of CO2 (or as GHG emissions per unit of land, expressed in equivalent tonnes of CO2 per hectare per year). The methodology will be based on the assumption that the project activities have the greatest economic benefit and potential for climate change mitigation. This GHG mitigation potential may also be used for economic analyses and for allocating additional project funds.

## C.1 Environmental and social risk assessment (max. 750 words, approximately 1.5 pages)

CAF has a strong working relationship with LFs from Latin America and works together with such institutions as well as project developers towards overcoming key barriers in financing sustainable investments. This experience has been very useful for CAF in its work with LFs on issues related to environmental and social governance (ESG) to strengthen the adoption of ESG principles in LFs. Such experience has also helped CAF to better understand the importance of having a robust Environmental, Social Management System (ESMS) and robust procedures and safeguards are in place for implementing climate change projects in the energy and land use sectors.

### Environmental and Social Planning at the Programmes Level

Together CAF and participating LFs will ensure that comprehensive and robust environmental and social screening and management systems are in place, and program activities and investments are closely monitored, evaluated and reported on. This will be achieved by ensuring that all projects under the Programmes are consistent with the ESMS of each institution and ensure that it is aligned with the ESMF of the Programme, including CAF’s Environmental and Social Management Framework (ESMF).

Participating LFs must have an environmental and social policy and, based on it, have implemented an Environmental and Social Risk Analysis and Management System for the categorization of its operations and for the evaluation of the environmental and social risks associated with each operation.

The ESMS will be reviewed by CAF to ensure that:

- Verify that the operations if finances are not prohibited by law;
- Verify that the operations are not included in the Exclusions List of CAF, and the program-specific exclusions list (if any) of the ESMF (Annex 1);
- Require compliance with national legislation in terms of obtaining permits and environmental licenses, use of natural resources, etc. (Annex 2);
- Categorize and evaluate the environmental and social risk of the operation’s finances;
- Verify that the operations are consistent with the ESMS of each institution and any other measures established to minimize the environmental and social risks of their operations.

Projects will be categorized by CAF based on its assessment of its operations, according to the frequency or established in the credit agreement, which will be fixed depending on the risk of its portfolio and the magnitude of the project.

CAF will conduct a screening on all participating LFs regarding the quality and robustness of their environmental and social management systems. This will include an evaluation of whether the ESMS is adequate to support the screening, approval, monitoring and evaluation of investments in the longlisted areas. When CAF identifies that there are weaknesses or gaps in these systems, it will provide technical assistance for such systems, and where possible support LFs to improve their ESMS to be able to participate within the Programme. This will include providing training on ESG issues and capacity building.

A gap assessment will be performed and when not fully aligned the LFs will require to meet the required adjustments and capacities for their participation in the Programme (within Activity 2.3.1). This is an added value of the programme which will contribute to strengthen the ESMS, contributing to the long-term sustainability of the projects.

Monitoring and evaluation of the environmental and social risks associated with each operation by high-risk projects will not be supported within the program, along with other investments described in the program excluding those of the category of A1. All operations categorized as B2 and B3 will be included in Category B projects and will not finance Category A projects. As such it is qualified as 1a.

Where necessary environmental and social management plans (ESMPs) will either be in place or developed at the final loan beneficiary level to manage identified risks and adverse impacts.

## I. Assessment of the Independent Technical Advisory Panel

### 1.1 Impact potential Scale: Medium/High

#### a) In many Latin American countries, the energy and land-use sectors are responsible for the majority of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. Emissions from the energy sector vary substantially across countries and regions. The transition to renewable energy sources and growing energy needs imply that the sector will continue contributing to increasing global GHG emissions unless significant efforts are made to reduce these emissions, particularly by the agriculture, forestry and other land use sectors.

#### b) Although investments in low-carbon technologies and land-use practices are generally recognized as key for climate change mitigation, the adoption of such technologies and practices remains limited in Latin America. Local financial institutions (LFIs) often do not have the technical capacity to identify projects that will contribute to reducing GHG emissions or mitigate climate change projects. LFIs tend to view the projects as risky and are therefore hesitant to develop suitable project pipelines.

#### c) Small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) and farmers are the primary investors for small-scale renewable energy (RE), energy efficiency (EE) and sustainable land use projects. They often lack access to financing, particularly from commercial banks. The proposed programme aims at identifying how projects would benefit them and/or how to identify the most appropriate projects. Even if suitable projects can be identified, they may not have sufficient financial resources or favorable financing terms from LFIs. According to the analysis conducted by the accredited entity (AE), the payback period of RE and EE investments ranges from four to ten years in the participating countries. The AE also found that the average payback period for EE investments is generally investment-specific, with a general investment payback period of two years for other investments in their businesses.

#### d) The unavailability of commercial bank financing with attractive pricing and longer tenure for loans.

#### e) The limited knowledge of investors and LFIs of low-carbon technologies and/or the potential benefits of such climate change projects.

# Existing Gender Inequality: Financial Inclusion of Women-owned SMEs in LAC

Financial inclusion constitutes an important factor for the empowerment and economic autonomy only for women. To ensure access to financial services, it is necessary to include them to broaden their opportunities from a production, personal and financial perspective. Nonetheless, financial inclusion remains a challenge for many women globally, where there is a funding gap of $287 billion for financing SMEs owned by women. It has been further estimated that 70% of women-owned SMEs in developing countries have inadequate or no access to financial services.

## Information Box: Key definitions

**Women-owned (IFC definition)** If an enterprise is:

- (A) ≥51% owned by woman/women, OR
- (B) ≥20% owned by woman/women; AND (i) has 1 woman as EO/COO/President/Vice President; AND (ii) has ≥30% of the board directors composed of women, where a board exists.

Such challenges are present in Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC), where it is estimated that around 39% of SMEs across all sectors are owned by women. The number is much lower in sectors such as agriculture (see below) and can be assumed to be mostly true for the services sector. In 2011-2012, only 22% of loans in LAC were provided to women-owned SMEs. Globally, there are significant differences between countries in terms of access to finance (estimated USD Figure 1). In LAC, SMEs have roughly the same distribution of women-owned formal enterprises across sectors as men-owned enterprises. However, this is not true for microenterprises, which play an important role for the green lending potential; as sustainable land use loans are only applicable under the category manufacturing (i.e., primary production and processing), while clean energy loans have potential demand across all three sectors.

### Barriers for Women-Owned SMEs to Access Credit

Beyond barriers for women to develop SMEs, additional barriers exist that limit women-owned SMEs' access to credit. These include the lack of affordable guarantee requirements, size of the credit and flexi credit products are oriented towards women, among others.

With social barriers it is often related to restrictions on mobility and time availability, especially since many women have a dual role in their households. Socio-cultural factors related to SME development are further relevant given the historical dominance of men in many commercial activities while women are often more associated with household labor.

Guarantee conditions further limit women's ability to access credit, as many of the requirements reflect socio-cultural discrimination, often related to the control or resources, land ownership.

If women do not have formal access to land tenure, it is often difficult for them to secure loans and other financial services.

Given the use of income as a main factor to assess credit conditions and amounts, women are often only able to access smaller loans with unfavorable conditions (e.g. high interest rates).20 While some banks in LAC have started to develop products that target women’s needs, they are fully developing such products at scale that target women's financial preferences and needs, while also addressing the identified barriers that they face to access credit. Many banks focus on providing microfinance lending for women; however, do not focus on lending small and medium loans to women.

### Possible Impacts and Risks related to Gender

As described above, there is a major financing gap for women-owned SMEs to access financial services in LAC. Thus, business as usual practices could impact women by continuing to maintain


---
**Page 106**
---

or even broaden the gap if women are not effectively targeted and empowered through supporting measures within the program.

The following social barriers will be taken into account to the extent possible when working with LfIs:

- Women face a greater workload in childcare and home responsibilities, which could affect the size and performance of a SMEs.
- The level of education and training of women entrepreneurs is lower than that of men.
- Women-owned SMEs are more likely to be small or micro-enterprises. They have less access to financial resources, as their companies are very small, and their nature does not meet the requirements requested by the banking institutions. Women entrepreneurs are normally financially illiterate and lack the necessary skills for business management.
- Women entrepreneurs have less availability of access to credit in traditional commercial banks since sometimes they cannot cover the requirements for a loan (for example: property collateral).
- Capitalizing is very difficult for women given the high interest rates and short credit terms, which does not allow them to have the financial support they need for growth.

Financial institutions often discriminate against women entrepreneurs. They tend to restrict their financial services such as savings or credit, or prefer to service property owners or members of households with high income levels and large companies. In this way, women are increasingly being pushed out completely out of the financial system.
- Women are often subject to discriminatory practices by financial institutions, even when these practices profit them in the same as that of men.
- Usually women receive little or no financial education.
- Women face negative stereotypes about their ability to do business.
- Women's SMEs are located mainly in the services sector. They have less growth access in financial terms and are often limited by lack of information, management training for their own company and scarce access to information technologies. That said, women still play a key role in the energy and land use sectors, and a major barrier for them is accessing credit.

The Programme is anticipated to have positive impacts by improving the access of women to finance for climate projects in the energy and land use sectors, building the capacities of women for SME operation, and improving gender-responsive M&E systems within LfIs to promote more effective targeting. The programme will also include a series of training sessions on credit operations on women. Additional gender-related training will be conducted to support women-owned SME development, and to support LfIs in strengthening their gender policy and design target-based interventions that are sensitive to the needs of women entrepreneurs with access to finance. The following section discusses key recommendations that have been integrated into the program to mitigate gender-related risks and promote positive impacts supporting the empowerment of women. These recommendations are further reflected in the Gender Action Plan.
