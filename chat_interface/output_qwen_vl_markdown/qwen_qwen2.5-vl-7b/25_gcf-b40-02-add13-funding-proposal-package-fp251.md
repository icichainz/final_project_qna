A.21. Executive summary (max. 700 words, approximately 1.5 pages)

Barbados faces significant water scarcity, with just 285 m³ of fresh water available per capita each year, relying mainly on underground streams through sinkholes to replenish its aquifers. The country is highly vulnerable to droughts and climate hazards, as rising temperatures may boost evapotranspiration, reducing soil moisture levels by up to 30%. This could lead to severe water shortages, with water extraction expected to exceed sustainable levels. Moreover, rising sea levels threaten to inundate the island's coastal aquifers, compromising the quality of the freshwater reserves.

While nearly all groundwater is used for rapidly developing tourism, increasing frequency in droughts, the island now experiences challenges on the water supply side.

Estimated annual water production in Barbados for 2030 as follows: potable groundwater production is 4.8 billion gallons per year (BGA), while non-potable groundwater production is 15 BGA; total groundwater production is 7.4 BGA (Cashman, 2020).

Agriculture, a key water user consuming over 66% of all fresh water production from SWA production, is expected to face significant competition for water among sectors like household (which consumes over 22% of the total), tourism and industrial uses. The island's limited freshwater resources are under increasing pressure as it seeks to ensure a resilient supply and protect its aquifers, aligning with the goals of the GCF-supported project.

In addition to grappling with water scarcity, the increased frequency of extreme weather events risks contaminating groundwater through surface runoff, which can lead to poor quality drinking water, sanitation, and environmental pollution. Barbados Water Authority (SWA) currently operates two municipal sewage treatment plants, which treat the wastewater of approximately 3% of the population (Cashman, 2020). The existing system is not designed for the island's current population size or future growth. Both plants are undersized compared to their design capacity and have suffered from failures due to a lack of maintenance.

Across the nation, the primary method of wastewater and sewage disposal is through septic tanks. Improper management of septic tanks poses significant environmental and groundwater contamination risks, as well as public health hazards (Cashman, 2020).

The existing SCSTP, commissioned in 2003, can process 9,000 m³/day, serving the southern island with primary wastewater treatment before marine disposal. The existing outfall for discharge is located next to the mangrove wetland and the sea, which has historically been a significant source of pollution affecting the mangrove wetland in the island². Historically, the plant's inefficiency and failures have significantly impacted the wetlands' water quality.

The current SCSTP effluent discharge is affected by the lack of connection to the sea and by pollution loads resulting from discharges of untreated effluents of the SCSTP plant.

### B.1 Climate context (max. 1000 words, approximately two pages)

Barbados is one of only 10 countries in the world most water scarce countries²⁰ with approximately 265 m³ of fresh water available per capita per year. Instead of surface water, Barbados relies on water filtering into underground streams through sinkholes, periodically replenishing the underlying aquifer. Based on the abstraction rate and recharge rate, it is estimated that the groundwater resources in Barbados could be more than 100% of sustainable aquifer yields, leading to water shortages²¹.

Water scarcity has been especially critical during the last 51st Nile Oscillation phase. At the onset of this 2020 wet season, there was a significant deficit in soil moisture and below-average seasonal rainfall that had a severe impact on aquifer recharge and resulted in seawater intrusion into coastal aquifers and reduced yields²².

According to the analysis conducted for the 3R's (Reduce, Reuse, and Recycle) for Climate Resilience Wastewater Systems in Barbados (3R-CWWS) Project, based on historical data from the KNMI Global Historical Climatology Network (GHCN) dataset and the KNMI Climate Explorer, there has been a trend of increasing rising occurrences of dry periods²³ during the last decades (1991 to 2020), while there is also an increase in extreme weather events. Notably, increases in daily minimum temperatures and extreme heat events were observed in that period.

To project climate change in Barbados until 2050, as part of the 3R-CWWS Project, precipitation forecasts with the KNMI Climate Explorer were used to drive a suite of hydrological models including ACCESS-ESM1.5, ACCESS-ESM1.5m, and others, were utilized with the WPS-4.5 scientific computing data from the KNMI Climate Explorer. The results showed that precipitation is forecasted to decrease by 119 mm annually, extending over a period of 30 years (2020–2050) compared to the historical average²⁴. According to the 3rd global warning level (GWL), it is likely that the annual average precipitation changes in the Caribbean will be in the range of –17% to +2%. Projectors agree to indicate a generally drier Caribbean and an increase in extreme weather events.

On the other hand, Barbados' climate projections until 2050 expect a significant rise in average annual temperatures, with a faster increase in minimum temperatures during the wet season²⁵. In this regard, the projected temperature changes for the 3rd global warming level (GWL) indicate that by the end of the third global warning level the Caribbean region will experience a 0.5°C–1.5°C warming compared to the 1971-2000 baseline period²⁶.

There is high confidence in the projections of an increase in warm days and warm nights over the region.

The agricultural sector is notably impacted by fluctuations in the duration and intensity of dry seasons due to the lack of water availability. This has led to a decrease in crop yields, increased vulnerability to post-harvest supply, poor soil quality, increases in food prices and decrease in production (FAO 2016). Extreme weather events have also caused significant damage to crops such as banana, sugarcane, and citrus, resulting in complete crop losses due to severe flooding, and many others were unable to harvest because of saturated fields. Additionally, farmers noted a rise in bacterial diseases affecting their crops (MAFFS 2022). The Government of Barbados has recognized the need for climate resilience strategies and has developed a comprehensive food security strategy. ## Vulnerability of Barbados to climate change

The DARA organization estimated losses between US $5 - $45 million in direct losses to the Barbados agriculture sector as a result of unmitigated climate change between 2020 and 2030 (MAFS 2022).

The Ministry of Agriculture is unable to meet the current demand for irrigation water, and there is likely a significant suppressed demand due to intermittent restrictions on the supply of water for irrigation purposes. Limited access to affordable irrigation water is a major challenge for the agriculture sector in Barbados, especially during periods of drought.

Barbados' water scarcity, compounded by the anticipated effects of climate change on freshwater sources prompts the country to focus on developing strategies for efficient water usage and implementing a circular economy approach to water management. The project will address these challenges through advanced treatment is crucial to enable safe and viable wastewater reuse, including irrigation and recharge of aquifers.

Further, the project will also address the issue of increasing salinity in coastal waters due to seawater intrusion and prioritizing ecosystem preservation. Untreated wastewater poses a threat to the fragile aquatic ecosystems of the island, which contain over 50 hard coral species and 600 species of fish.

The project is proposed as an expansion of the existing SCWTP (Scenic West Coast Water Treatment Plant) into the existing SCSTP at a futuristic New South Coast Water Reclamation and the Reuse Facility (SCWRF). The goals of the Project are multiple: augment existing water supplies, address seawater intrusion and sea level rise, drive economic development, and reduce pollution while reducing the impact of treated effluent on marine life and the environment.

## Sizing

The new treatment system was designed based on an average dry weather flow (ADWF) of 9,000 m³/day, which matches the current average daily capacity of the SCSTP, also at 9,000 m³/day. The sizing of the plant considers both existing flows as well as future flows. Existing flow rate data indicate an average daily flow rate of approximately 12,500 m³/day and a maximum daily flow rate of approximately 36,000 to 40,000 m³/day. These flow rates were, however, measured during the Covid-19 pandemic and do not account for flows from the new connections in the near future. Additionally, the BWC has indicated plans for expansion of the sewerage network (starting with an expansion of 300 connections in the near future).

The influent flow (20,000 households currently connected to SCSTP) will be received by the headworks and conveyed via a gravity channel to the primary clarifiers. The effluent from the primary clarifiers will be pumped to the channel upstream of the CAS (Conventional Activated Sludge systems two operating in parallel), while the flow from the headworks and the Return Activated Sludge (RAS) will be combined before entering the CAS. The RAS will be returned to the secondary clarifier, where it will be mixed with the wastewater.

The water from the CAS will flow by gravity to the secondary clarifiers. The secondary clarifiers will remove a large portion of the solids formed during the biological treatment. Through a dedicated pump station, the secondary clarifier underflow will be recirculated to the CAS as RAS. The waste activated sludge will be directed to a dissolved air flotation (DAF) thickener and, after thickening to the aerobic digester. The sludge from the aerobic digester will be pumped to the drying beds and, after drying, it will be collected for off-site disposal.


---
**Page 28**
---

The effluent from the secondary clarifier will be conveyed by gravity to tertiary disk filters or diverted to the marine outfall when the flow exceeds 18,000 m3/day. The tertiary filters will remove residual solids from the secondary clarifier.

The tertiary disk filters flow by gravity to the UV system. The UV system will provide disinfection by inactivating pathogens. Downstream of the UV system, the disinfected water will be collected in an underground basin that will function as a wet well for pumps that will feed the advanced water treatment (AWT) facility. The AWT facility will remove dissolved solids and other contaminants from the tertiary effluent when flows to the treatment plant are low.

A side stream will be treated through reverse osmosis (RO) to meet the 450 mg/L TDS limit for the marine outfall. The RO feed water will first be pumped through a strainer and then through the microfiltration (MF) or ultrafiltration (UF) skids. This treatment will remove fine particles before RO. After MF/UF treatment, the flow will be collected in an MF/UF break tank that will provide a point for hydraulic control and simplify chemical dosing. The RO feed water will then be treated with sodium hypochlorite to provide residual disinfection.

The cartridges protect the RO from fine particulate and biological growth that may have developed in the pipes feeding the RO skids. The RO permeate will be collected in an RO break tank, which will provide a point for hydraulic control and simplify chemical dosing. The RO permeate will then be treated with sodium hypochlorite to provide residual disinfection (an in-line static mixer should be provided). A small dose of chloramine is also recommended to limit biological growth on the RO membranes.

The RO reject water will be conveyed through a 10-inch diameter pipe to the marine outfall. The chemical stripping of ammonia from the RO-treated water through an air stripper will provide residual disinfection. Alternatively, the RO reject water can be treated with sodium hypochlorite and then conveyed to the marine outfall.

The rejected brine from the RO will be conveyed to the marine outlet. The chemical stripping of ammonia from the RO-treated water through a caustic bed for remineralization should be sufficient. In the end, RO permeate will be collected in an RO break tank that will provide a point for hydraulic control and simplify chemical dosing.

The blended water will be collected in a storage tank that will function as a wet well for pumps to feed the irrigation and drainage pipeline.

The marine outfall associated with the SCSTP® will discharge into the sea:

- Effluent from the secondary clarifier when the flow exceeds 18,000 m3/day.
- Excess disinfected water (with tertiary treatment) if a storage tank for this water is not foreseen in the project.

### Barbados Economic Context and Overview
Barbados has faced economic challenges since the 2008 financial crisis with the COVID-19 pandemic exacerbating existing issues. Inflation has averaged approximately 4% annually, with forecasts suggesting a stable inflation rate of around 3%. The economy is highly dependent on tourism, which is subject to fluctuations, natural disasters, and the pandemic, contributing to a rise in the public debt-to-GDP ratio, reaching 108.9% by September 2019.

The GDP saw a reduction of 17.3% by Q1 2021, and the unemployment rate rose to 10.8% by September 2019. The poverty rate increased from 15.1% in 2010 to 17.2% in 2016, with further rises due to the pandemic.

Looking ahead, Barbados projects a 4.5% growth in real GDP and a decline in inflation to 5% by the end of 2023. However, risks such as a slower-than-anticipated recovery in tourism, global geopolitical uncertainties, and adverse weather conditions could impact the economy adversely. ### Barriers to Investment

Given this economic backdrop, the investment climate in Barbados, particularly for large-scale infrastructure projects like the South Coast Sewage Project, faces several barriers:

- **High National Debt:** The substantial debt-to-GDP ratio limits the government's capacity to finance large-scale investments.
- **Inflation and Currency Risks:** Inflationary pressures and potential currency fluctuations increase the cost of borrowing.
- **Vulnerability to External Shocks:** Dependence on tourism and susceptibility to natural disasters create an unpredictable economic environment.
- **Limited Access to Capital:** Limited access to capital under current conditions limit access to capital, especially for environmentally-focused projects that require substantial upfront investment.

The concessionality of GCF financial instruments, in this context, is vital as it offers a more favorable and less risky financing option. The World Bank (WB) and the International Monetary Fund (IMF) are crucial to make the SCWPViable, considering Barbados' high indebtedness and economic vulnerabilities.

Extending the concessionality for the solar plant subcomponent aligns with Barbados’ efforts to increase renewable energy capacity while also addressing its debt sustainability goals. This not only supports the country's sustainability goals but also helps reduce reliance on fossil fuels, enhance climate resilience, and address economic challenges effectively.

### Alternatives Explored

In evaluating potential funding for the project, the Government of Barbados (GoB) and the BWA conducted a thorough analysis of potential financing options:

- **Bilateral or Official Sector Loan Direct to BWIA:** One of the first options considered was securing a bilateral or official sector loan directly to the BWIA, bypassing the GoB balance sheet. Moreover, the viability of this option hinged on the ability of BWIA to secure financing that did not compromise its financial constraints.
- **Private Sector Securitized Concession (Public-Private Partnership - PPP):** Another alternative was a private sector financed concession, leveraging a Public-Private Partnership (PPP) model. #### Gender assessment and action plan (max. 500 words, approximately 1 page)

## Summary of the Secretary’s assessment

### Project background

Barbados is one of the world's most water-source countries, with water abstraction levels exceeding 100 per cent of sustainable aquifer yields during below-average rainfall periods. The country faces severe climate challenges, including significant water scarcity along with environmental degradation and biodiversity loss. In addition to these challenges, storm surges in the South Coast area is only screened and discharged into the ocean. In 2018, breakdowns and blockages at the aging South Coast Sewage Treatment Plant resulted in raw sewage flooding the streets, causing severe health risks for residents and tourists alike. The country’s water consumer, is heavily impacted by climate change, affecting crop production and food security. Additionally, high non-revenue water losses of 46 per cent, further reduce water availability.

The goal of the Climate Resilience (Cost Sharing Reclaimed Water Project) (CRWRP) is to increase overall resilience in Barbados to climate change with a focus on improving water security, food sovereignty and environmental conditions via the holistic approach of upgrading the South Coast Sewage Treatment Plant.


### The key strategic value of the project

The core objective of this project is to improve water resilience. This will be done by increasing the availability of high-quality reclaimed water with energy-efficient technologies efficiently delivering reclaimed water for agricultural irrigation and aquifer recharge; enhancing the ecological function of the upgraded South Coast Sewage Treatment Plant (SCSTP); supporting biodiversity and ecosystem services; strengthening the institutional capacity of the Water Authority of Barbados (WAB) and the Water Resources Authority (WRA), and promoting gender and social inclusivity for water resource management.


### The key strategic value of the project

The key strategic value of the project is that it will introduce a novel financing approach through development of a blended finance facility with contributions from the International Development Bank (IDB) and the European Investment Bank (EIB) at USD 150 million each. This will create more fiscal space for the Government of Barbados without exacerbating its fiscal burden. GCF will provide USD 70 million in grant funding to support the project, with a co-financing requirement for BWIA to deliver the required measurement, reporting and verification duties under IDB, specific technical assistance from WRA and other related costs associated with project investments.

# 4.4 Sexual and Gender Based Violence

Studies conducted by the Bureau of Gender Affairs/Caribbean Development Research Services in 2006 reveal that there is a 27% prevalence of violence among adult women in the region. Patriarchal gender norms, which are deeply rooted in the socialization process, reinforce traditional expectations within the confines of the private, domestic sphere. These norms reinforce male dominance over the bodies of women and girls, claiming entitlement to sexual gratification and providing a rationale for acts of violence against women.

The study also found that 40% of women in three Caribbean countries—Barbados, Jamaica, and Trinidad and Tobago—understood that violent acts are predicated on gender norms. The study further revealed that the prevalence of physical violence was higher among female victims than male victims. Notably, the study found that violence levels decreased with the age of victims, falling from 72.4% among 15–19-year-olds to 63.8% among 27–30-year-olds. ## 4.5 Gender Equality and Climate Change

Barbados, like most Caribbean countries, has a high risk of vulnerability. Most of the population is concentrated in an urban corridor that lies within two km of the shore and below 25 meters in elevation. The most vulnerable are low-income families who have fewer choices in their living situations and limited access to resources during danger times. Vulnerable groups impacted by climate and disaster risks in the country include working-class men and women, youth, single mothers, adolescent mother, the elderly and persons with disabilities.

### Gender Responsive Disaster Preparedness and Recovery in the Caribbean

The Global Facility for Disaster Reduction and Recovery (GFDRR) of the World Bank, in collaboration with the Caribbean Resilience Facility (CRF), conducted a comprehensive desk review on Gender Responsive Disaster Preparedness and Recovery in the Caribbean. The primary findings of this review are as follows:

- Women in the CRF countries encounter diverse gaps in accessing health services, social protection, vocational and technical education, productive resources, economic opportunities, and avenues for voice and agency. These disparities are intricately linked to their household responsibilities.
  
- While indicators for educational attainment, measured in terms of the number of years of education, are nearly identical for men and women in CRF countries, a notable gender gap persists in access to vocational and technical education, further limiting women's opportunities.

# Monitoring and Evaluation

The success of the Gender Action Plan within the Barbados South Coast Water Reclamation Project hinges on a robust Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) Framework that systematically tracks progress, measures impacts, and identifies areas for improvement. The processes are designed to be dynamic, responsive, and participatory, ensuring that gender inclusivity remains a core focus throughout the project's lifespan.

Regularly assesses the Gender Action Plan’s progress through gender-sensitive indicators, stakeholder feedback, and periodic reviews. Adjust based on lessons learned and emerging gender and diversity considerations, ensuring continuous improvement.
