## Indonesia is in urgent need of rapid actions to address gradually deteriorating climate change impacts and their consequences. The climate-vulnerable archipelagic state submitted its nationally determined contribution (NDC) under the Paris Agreement in September 2015, committing itself to a low-carbon development pathway with strong willingness for transition towards a low-carbon and climate-resilient pathway. The NDC of Indonesia is characterized by energy efficiency as one of its key mitigation actions. While Indonesia utilizes 65-65% of the total greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from the power sector, it has been unable to achieve significant progress in this area due to the fact that the ensuing energy savings have been long hampered by a range of barriers, such as access to finance, regulatory and policy frameworks, demand-side barriers, and lack of technical assistance. The situation has worsened since the Coronavirus Disease (COVID-19) outbreak and spread, the state budget and relevant stimulus measures for economic recovery, and the global financial crisis. In this context, Indonesia is currently in a period of plenty. Surrounded by further setbacks from the pandemic and related budget deficits, an innovative financing mechanism is needed to address these barriers and support the implementation of the Programme entitled "Supporting Innovative Mechanisms for Industrial Energy Efficiency Financing in Indonesia" (hereinafter referred to as the Programme). The Programme aims at providing technical assistance to support Indonesia and its neighbouring ASEAN countries to re-focus on climate actions amid the post-pandemic recovery era, strengthening the foundation for the NDC achievement and the national alignment with the Paris Agreement.

The Programme, therefore, shall support Indonesia and other ASEAN countries to be ready to drive a low-carbon development pathway with enhanced energy efficiency and conservation performance, addressing the three barriers of financial, regulatory, and demand-side. The Programme will also aim at breaking down a vicious circle of implementation failures of similar precedents. This initiative pays special attention to the three components of the Programme as described below.

### Industrial Energy Efficiency in Indonesia – Potentials and Challenges

In emerging economies, the energy use from industries is increasing exponentially, directly linked to GDP. Between 2000 and 2015, Indonesia's GDP has doubled and its demand for electricity increased by 150%. According to the Ministry of Industry Republic of Indonesia and UNDP (2018), the industrial sector accounts for approximately 77%, which is around 30% of the total energy consumption in Indonesia. These figures highlight the importance of improving energy efficiency within this sector.

The largest contributor to Indonesia's GHG emissions from industrial activities that include fossil fuel combustion, industrial processes and products used, and waste treatment activities rank second after land use in Indonesia. Amongst manufacturing industries, cement production is responsible for approximately 15% of total GHG emissions; chemicals, pulp and paper, textile, iron and steel, and food and beverage – contributed approximately 55.9 percent of the total industrial sector's GHG emissions.

The inefficient energy use in the industrial sector has led to high energy waste, which is estimated at around 30% of the total energy consumption. Improving energy efficiency within this sector could provide a substantial benefit to the GHG emissions reduction as well as the energy intensity reduction of Indonesia. In particular, potential energy savings would be significant in energy-intensive industries such as cement and chemicals.

## Challenges in the Industrial Energy Efficiency Market in Indonesia

### Financial Barriers
- Local financial institutions are unfamiliar with energy efficiency financing mechanisms.
- Banks are highly risk-averse in energy efficiency financing, thereby imposing high interest rates and collateral and high equity injuncts which local SMEs are remotely capable of obtaining.
- No credit mitigation technique including the de-risking mechanism (such as guarantee or insurance) for energy efficiency in the local market.

### Regulatory Barriers
- Existing fiscal or non-fiscal incentives from the government to promote the energy efficiency area have not been disseminated to industries or managers, despite the high GHG emissions.
- Article 20 of Government Regulation No.70/2009 (Energy Conservation) states that financial institutions are required to provide a low-interest facility for energy saver equipment to low-interest rates for the needs of investment in energy efficiency projects and to promote the development of the market.

### Demand-side Barriers
- Low demand for high-energy efficiency facilities due to low energy tariffs.
- Many industries lack the necessary knowledge, skills, capacity and resources in carrying out its cost-benefit analysis, which potentially results in a low prioritization of investing in energy efficient projects.
- Industrial managers are not well-trained or familiar with energy efficiency reporting and implementation.

B.S. Justification for GCF funding request (max. 1000 words, approximately 2 pages)

Advanced Financing Schemes to Overcome Low Demand and Financial Barriers. Local FiS shared that it is not possible to use a private sector financing mechanism for increasing borrowers’ access to participation in energy efficiency finance or other sectors in general. Securing financing through global banking or capital markets on competitive terms, aside from the loan funding from the GCF, is accessible without substantial barriers provided by the local financial market. However, this is not possible with the current structure of FiS and the lack of prospective FiSs and consideration of the Indonesian Financial Services Authority (OJK) that is the perceived associated risk of providing credit guarantees for low-income borrowers in Indonesia. The OJK has historically been reluctant to provide credit guarantees for low-income borrowers.

Though a number of prospective FiS have been interested in providing loans and other financial services related to the energy efficiency market, the lack of historically successful energy efficiency projects reveals a significant barrier to overcome. This is due to the fact that there are no established models or examples of financing such projects. To address this barrier, the Programme was curated to create a credit guarantee component for low-income borrowers in Indonesia. The GCF Credit Guarantee Cover Agreement will provide access to the evolving energy efficiency market, rather than directly providing loan capital. With GCF providing cover guarantees, it is expected that FiSs will be able to offer competitive terms and conditions to borrowers.

The Programme will also allow FiSs to be able to accept any credit risk in lieu of final beneficiaries, in particular those of the least developed SMEs so that those beneficiaries can access energy efficiency loans and begin to expand the market.

The GCF Credit Guarantees Cover Agreement will also diversify the existing energy efficiency financing model. The current model is based on a public sector-led approach with the government as the main actor in the energy efficiency financing model, with financial institutions, primarily banks, issuing loans to final beneficiaries for projects (DeSoto et al., 2010; Kate et al., 2011; MacKean & Purcell, 2014; Würtzinger, 2015). The government has been the main actor in financing energy efficiency projects. Indonesia’s GCF portfolio for 4% of the total investment into climate finance of USD 13 billion between 2015 and 2018, reflecting the deep need and significant opportunity to pursue an increase in energy efficiency investment in Indonesia.

Based on the information above, KDB developed the concessional GCF Credit Guarantee Cover portion of the Programme as its primary financial component. As detailed in B.3 and B.4, the Programme does not simply provide low-interest funding to final beneficiaries but provides credit risk guarantees for FiSs for their own loans to borrowers with lower interest rates than the market loan rate, which leads to increased energy efficiency financing and addressing of the market in Indonesia.

- Mainstream gender considerations in the design of energy efficiency finance.
- Apply the women-led SME framework under the financing structure, i.e., priority given to relevant women-led SMEs in terms of access to energy efficiency financial products and services.

Though some LFs have been interested in providing loans to SMEs, they are reluctant to lend at levels below 10% of the loan amount. Energy efficiency projects have kept them out of the acceptable risk level for LFs. To address this barrier, the Programme has proposed a guarantee mechanism (Guarantee) that will help mitigate the risk gap that they would face as they invest in the evolving energy efficiency market. The Guarantee is designed to provide an additional layer of security and efficient use of GCF concessionality based on the realistic needs of the target market. In turn, this will lead to those SMEs being more willing to invest in energy efficiency projects demonstrating true cost-benefit data that will allow for future expansion of the market.

The Guarantee is designed so that benefits reach both beneficiaries and that use of GCF funding is optimized. By using a guarantee, the Programme is crowding in the local market actors to use their own funds. It also avoids currency mismatch, which is a key issue prevalent with hard currency loans.

# NATIONAL GENDER CONTEXT

## Laws and Policies on Gender Equality

The Constitution of the Republic of Indonesia, adopted Under Decree 1945, upholds a commitment to the principles enshrined in the Universal Declaration. Paragraph 1 of Article 27 stipulates that "every citizen enjoys equal status before the law and government, and is obliged to uphold this status without exception." This principle applies equally to men and women. Every citizen shall have the right to employment and to conditions of life commensurate with human dignity.

The democratic reforms of 1999 caused the Government to pay increased attention to issues of human rights and gender equality. The first Indonesian law on gender equality was, consequently, issued in 2000 which recognizes that "every person shall have the right to be free from discrimination." This law is a landmark in Indonesia's history as it is the first law to prohibit such discriminatory treatment." While the Constitution provides a sound foundation for the establishment of equal rights between men and women, much remains to be done in the passing of laws and regulations that will ensure the implementation of these principles. The Government has used Indonesia's equal policy instruments in economic system setting, scores Indonesia at 62 out of 100 on average in terms of gender equality.

The Indonesian legal system is a complex confluence of three distinct systems of civil, customary, and religious laws. Prior to the arrival of Dutch traders and colonists in the late 16th and early 17th centuries, the archipelago was divided into hundreds of independent kingdoms and principalities with their own civil (customary) law as well as laws drawn from the teachings of various religions and beliefs. Dutch presence in Indonesia began in 1602 when the Dutch East India Company established itself here leaving a legacy of a Roman-Dutch based system of colonial law. The Indonesian legal system is, as a consequence, a complex confluence of three distinct systems of civil, customary, religious laws and based on religion, primarily that of the Islamic faith. Statutory legislation, government directives, instructions, and official compulsions of Islamic law together add up to a pool of laws that interrelate with each other.

Conflicts often ensue between adat laws and the Constitution. The adat laws are remarkably diverse and their interpretation varies from region to region. In some cases, different adat laws have been established in different localities. Consequently, the influence of "adat" laws on women also varies by locality.

The nation's ambition towards a more decentralized system of governance has unironically made way for a number of local governments to advance their aspiration of instituting public policies based on their own interpretation of adat law. In Aceh, for example, there is a general appreciation that its practice will engage upon the rights of women. In Aceh, for example, there is concern over whether the practice of Shar'ī will impose upon women a dress code of what is perceived as being modest and appropriate.

Indonesia has ratified the major international conventions that uphold principles of gender equality and the empowerment of women. These instruments include the United Nations Convention on the Political Rights of Women (CPRW) adopted by General Assembly Resolution 34/180, the International Covenant of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) ratified by Law number 7/1984, and the Optional Protocol to CEDAW adopted in 2005. Indonesia has also ratified the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) ratified by Law number 7/1984, and the Optional Protocol to CEDAW adopted in 2005. Indonesia has also ratified the International Labor Organization's (ILO) Convention number 100 on Equal Remuneration for Men and Women Workers in Work of Equal Value, as well as the ILO's Convention No. 189 and other ILO Core Conventions. An additional form of discrimination is the inherent bias towards married women with regard to credit access. Article 108 of the Civil Code impedes married women from entering into contracts on their own behalf. It also prohibits married women from entering into any contract for the purpose of hiring out or leasing contracts. There is also an inherent bias against married women earning income from individual business activities, as they are not allowed to use their earnings for personal purposes and require them to use their husband’s name. Married women thus find it difficult to engage in formal financial activities. This means that married women have no access to credit facilities or savings accounts. Single women, when they are married, will need to ask permission from their husband for any important financial decision. It is common in Indonesia for women to not have any access to a financial account if they do not work.
