### A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

#### Description of the project/programme
The proposed GCF regional programme for geothermal energy development in the Eastern Caribbean region is a partnership between the Green Climate Fund (GCF) and the Caribbean Development Bank (CDB). The CDB will be the implementing entity, with GCF providing financial resources. This programme aims to address the challenges of developing geothermal energy projects in the Eastern Caribbean Small Island Developing States (SIDS), which are located in the Western Caribbean region.

#### Project/programme objectives
The project/programme has two components:
1. **Component 1: GeoSmart Initiative**
   - A partnership initiative led by CDB, aimed at supporting geothermal energy development.
2. **Component 2: Regulatory Framework, Institutional Strengthening and Capacity Building**
   - Technical assistance for strengthening regulatory frameworks, institutional capacity building, and public-private partnerships.

#### Expected impacts
The programme is expected to have the following main impacts in the SIDS:
1. GHG Emissions Reductions of 313,421 TCO2e/year and 9,402,621 TCO2e over the lifetime of the programme.
2. Reduction of 722 thousand barrels of oil imported for electricity generation.
3. USD 50 million reduction in spending on Oil imports (at a fuel price of US$70 per barrel).
4. Reduction of average electricity generation cost and, if generation cost reductions are passed on to customers, this should lead to an average decrease in tariffs from US$2.35/kWh in 2015 (at a fuel price of US$100 per barrel).

### B.1. Description of Financial Elements of the Project / Programme

GE development is hindered by:
- (i) high capital costs;
- (ii) lack of access to credit at appropriate terms (e.g., affordable rates);
- (iii) limited availability of private sector financing for exploration and new public debt;
- (iv) lack of economies of scale;
- (v) high resource risk in early exploration phases for GE and;
- (vi) insufficient specialized expertise and capacity within the public sector to engage effectively with private sector counterparts, project financing, financial modeling and analyzing and managing and coordinating the implementation of GE projects.

These barriers will be addressed by the Programme through:
- (i) contingent grants for drilling exploration which will help reducing capital costs and unlocking investments in the subsequent stages of production drilling, field development and plant construction;
- (ii) concessional loans which will help to reduce funding costs lowering overall capital cost and increasing the likelihood of project viability;
- (iii) equity structures which will help limit new public debt and bring in the private sector; and
- (iv) technical assistance to improve the regulatory framework and capacity building will provide the necessary base for successful implementation of GE projects.

# Detailed Project / Programme Description

## C1. Strategic Context

The independent ECC (ECCM, GRE, SKN, SL and SVG) participating in the Sustainable Energy Facility for the Eastern Caribbean (the “Programme”), are small island developing states with small and isolated electricity markets. The fact that these countries have not yet developed their RE endowments makes them dependent on costly imported fossil fuels for electricity generation.

Electricity tariffs in the ECC are indexed to fuel prices, or include a fuel surcharge with a direct pass through to end consumers. Hence, customers often see high electricity tariffs and high volatility in their monthly bills. In 2013, the average electricity tariff was US$0.45/kWh (USD 0.68/kWh) for ECC countries compared to an average of USD $0.33/kWh. By comparison, in the State of Florida in the United States (US), the average tariff was US$0.11/kWh.

On average, in 2014 the fuel cost represented 53% of the total cost to end users.

## Fiscal Implications of liquid fossil fuel dependence

Even since the first oil shock of 1973, oil dependency has been a major concern for ECC countries. Oil dependency is exacerbated by low per capita income growth and economic growth, and a high public sector energy bill draws public resources that could be used to provide more social services. Governments in the region face fiscal constraints as they are unable to increase taxes on fuel products due to their role as a key export earner. The Gross Domestic Product (GDP) excise tax for all ECC countries is limited borrowing capacity as implied by the Debt-to-GDP ratio, averaging 70% of GDP across the ECC. Limited borrowing capacity in SE technologies thus perpetuating dependency on imported fossil fuels and its lighting effect on fiscal space.

## Lessons learned from international experience

Among RE solutions, GE development in particular necessitates significant upfront support and investment. The lessons learned from the successful implementation of RE projects in El Salvador, Costa Rica, Nicaragua, Kenya, US, Iceland and Guadeloupe were analyzed and lessons were extracted as to part of the design of the programme. The main factors are: (i) the existence of high quality GE resources; (ii) availability of grant funding for project development; (iii) the ability to access low-interest financing from international sources to develop GE; (iv) an enabling legal and regulatory frameworks that sets incentives to develop RE technologies and provides a conducive environment for private sector investment in RE; and (v) the development of early geothermal projects by government-owned agencies with support of international donors.

#### Laws and regulations governing the electricity sector

The ESA—which was passed in 1973 and amended in 1974, 1976, 1985, and 1997—governs electricity supply in SVG. The ESA provides a framework for the generation of electricity and gives it responsibility for issuing licenses to other parties that wish to generate, distribute, or transmit electricity in the country. This is an important provision because it allows the Government to regulate the industry and ensure that all parties involved are operating within the law.

The ESA also establishes a mechanism by which the Electricity Supply Commission (ESCC) can establish a tariff adjustment at any time. If the parties disagree on a suggested tariff, they jointly appoint an arbitrator to determine the tariff. The ESCC is responsible for VNL/EC's services (which are no longer the tariffs used), but does not create a formula for adjusting tariffs over time.

The legal framework governing the exploration and exploitation of geothermal resources is contained in the Geothermal Act which was passed in 2013.

# RATIONALE FOR GCF INVOLVEMENT

## D1. Value Added for GCF Involvement

ECC face high-energy prices due to high and volatile cost of liquid fossil fuels and low economic growth and macroeconomic imbalances. Public sector institutions are weak and have very limited fiscal space to take on GE project development. Due to the spot market for oil, the local economy is vulnerable to high energy prices and high early exploration resources, grant and concessional finance is necessary in order to reduce project risk and provide an incentive for public and private sector to engage in GE project development.

The programme will support GE development in the SECC with projects at different levels of development, funding from the GCF. The GCF contribution will be crucial as it provides additional resources. Even though some resources could already be mobilized the estimated total funding needs are above USD 500 million (see Table 7) and as such the GCF contribution is crucial.

GCF will provide funding for drilling exploration and production drilling in SL and SKN where no other funding sources with the required risk profile (i.e. contingent grants) have been identified yet. Without GCF concessional grants for SL and SKN, there would be no way to finance these projects. The GCF contribution is also crucial as it will provide GCF funding (senior loans) in GRE, SVG and DOM will be used for field development/construction work. Without GCF concessional funding, the project would not be able to proceed with the construction of the power plant and the installation of electricity tariffs. Moreover, by securing funding for both, early stages of development and the later stages of plant commissioning, the programme provides a seamless transition from one stage to another.

The GCF contribution therefore is essential to transform the energy matrix in the SECC towards a sustainable, low carbon economy with access to affordable energy services.

Furthermore, the execution of the program by the CDB will add the value of exposing the regional Bank to a process that GCF has in place, providing an "on the ground" strengthening for future direct access to GCF as a potential investor.

## D2. Exit Strategy

The programme has a duration of 8 years and will be supporting the whole project development cycle from drilling exploration, to production drilling, electricity production and construction of transmission lines. It is expected that all participating countries will have their own power plants in operation by the end of the 8-year period (i.e. commissioning of lines) once the programme ends. The investments, operational and maintenance costs will be funded through 25 years PPA with private sector for the construction phase of the project.

In addition, loans will only be provided to already established PPPs with all contractual agreements in place, including financial engagements of the private sector and public sector for different stages of the project.

## CONTRIBUTION TO THE CREATION OF AN ENABLING ENVIRONMENT

The programme introduces a new financial instrument for early investment drilling – contingent grants – which has not been used in the region before and will have the effect of removing the barriers for private sector investment in the early stage of GE development. Once the first stage of exploration drilling is overcome, private sector investments are more likely to be secured.

Sustained participation of public and private sectors will be assured by implementing the programme through PPP structures with a strong enabling EEC. The programme will only provide loans to already established PPPs with all contractual agreements including financial engagement of the private sector and public sector in place.

CDB may decide on a case-by-case basis how the contingent grants will be used, either as a Grant Convertible to Loan (GCL) or as a Loan Convertible to Grant (LCGR).

- In the case of a GCL fund, the PPP will receive a grant and in the case that the exploration is unsuccessful, the GCL remains as a grant. In case the exploration is successful, the grant will be converted into a loan and the loan terms will apply for GCLs.
- In the case of the LCGR, a loan is provided from CDB to the PPP using a pre-approved EIB JCA and GCF (concessional) funding. If the exploration drilling is unsuccessful then the PPP will repay the loan. If successful, the OTE or GCF resources remain untouched and can be used for other projects in the region. This case is recommended when the uncertainty of the geothermal source is very high.

In the case of the LCGR, a loan is provided from CDB to the PPP using a pre-approved EIB JCA and GCF (concessional) funding. If the exploration drilling is unsuccessful then the PPP will repay the loan. In case the exploration is successful, the OTE or GCF resources remain untouched and can be used for other projects in the region. This case is recommended when the uncertainty of the geothermal source is less than in the previous case.

### Environmental Assessment

The environmental exploration phase of each project will include possible negative environmental impacts and risks, though the more significant impacts are expected during construction. Each of the projects will involve driving and testing of new production wells, reection wells, construction of the power plants, building roads and other infrastructure to support the projects, as well as the installation of various pipelines and facilities.

Main construction impacts expected are: (i) potential contamination of soil and ground water by drilling mud and solid cuttings; (ii) land use changes due to the construction of new wells, roads, and other infrastructure on existing land; (iii) cutting or the resection of the geothermal field (essentially a mixture of hot water and steam at temperatures up to 200°C); (iv) potential contamination of soil and ground water by drilling mud and solid cuttings; (v) land use changes due to the construction of new wells, roads, and other infrastructure on existing land; (vi) increased traffic and potential traffic accidents in the vicinity of the project site; (vii) noise and dust emissions; and (viii) soil erosion and loss of vegetation.

Most of these environmental impacts and risks can be adequately mitigated through the implementation of appropriate environmental health and safety management plans and standard operating procedures (SOPs). Once in operation, main impacts and risks are: (i) an increased level of micro-seismicity in the region; (ii) land subsidence; (iii) surface and underground water contamination due to leakage from pipelines and other infrastructure; (iv) potential hydrogen sulfide; (v) health and occupational accidents; and (vi) increased exposure of community and workers to explosions, well blowouts and pipeline failures.

As at now, no Environmental and Social Impact Assessments (ESIA) have been done for DOM, Nesom (exploration phase), and DSV (exploration phase); the latter is financed by ILO. Further ESIsA will be financed by the programme and/or ILO-DBF as required.
