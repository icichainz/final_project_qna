A.2. Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

Kazakhstan is one of the largest emitters of greenhouse gases ("GHGs") in Central Asia, with ca. 72% of its electricity generated from coal-fired power plants. The country has a significant potential for renewable energy sources and aims to diversify its energy mix. At the same time, Kazakhstan possesses significant resources of renewable energy, such as wind, solar and hydro. Access to a reliable source of energy is critical in order to achieve sustainable development goals and reduce poverty in rural areas of Kazakhstan.

Consequently, the diversification of energy sources and decarbonisation of the economy are priorities of the Government of Kazakhstan. To achieve the renewable energy targets, the Government of Kazakhstan adopted renewable energy regulation that includes feed-in-tariff (FIT) for RES and a Renewable Energy Investment Programme (REIP).

The ERBP proposes to the GCF to implement an investment framework programme with a 5 year duration leading to the total investments of USD 550 million from the ERBP, private sector investors, other financial institutions along with the proposed investment. The project will be implemented in two phases: Phase I (2016-2018) and Phase II (2019-2020). It includes the construction, connection to the grid, commissioning and launch of the RES projects (solar, wind, small hydropower and biogas) as well as the development of a renewable energy investment framework programme. The project will enhance the grid to enhance the integration of RES into the electricity grid. Under the investment Programme, financing of small hydropower projects will not constitute more than 20% of the total Programme costs and will be limited to only financing of small hydropower projects with capacity up to 35 MW. Biogas will constitute a very small portion of the Programme and is unlikely to exceed 20 MWh on a cumulative basis.

The total investment amount of USD 550 million is estimated based on the projected pipeline of up to 330 MW RES projects. The total project cost contribution from the GCF is USD 110 million - for concessional loans (USD 106 million) and technical assistance (USD 4 million).

The main objectives of the Programme are the following:

- to facilitate competitive entry of low-carbon investors into the market currently dominated by conventional fossil fuel power plants, which have been slow to respond to market barriers;
- to support the construction of an estimated 350 MW of new RES capacity (ca. 18% of the Government's RES target for 2030);
- to support the development of a renewable energy investment framework programme in Kazakhstan, in conjunction with the power network upgrades to facilitate this RES uptake;
- to support the transition from a carbon market of Kazakhstan, including the development of the auction system which is expected to lead to costs reduction ensuring long-term sustainability of RES projects; and
- to promote gender equality within the RE sector through equal opportunities policies and practices within their operations.

Based on the experience with the current portfolio, the expected direct CO2 emissions reductions by the new RES generation are ca. 0.4 million tons CO2-equivalent per year once all sub-projects are operational (with the expected project lifetime of 20 years).

The GCF-EBRD Kazakhstan Renewable Energy Framework will support Kazakhstan to develop capacity aggregating ca. 330MW and will be structured as two components:

The provision of concessional financing from the GCF will help overcome risk and cost barriers for the development of RES projects in Kazakhstan, which are not sufficiently addressed by the FIT and/or other forms of Government support, due to the nascent development of the sector.

The provision of grant in the amount of USD 4 million for technical assistance by the GCF will enable the EBRD to work with the Government of Kazakhstan on strengthening the institutional capacity within the legal and regulatory framework, and building the national capacity necessary for promoting the development of RES projects.

The provision of GCF support is expected to have a transformational impact at a sector and country level, and will build upon the basis set by the Clean Technology Fund in Kazakhstan.

### Section C: DETAILED PROJECT / PROGRAMME DESCRIPTION

#### C.1. Strategic Context

Kazakhstan has been an early and enthusiastic proponent of sustainable development, aligning national strategic plans and programs with the green economy objectives. These are underpinned by ambitious low carbon and sustainability targets defined in the Kazakh Green Economy Strategy (2030). Achieving these targets established by these policy commitments require a significant scaling up of financing from the public and private sources.

Kazakhstan possesses significant renewable energy resources, such as wind, solar and hydropower. The country's hydro power potential is estimated at 27 TWh per year and wind potential at 43 TWh per year while solar potential is estimated to be 5.9-10 TWh per annum with the highest potential in the south-eastern part of Kazakhstan.

The leading coal- and gas-fired power plants in Kazakhstan representing 80% of the fossil fuel-based installed capacity are built in the 70s, 80s and early 90s. These plants have reached their end-of-life stage and require modernisation on the back of the forecasted increasing electricity demand at ca. 2.5% per year. The coal-fired power plants are also a major source of air pollution in Kazakhstan due to their age, inefficient operation system.

The levelised costs of electricity for supercritical coal (meeting the EU standards on pollutant emissions), wind and solar power generation have been estimated by the International Energy Agency (IEA) to be 10-25% lower than those from existing coal-fired plants. Climate change and external economy costs of emissions are taken into consideration, together with the anticipated cost reduction in power generation from wind and solar on the back of the steep learning curve (e.g. IEA estimates that LCOE for solar and wind installations will be 20-35% lower by 2040 than today). The transition to a low carbon economy and increased share of renewable electricity will significantly narrow the gap between RES and new coal.

As an example if the Kazakh Emissions Trading Scheme (ETS) were put in place, it would require that all power plants reduce their emissions intensity by at least 25% below projected business as usual. The most efficient coal based power plants, which will cover 13% of the gap between RES and new coal.

The investment results under the Frameworks will encourage the Government of Kazakhstan and the sector to achieve the RES targets between 2020 and 2050 in the scheme and carbon market. As the market grows over time, it will become attractive to the private sector.

Recognising climate change and environmental challenges associated with the coal-fired generation, and seeing the need to diversify the country's economy due to its over dependency on the natural resources sectors, the Kazakh Government launched a national initiative in 2016 to promote the development of renewable energy sources. The initiative became the platform for Kazakhstan’s commitment during the UNFCCC Conference of Parties (COP) in Marrakech in November 2016 and was formalised through the National Determined Contribution (NDC) to reduce GHG emissions.

The NDC, and now NDC+ in Kazakhstan ratified the Paris Agreement, calls for an emission reduction target of at least 50% below projected business as usual by 2030. This target reflects a reduction of at least 22% below projected business as usual on emissions. On 27th October 2016, the Kazakh Senate adopted the Law “On ratification of the Paris Agreement”. The current renewable energy policy framework was introduced in Kazakhstan with the support of the EBRD technical assistance in June 2013 and assumes support of renewable projects through the RTF. Due to the introduction of the RES support mechanism, Kazakhstan has seen a significant increase in the share of wind power generation from 5% in 2014 to 9% in 2016, as well as hydropower plants. Power generated from these sources increased by 32% in 2016 as compared to 2015, though in absolute terms it is still a small share of the total power generation.

The EBRD has been supporting the Government of Kazakhstan in developing the Green Economy legislation over the last 8 years, which includes the work on the development of commercially viable renewable energy legislation, the development of an emissions trading system (ETS) and the establishment of a carbon market. The EBRD also supported the government with the development of Kazakhstan's INDC to the Paris Agreement in 2015. The EBRD is currently working with the Astana International Finance Centre on the consideration of a Green Financial System, which would help to align capital flows with the green economic development.

Achieving Kazakhstan’s vision of the green economy transition and closing the green investment gap will require a significant mobilisation of the private sector. There is a growing base of knowledge which demonstrates that private sector investment can be leveraged in order to achieve this goal. However, there are several barriers to this happening. The government currently unable to support the private sector in opening up the innovative and technically demanding niche of RES projects. Access to finance for renewable energy projects remains limited as the financial appetite from the international financial sector to support the Paris Agreement is limited. The financial sector is currently shallow in both absolute and relative terms, and it is in need for financial instruments that can help to develop this nascent market. The EBRD proposes to scale up investment in RES projects through a public-private partnership model with that will, with the support from the GCF, finance independent RES projects and scale up the RES investments in Kazakhstan.

## Barriers
Renewable energy production in Kazakhstan is insignificant at less than 1%. This has been the case due to the following barriers:

- Financial barriers: The lack of availability of long-term financing from both local and international banks in Kazakhstan to fund RES projects, particularly given the long tenors these projects typically require. Availability of equity also constrains the development of RES projects.
- Constraints on institutional capacity: The regulatory bodies have constraints in their capacity to manage grid balancing, integrate renewable energy into the grid, and provide supporting services for the operation and maintenance of the RES tenders.
- Existence of an abundant and cheap gas generation source of heat, as well as a lack of an appropriate carbon price, it has been challenging for private sector project developers to compete with coal-fired power plants in terms of cost given absence of internalisation of the environmental cost of using carbon-intensive energy sources.

## Framework impact on baseline
The framework will contribute towards systemic change in the country's power generation sector where coal remains the dominant fuel by supporting the development of private renewable energy projects and moving towards sustainability of the power market. Furthermore, it will support the achievement of Kazakhstan’s 2030 target to reduce its carbon intensity by 45% compared with 1990 levels as well as the reduction of Kazakhstan's carbon intensity, with the expected GHG emission reductions of ca. 640,000 tons of CO₂ annually once all projects are operational.

The framework has developed a methodology to assess CO2 emissions driven by the proposed Framework. For details, please refer to section E1.

In addition to the installed capacity target that has been announced but based on certain assumptions related to the average load factor of various technologies and the future power generation, it is estimated to be ca. 1,800MW.

### C.3.2. Project/Programme Description

#### Proposed Operation

The EBRO proposes to the GCF to implement an Investment Programme with a 5-year duration leading to the total investments of USD 160 million in the renewable energy sector, after financial institutions along with the proposed investment from the GCF. The investments will be in the form of loans to RES developers to finance the construction, connection to the grid, commissioning and start-up costs for small-scale (up to 5 MW) hydroelectric power plants.

The proposed Programme builds on the following milestones achievements and experience of the Bank:

1. Implementation of the Ukraine Sustainable Energy Financing Facility (USEFF) (see Annex 1);
2. Successful development and operation of the largest solar power plant in Kazakhstan – the 50MW Burabay SPP (see Annex 2);
3. Policy dialogue up to over 8 years with the Government of Kazakhstan on the green economy transition, including renewable energy.

The benefits of the Programme for Kazakhstan is to develop the market of independent RES developers by creating a large pool of projects and project sponsors, attracting attention from both local and international banks, increasing the integration of RES into the power system, reducing the cost of electricity generation from RES, and contributing towards the achievement of the goals in the RE market. It will contribute towards the achievement of the 3% target share of renewable power generation at a country level by 2020, substitute carbon intensive power generation in the north and reduce the power deficit in the southern regions of Kazakhstan.

Furthermore, the Programme will contribute considerably to Kazakhstan’s climate mitigation efforts. Kazakhstan is a country with very high emissions of CO2 from power generation, and the RES financed by the Programme will therefore have a substantial impact on reducing these emissions. The expected reduction in CO2 emissions for the entire programme based on the reported data and the current portfolio, the expected direct CO2 emissions reductions by new and additional RES generation are ca. 0.64 million tons CO2 equivalent per year once all sub-projects are operational, with an estimated lifetime of 20 years.

### GCF Contribution

The proposed committed contribution from the GCF is USD 160 million for concessional loans and technical assistance.

#### Component I: Scaling up renewable energy investments

The GCF is required to provide concessional financing of up to USD 106 million, or ca. 19% of the total projects costs of USD 550 million to address the real project costs and cost disadvantages as well as to cover the interest rate risk premium for loans that are fully disbursed in Kazakhstan. The GCF will finance sub-projects either (i) awarded a PPA under the FIT Scheme or a competitive tender, in each case in terms acceptable to the EBRO; or (ii) electricity distribution and transmission companies will not constitute more than 20% of total programme costs and will be subject to the same conditions as the sub-projects on the modernisation and expansion of the transmission and distribution networks aimed to remove technical barriers for RES integration. The GCF will finance small-scale hydroelectric power plants with a capacity up to medium-size wind and PV projects. Typical projects on the electricity distribution and transmission networks involve modernization and expansion of the transmission and distribution networks, including SCADA systems, automatic control and communication systems, energy management systems and smart metering systems. Financing of electricity distribution and transmission companies will not constitute more than 20% of total programme costs and will be subject to the same conditions as the sub-projects on the modernisation and expansion of the transmission and distribution networks aimed to remove technical barriers for RES integration.

Under the Kazakh FIT scheme, small hydropower is defined as an installed capacity below 55MW.

## C.5. Market Overview (if applicable)

### Electricity Market Overview

The Kazakh electricity market was shaped during the Soviet era and predominantly relies on coal-fired power plants concentrated in the North (ca. 72% of generation), next to large open pit coal deposits. Approximately 17% of electricity is generated by gas fired power plants and only about 10% comes from nuclear fuel – five hydropower plants built several decades ago.

As a result, most of the power supply in North Kazakhstan comes from Soviet-era thermal power plants with low efficiency and relies on high voltage transmission lines delivering electricity from the North as well as from the neighbouring Kyrgyz Republic. In total, there are 10 major power companies which account for more than 95% of the total installed capacity (TIC) in the country at the same time, there are six power companies of which the five largest account for ca. 85% of generation, while the ten largest account for ca. 80% of the total electricity output.

### Electricity Output and Consumption

Electricity output has been growing steadily in Kazakhstan over the past 10 years apart from minor reductions in 2009 and 2013. Overall, the Kazakh economy remains energy intensive with oil & gas, mining and industrial companies being the major consumers of electricity. The country is a net importer of electricity as it has been unable to meet its domestic electricity deficit which was covered through imports from Russia and the Kyrgyz Republic; however, in 2013 Kazakhstan became a net exporter of electricity supplying excess from the northern plants to Russia but this was suspended in 2015 due to the unfavourable terms of supply. The country’s power sector is characterised by high generation costs and low efficiency levels. At the same time, the country's power system lacks manoeuvrable balancing capacity and has to rely on Kyrgyz hydropower plants for balancing.

### Component 1
The framework focuses on financing of the RES projects under the FIT scheme. Under the Programme each Borrower planning to develop an off-site project concludes a PPA based on the Ministry Energy approved template, with the FSC.

* For each facility (the “Seller”) sells electricity generated at the relevant power plant (the “Facility”) in accordance with and in manner contemplated by the PPA.

* Seller is responsible for designing, constructing, erecting, installing, testing, commissioning, owning, operating and maintaining the network assets necessary to interconnect the Facility to the transmission network in accordance with the requirements of the FSC. The Seller is also required to install an electric meter on the site and comply with REGOC’s standards and instructions for the network operation.

* The Buyer (the “Buyer”) is required to buy all electricity generated by each Facility at a price determined by the terms of the PPA.
The electricity generated by each facility is then proportioned to the thermal electricity generation in proportion to their electrical output. Interest of 2% is charged on overdue payments and interest of 10% will be charged if the payment is not made within 3 days or capitalised at 50% of the overdue amount.
The term of the PPA’s starts 15 years starting from the commercial operation date.

In display, the electricity generated by each facility is deemed to have been generated/consumed in the wholesale electricity market provided by the National Distancing Centre within REGOC.

A detailed economic and financial analysis will be conducted at project level for investments that meet the Framework's eligibility criteria. The financial analysis of a typical project to be financed through this Framework has been provided to the GCF Secretariat, which is available in Annex 3.

### Project Categorization
EBRD categories each project to determine the nature and level of environmental and social investigations. A project is categorized as:

- A: When it could result in potentially significant adverse future environmental and/or social impacts which, at the time of categorization, are readily identified or assessed, and which, therefore, require a formal and participatory environmental and social impact assessment process.

Environmental and social approval requirements may vary depending on the project and will be determined by EBRD on a case-by-case basis.
- C: When it is unlikely to have any significant adverse future environmental and/or social impacts, and can readily be addressed through limited environmental and social appraisal.

Initial Environmental and Social Examinations are carried out where insufficient information is available at the time of categorization to determine the appropriate category and scope of appraisal.

### H.1.1. Paradigm Shift Objectives and Impacts at the Fund Level

#### Paradigm shift objectives

The fundamental purpose of the framework is to support the efforts towards low-carbon growth in Kazakhstan through achieving sustainability of the RES market by creating a large pool of projects that can be developed, financed, and implemented over time, supporting energy sector development and enabling carbon market access, thus enabling the state necessity to bring the energy sector on the path for a lower-carbon development path.

The paradigm shift objectives are therefore the following:

- To provide a transformational impact at a sector/country level by a number of capacity building activities, including developing and implementing a national policy framework, creating and balancing, integrating RES projects into the domestic emissions trading system, monitoring and further developing the calculation mechanism for the FIT; and to address barriers to the implementation of RES projects.
- To support clarity and eliminate policy ambiguity and regulatory uncertainty, which in turn will increase investor confidence and reduce transaction costs associated with implementing the RES projects.
- To share knowledge and best practices to ensure first mover experience by learning in best practice in project management, tender processes, and environmental and social safeguards.
- To help finance and bring financial close a critical number of first projects in RES to demonstrate the economic viability of renewable energy technologies, thereby increasing investments attractiveness and potentially make the RES projects attractive to local financiers.

#### Shift to low emission sustainable development pathways

The multi-level approach encourages the development of a private sector-led investment market for RES in Kazakhstan. The project will support the government’s efforts towards decarbonizing its power sector over the long-term. Directly, the impact indicator for the Framework is the reduction of carbon dioxide emissions from the generation of electricity to be achieved via the generation of clean energy through the lifetime of the assets. This will be around 12.8 million tonnes of CO₂ (with the expected project lifetime of 20 years).

Finally, by serving as a successful and practical example of low-carbon development approach, the framework has the potential to influence planning in other countries in Central Asia and elsewhere.

# UKRAINE SUSTAINABLE ENERGY LENDING FACILITY (www.usef.com.ua)

In 2009, the EBRD launched a financing facility, initially called the Ukraine Renewable Energy Direct Lending Facility and later renamed as the Ukraine Sustainable Energy Lending Facility ("USEF").

USEF accelerated the development of the renewable energy sector in Ukraine through an innovative combination of EBRD commitment to provide non-recourse finance (climate finance) and concessional co-financing (climate finance). USEF was initially approved for 2009 for up to EUR 130 million. The Facility consisted of EUR 84.5 million of EBRD committed non-recourse finance, with a grant from the Global Environment Facility ("GEF") and EUR 46 million of sponsor equity. This was supported by technical assistance of USD $4.5 million (EUR 6.22 million equivalent) from the GEF to support project preparation and implementation.

USEF aimed to provide non-recourse finance directly to domestic enterprises to fund small and medium-sized renewable energy projects, including solar, wind, small hydro and biogas.

To ensure effective implementation of the Facility, the EBRD applied its proven business model for sustainable energy businesses, which combines investment with technical assistance and policy dialogue.

A grant of USD $4.5 million provided by the GEF for technical assistance was used to support work on:

- Conducting environmental assessments
- Developing project preparation plans

The Facility marked the first implementation of non-recourse finance or project finance in Ukraine for smaller-scale renewable energy projects, further supported in a fully integrated package by policy dialogue, institutional capacity building, and concessional co-financing. This innovative approach was made possible without generous contributions from the CTF and GEF. Through these activities USEF has strengthened the sustainable energy sector in Ukraine, increased its profile, and formed the development of renewable energy projects.

After a slow start due to the complex nature of the sector and lack of project preparation capacity, USEF expected first seven renewable energy projects by the end of 2013. These uses biogas, small wind, hydrot, or solar energy to generate heat and power in rural areas, with the potential for further expansion.

With a robust project pipeline remaining, and ongoing weakness in the commercial financing sector for renewable energy, the Facility is now well positioned. Using further lessons from this experience, USEF continues working on strengthening the long-term sustainability of the sector. Second replenishment is under consideration potentially for 2013/14.

# ANNEX 3: GENDER ANNEX

## A. Kazakhstan Country Gender Profile

Purpose: This annex provides a summary of a gender profile prepared in 2015 for the EBRD and updated in 2016 using publicly available sources. It is not intended to be an exhaustive review of all gender-related issues in this country.

### Key Points

The population of the Republic of Kazakhstan is highly diverse, comprised of over 100 ethnic groups.

This is a supportive legal framework for gender equality, although in practice women's access to finance, employment and services remains influenced by traditional norms and stereotypes. Women are more likely than men to be employed as teachers or nurses; they also retain primary responsibilities for family care and unpaid domestic work.

Women face significant barriers when it comes to land ownership and inheritance, and women obtain in many cases less favorable terms compared to their male counterparts when obtaining land and property or in accessing public services compared to their urban counterparts.


## Access to Services

### Rural/Urban Divide

Economic growth has not translated into a better socio-economic situation for women and men outside Almaty and Astana. As such, the rural/urban divide is a major determinant of gender equality.

Women are more likely than men in rural areas to be employed as teachers or nurses with fewer opportunities for paid employment, a greater burden of domestic work and a lack of infrastructure and services. Women also face significant barriers when it comes to land ownership and inheritance, although it is also particularly high in households with large numbers of children, and increases in female-headed households.

### Transport

Women tend to use public transport more than men in Kazakhstan, and they travel more often by bus or train (ADB, 2012). Access to transport is more difficult in rural areas compared to urban areas, where some municipal governments—including Almaty—have started investing in public transport infrastructure. Women are also more likely to be victims of violence suffered from the after-effects of underinvestment for more than 20 years. Field research for this project found that women were more likely to be victims of sexual harassment on public transport amongst passengers or drivers; however, sexual harassment is a difficult issue to measure in Kazakhstan and elsewhere and requires further research.

### Water and Sanitation

According to ADB (2012) study, access to water is a particularly acute problem in rural areas where women are responsible for collecting it. In urban areas, the responsibility of household use is predominantly a female responsibility. Some studies found that women in rural areas spend more time on domestic chores than men and have less time available for other activities (eating, bathing, cooking, drinking and laundry). This was particularly the case in remote areas, where water sources are far from home.

### Energy

Women tend to be the frontline users of electricity (given they are more bear primary responsibility for household chores and child care and therefore spend more time in the home), but men are most often responsible for paying the bills (ADB, 2012). In rural areas, women are also responsible for collecting firewood or other fuel collection (i.e. wood or dried dung animal).

## Labour force participation

Women’s labour force participation reaches 70% compared to 78% for men, which is higher than in other regions of Europe and Central Asia (World Bank Gender Indicator). The gap between women and men's labour force participation narrowed slightly over most of the period. This narrowing was largely accounted for by a lower retirement age for women. ### Notable trends

Women remain concentrated in lower-paid sectors and occupations. To some extent, women are excluded from working in a number of occupations in traditionally male-dominated sectors such as agriculture, construction, and manufacturing. A large share of jobs that are prohibited for women by legislation (women are prohibited from working in 29% of all jobs) is found in these sectors (agriculture, construction, and the mining and quarrying sector). # ANNEXE: STRATEGIC ENVIRONMENTAL REVIEW FOR KAZAKHSTAN RENEWABLE ENERGY SECTOR (SUMMARY) [http://kazrenew.kz/index.html]

## THE EBRD COMMISSIONED A STRATEGIC ENVIRONMENTAL REVIEW FOCUSING ON RENEWABLE ENERGY TECHNOLOGIES IN OPTIMAL AREAS OF DEVELOPMENT FOR KAZAKHSTAN. THESE ARE HYDROPOWER, WIND POWER, SOLAR POWER, BIOGAS AND GEOTHERMAL TECHNOLOGIES.

### THE EBRD'S STRATEGIC ENVIRONMENTAL REVIEW (SER) IS COMPLIANT WITH THE BANK'S ENVIRONMENTAL AND SOCIAL POLICY AND ITS PUBLIC INFORMATION POLICY AS WELL AS BEING GUIDED BY THE EU DIRECTIVE 2013/34/EC ON THE ASSESSMENT OF THE EFFECTS OF CERTAIN PLANS AND PROGRAMMES ON THE ENVIRONMENT.

### THE SER IS COMPLIANT WITH BOTH THE EUROPEAN UNION (EU) DIRECTIVES AND NATIONAL LEGISLATION. IT PROVIDES A HIGH LEVEL OVERVIEW OF POTENTIAL ENVIRONMENTAL EFFECTS ALONG WITH RECOMMENDATIONS FOR MITIGATION MEASURES TO BE IMPLEMENTED IN KAZAKHSTAN.

### THE SER WAS COMPLETED IN FIVE STEPS AS FOLLOWS:

- Stage 1: Scoping - Setting the context and objectives, establishing the baseline, and defining the scope.
- Stage 2: Developing and refining alternatives and assessing effects;
- Stage 3: Identifying mitigation measures;
- Stage 4: Consulting on the draft SER report.

### RENEWABLE ENERGY SCENARIOS

Areas with good potential for renewable energy development in Kazakhstan have been identified at the scoping stage. These areas include hydro, wind, solar, biogas and geothermal technologies. Further analysis of potential locations, technologies and operating conditions will be carried out to identify optimal areas of development.

### STAKEHOLDER ENGAGEMENT

The SER was developed in compliance with the EBRD's Environmental and Social Policy and its Public Information Policy as well as being guided by the EU Directive 2013/34/EC. Stakeholder consultation was been on-going during scoping, developing alternatives, and identifying mitigation measures. The draft Strategic Environmental Assessment Plan (SEAP) was being developed and implemented, setting the scope and timelines for consultations throughout the SER process.

### RESULTS

The SER resulted in:

- Assessment recommendations specific to investment projects and their spatial and temporal planning.
- Two comprehensive first ones are an annotated list of areas/locations most suitable for development of renewable energy technologies (to provide an indication for future development).
- The second one is a recommendation for a focused environmental and social due diligence process for individual projects, including the identification of potential adverse effects on people and the environment in line with the requirements of the Bank's Environmental, Social and Policy and associated Performance Requirements.
- Identification of key areas of concern and opportunities to avoid or mitigate adverse effects at an early stage of project development.
- Capacity building for consultants and developers in environmental and social compliance with the Bank's Environmental, Social, and Public Information Policy; and for regulations in SEA procedures and processes.

# ANNEX I: THEORY OF CHANGE MODEL

## Activities

1. **Concessional finance**
   - Investment in building generation capacity from renewable energy sources and modernizing transmission infrastructure.

2. **Technical assistance and capacity building**
   - Provide project-level technical support to public entities.
   - Support the implementation of MRV systems, the introduction of an auction-based system for RES projects, and the development of requirements for remote monitoring of RES projects.

3. **Policy dialogue**
   - Support regulatory reforms to address market barriers.
   - Review and further develop the domestic carbon market to integrate RES projects.

## Results

- Enhance access to finance for RES-related projects.
- Build at least 330MW of RES capacity, contributing to at least 18% of the national RES target in the country's energy mix.
- Modernize the MRV system and regulatory framework for RES projects to create a competitive entry of RES.
- Reduce CO2 emissions by 12.8 Mt over their lifetime (GHG emissions reduction due to network loss is not included).

## Impacts

- Demonstrate new replicable RES projects and models for investment in the renewable sector, leading to an increased share of RES in the country's energy mix.
- Create a financial and regulatory system for RES projects that will enable competitive entry of RES.
