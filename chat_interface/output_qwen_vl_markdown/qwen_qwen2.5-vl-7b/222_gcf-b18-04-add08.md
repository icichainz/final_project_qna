C1. Strategic Context

The IPCC has singled out low-lying river deltas to be one of the most vulnerable systems to climate change impacts. These deltas are home to a large proportion of the world's population and provide agricultural lands, industrial/transport infrastructure and valuable touristic assets. Compounding the vulnerability is the fact that many of these deltas are located in regions where freshwater flows into sea, are sinking due to both natural factors (i.e., compaction of river sediments over time) and anthropogenic causes (e.g., land subsidence), and where the flow of sediment is low or absent each year. This results in mouth build-up and delta lands, groundwater depletion, and increased motion heights vulnerability to coastal flooding, particularly when combined with sea-level rise.

The Nile Delta accounts for more than 50% of Egypt's economic activity through agriculture, industry and tourism. It provides a livelihood for over 12 million people (37% of the population), representing around 40% of employment, around 30% of the labor force. As Egypt does not produce enough food to feed its current population, it is heavily dependent on imports from abroad. The Nile Delta and Alexandria have had a direct adverse impact on the livelihoods of millions of people and lead to hardship throughout the entire economy.

Coastal areas in the Nile Delta are especially vulnerable to climate variability and changes at sea level. Extreme events that result in increased sea-level events, driven by the combination of high tides secondary to storm surges and rising sea levels, have caused significant damage to coastal infrastructure and loss of life. The cost of these extreme events is measured in billions of dollars in damages. The impacts, including the loss of life during coastal floods in Alexandria in 2015, are expected to increase as sea level rises. In addition, areas located hundreds of meters inland were significant. The rate of sea-level rise for the Nile Delta ranges between 3 and 8 mm/year from 1976-2014, with a mean value of approximately 5.8 mm/year. This is expansion; locally sinking land due to compaction of sediments; and loss of annual replenishment of sediment by the Nile River. The rate of sea-level rise for the Nile Delta has increased from around 3.6 mm/year from 1993 to 2010. During the same period, local land subsidence has been evident across the entire Delta, with actual rates ranging from about 0.4mm/year in Alexandria to West to around 35mm/year in Port Said.

Coastal areas in the Nile Delta will be more vulnerable to an increasing frequency and intensity of extreme weather events due to climate change. The projected increase in sea-level rise, combined with a changing outcome, regional projections at the spatial scale of the Nile Delta suggest that the southern Mediterranean coast could experience an average rate of 10 mm/year from now until 2050, compared to an average of three natural disasters/year in 1980 to an average of >10/year in 2050. An increase in frequency and intensity of extreme weather events is expected due to climate change, including more frequent landslides and more frequent and intense storms is a necessary inference from the review of recent trends and future climate change forecasts.

The projected increase in sea-level rise induced sea-level rise on the North Coast of Egypt has been and will continue to be direct and far-reaching. As of 2017, much of Egypt's population, industry, infrastructure, agriculture, and tourism are located along the coast or within a few kilometers from low coastal lands, and the reliance on the Nile Delta for prime agricultural land is critically important to the country’s economy. Studies on the vulnerability of Alexandria indicated that sea level rise of 0.3m would lead to infrastructure damage worth billions of dollars, displacement of over half a million

### Coastal baseline protection activities and investments in the North Coast

The Gulf began addressing coastal zone management issues in 1992, but the proper addressing of GCM issues was only initiated after the mid-1990s. The recent growing awareness of existing and potential coastal problems has been manifested in several legislative and institutional developments that have led to the establishment of a Secretariat for the Protection of Coastal Communities and institutions responsible for management coastal resource. The Law on the Environment (Law No. 7/2003) provides for the preparation of an environmental impact assessment (EIA) for any new project, authorized construction of coastal structures, or any other activity that may have a significant adverse effect on the environment. It also provides for monitoring networks; facilitated management and supervision of natural protected areas, called for IZCM; and promotes public participation in decision-making processes through public hearings and coordination among concerned authorities (El Rasy, 2004). As the law establishes the Secretariat of the Environment, it is expected that this institution will be responsible for coordinating with other bodies to engage with the concerned agencies and ministries in the preparation of a National Integrated Coastal Zone Management Plan for Egypt's Coastal Zone.

Up through the mid-1990s, coastal protection measures and investments in protective measures along the Nile Delta coast have included an emphasis on "hard" coastal structures, such as sea walls, jetties, groins, and breakwaters. These structures were intended to protect against flooding and other unintended adverse outcomes. For example, while the stabilization of the tip of the Damietta peninsula was intended to prevent erosion, it has had the unintended consequence of reducing the scouring of the seabed and steepening of the beach profile in front of the west. West of the seawall, the coastal zone is characterized by a series of small islands and sandbars that have been eroded, shoaling and submerged spits being formed in the shade of each breakwater unit. E.3 Particular Problem

Degree to which the proposed activity can catalyse impact beyond a one-off project/programme investment

101. **Problem:** The North Coast of Egypt is particularly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change, notably flooding impacts associated with sea level rise and the increased frequencies of extreme weather events such as storms and hurricanes. This vulnerability is most evident in low-lying lands in the Nile Delta where the impacts from climate change constitute a long-term flooding threat to both land and natural environment, posing risks of damages to key infrastructures and loss of agricultural productivity and livelihoods for local communities. Today, sea levels have already risen several centimeters relative to historical mean sea levels, resulting in increased coastal erosion and saltwater intrusion into freshwater aquifers. Sea level rise threatens to inundate large swathes of low-lying land along the Northern Coast potentially affecting millions of people living there. This is further exacerbated by the already evident and worsening impacts of climate change.

102. **Key structural reasons for the problem:** There are several underlying reasons regarding the vulnerability of Egypt's North Coast to climate change. First, the entire region is very flat with several large areas below sea level. Rising seas over the past century have resulted in severe flooding events that will likely become more frequent and intense in the coming decades. Second, there is widespread land subsidence in the Nile Delta, a result of the loss of annual sediment deposits from the Nile River due to upstream damming. This means that not only are seas rising but land is subsiding, which amplifies the coastal threat. Third, apart from industrial and urban development, human activities such as agriculture have also contributed to sea level rise and storm events. What coastal protection structures are in place to prevent flooding are inadequate or poorly maintained due to lack of funding and maintenance costs associated with rising sea levels.

103. **Key structural reasons for the problem:** There are several key structural reasons regarding the vulnerability of Egypt's North Coast to climate change. First, there is a lack of coordination across governmental organizations that have a stake in coastal development planning and management. Second, there has been no comprehensive assessment as yet on how serious an accounting of the threat of climate change in planning for coastal zones. Shore protection structures are often designed based on historical sea levels. Thus, design storms (events e.g., the 100-year storm event) used in building these structures are not representative of future conditions and there is a lack of a systematic observation system that is capable of monitoring changes in the marine environment.

## 160. The cross sections of Model 1 designs are presented in Figure 1 and will be constructed in areas where the adjacent land elevation is less than one meter above mean sea level. There are no large stone face coverings included within this sub-project.

Sub-designs are distinguished by the quantities of dredged material required to construct them. Sub-design A requires the least amount of dredged material in comparison with Sub-design B and a larger amount of such material. All three sub-designs require that there be at least 10 centimeters between sand fill and the substratum.

The Model 4 design will be constructed in areas where the adjacent land elevations are greater than or equal to three (3) meters above mean sea level and less than five (5) meters above mean sea level.
