The Renewable Barbados project will be the first large-scale hydrogen storage solution in the country. The solar PV power generation with batteries and hydrogen storage will provide clean firm capacity to meet peak demand during evening peak hours providing 6 percent of the country's electricity demand, avoiding 693,000 tonnes CO2e over a 25-year lifetime.

The government of Barbados (GoB) has set ambitious decarbonization targets for 2030, including a target to achieve net-zero emissions by 2040 under the renewable energy matrix. The Barbados Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) target GHG emissions reduction of 44 percent compared to its business-as-usual scenario in 2019 levels and 58 percent compared to 2010 levels, with a 67 percent reduction by 2030 under a base scenario, implying a 35 percent increase from its 2019 levels. Given the current power generation mix of fossil fuels (coal, diesel, and gas), Barbados is in need of more renewable energy generation. Barbados has abundant solar resources with high irradiation and capacity factors thanks to its location close to the equator. A 1 MWp solar power plant is already operating, but developers of additional projects are facing challenges due to grid stability concerns.

Barbados is subject to hurricanes and other regional climate events. These climate events can cause disruptions to the grid and generation facilities and, consequently, loss of power in various areas in the island. The current electricity supply system is heavily dependent on imported fossil fuels for power generation. This challenge relates to the access to and pricing of fossil fuels that currently are needed for most power generation capacity (coal, diesel, and gas). The proposed Renewable Barbados project will highlight the vulnerability that this dependency on imported fuel costs to countries around the world and primarily to small island economies such as Barbados.

The proposed Renewable Barbados (RBB) project will use intermittent solar PV generation and combined with hydrogen storage and electrolysis for hydrogen storage to provide a clean generation facility that can be dispatched 24 hours a day. The solar is over-sized (50 MWp), so that it can charge the battery storage system during the daytime, and also generate excess power which is also generating additional electricity to fully charge a Battery Electricity Storage System (BESS). Once charged, the BESS will discharge its energy into the grid at night when there is no solar generation available through water electrolysis to then convert back to electricity at a lower capacity of 3 MW throughout the night. The project has been approved by the Barbados Planning Commission (BPC), the Building Light and Power Company (BLPC) and is based on the demand profile for the grid and the dispatch profile of existing generation facilities in Barbados. The RBB project will be connected to the grid via a 132 kV export line of a gas and diesel turbine (GT32) that was originally scheduled for 2019, in which case arguably the GHG emission mitigation could be linked to the emissions of this plant in the order of 55,555 CO2e/year. However, the RBB project will also provide additional benefits by reducing the carbon dioxide emission factor of the grid that results in mitigation of at least 27,719 CO2e per year, for a total of 693,000 CO2e over the 25-year lifetime of the RBB project.

### B.1 Climate context (max. 1000 words, approximately 2 pages)

#### Climate Change Problem
Barbados is among the most vulnerable Caribbean nations to climate change. The Barbados electricity grid is centralized and vulnerable to hurricanes. Since 2010, hurricanes Tomas (2010), Ernesto (2012), Hernandez (2013), and Sandy (2012) have caused significant damage to the island's power infrastructure, impacting the local island, causing power outages and enormous damage to the people in Barbados. Unlike its neighboring islands, Barbados has not experienced a major hurricane since 1985. However, the increasing frequency of tropical storms and hurricanes would typically be very land-intensive for this SIDS country. Adding to these challenges with the current energy landscape of Barbados is its high dependence on imported fossil fuels, which accounts for approximately 70% of total primary energy consumption in 2019 (IEA, 2020). The island also experiences price fluctuations that directly impact the cost of electricity. The country spends about 7 percent of its GDP on power imports and has experienced a significant increase in electricity prices over recent years, rising to $0.27 per kilowatt-hour (kWh), almost double the LAC average of around USD 0.15 per kWh, and outages are common (Energy Transition Initiative, 2020). The cost of fuel purchased is recovered by the utility and deployed directly as a surcharge through the Fuel Adjustment Clause which has represented USD 0.21/kWh on average in 2022.

#### Climate Targets and Strategies
The government of Barbados (GoB) developed a National Energy Policy (NEP) for 2019-2030, targeting 100 percent renewable energy and carbon-neutral island-state transformation by 2030. In 2019, about one-third of the country's GHG emissions came from its energy sector, according to Climate Watch data (World Bank Group, 2020). The NEP aims for a 50% reduction in greenhouse gas emissions scenario by 2030. In absolute terms, this represents a decrease of 23 percent compared with the baseline year 2019. The NEP also sets out to reduce energy demand and increase the share of renewable projects electricity demand to increase to 1,727 GWh in 2030 under a base scenario, implying a 5 percent increase from its 2019 levels. As part of this strategy, the NEP aims for mean generation capacity (totaling 46MW), being operational as soon as possible and that gas turbines (stationary engines >100kW) are not suitable for continued operation and expected to be used only as backup and emergency generation. The NEP also sets out a number of other strategies, including the identification of renewable energy potential, including wind, solar, PV waste, and biomass (primarily sugarcane bagasse). To this purpose, the GoB has launched several initiatives such as the Barbados Solar Energy Programme (BSEP), which provides technical support to Renewable Energy (RE) and Energy Efficiency (EE) projects in Barbados through subsidized loans, technology assistance grants, and replacement rebates for air conditioning units and the Public Sector Energy Efficiency Programme (PSEEP). The BSEP also includes a number of initiatives focused with EE technology, and installation of solar PV panels in government buildings. (b), a fleet of government electric vehicles, and the development of a national energy strategy. The PSEEP is an initiative by the government of Barbados on sustainable energy. These entities aim at developing public and private projects focused on climate resilience, energy efficiency, and renewable energy generation, showcasing the increased political interest and will to support renewable energy projects.

Given the vulnerability of Barbados to extreme precipitation events, such as tropical storms and hurricanes, this project will increase the resilience of the population by reducing dependence on fuel imports, whose delivery depends on the fair weather. In addition, the project increases the resilience of all the population of Barbados against climate change impacts. If a large-scale solar plant is connected to the grid during an extreme precipitation event, it is likely that batteries and hydrogen tanks will be charged/filled to capacity and cannot discharge quickly enough to meet demand. The same applies if the grid itself is damaged and cannot be dispatched very quickly; this is particularly important because some thermal plants require a start-up period. The project will also increase the resilience of Barbados against climate change impacts by increasing solar capacity while also providing a back-up at non-a peak rate of 3MW capacity of 40 hours, even if the solar plant is out of service for one day (this level of damage is unlikely unless the solar plant's climate-resilient design). Furthermore, the project’s location in the north-west of Barbados will provide energy security and independence to this area, which currently relies on national grid and generation plants located the west coast. If the transmission lines are damaged or cut off by extreme weather events, such as hurricanes, when a line is cut out of service, the project can operate in "island" mode (especially because the cable layout within the plant and the substation will be underground so that they are more resilient), guaranteeing energy to the residents. The island mode also allows for the use of other sources of electricity such as diesel generators or water pumps near the site can use the electricity from the project to operate. Also, if other sources of electricity in the area are damaged (e.g., a thermal facility flooded out or port terminal damaged), and the transmission lines are not damaged then electricity can also be delivered to the areas of the country being serviced by the damaged facility.

The project itself will be Paris Aligned for power resilience, in line with the Climate Risk assessment that was conducted for the project.

The project will also be supported by private sector business and other islands. Equity contributions for the HFO project in Barbados will be provided by foreign sponsors and potentially local credit unions. Part of the debt is expected to be financed by development banks and local banks with a portion of the debt being covered by concessional funding from GCF.

## Current electricity tariffs make the renewable energy option less viable:
The proposed solution is competitive when compared with diesel generation, which is the most obvious alternative, but dependent on a fuel that has high and volatile prices, is imported (resulting in balance-of-payments pressures), and is subject to price controls. The current electricity tariff structure is also quite complex, with a base tariff for consumption up to 100 kWh/month, a higher tariff for consumption between 100-250 kWh/month, and a third tier of tariffs for consumption above 250 kWh/month. This results in the average consumer paying a higher electricity bill than the actual cost of electricity. The proposed solution given that batteries only last around four hours of discharge, so a few banks of batteries would be required to provide sufficient power for the island's needs are being explored but at an earlier stage of development. The proposed project could be viable in the absence of concessional support but at a higher tariff for the electricity sold to the grid by the project. This is because the cost of electricity generated from solar PV and battery storage would be lower than electricity bill. End consumers in Barbados already pay much higher electricity tariffs ($0.27/kWh) than the ACI average of $0.15/kWh. The proposed project could also be viable as a concessionally priced, concessional source of funding which would help keep tariffs at the same or lower level for the people of Barbados (i.e., maximizing the concessionality in otherwise set financing plan will directly benefit the tariff). However, this is not possible with the current electricity tariff structure. The project could only be viable if it has an installed capacity below 10 MW and aimed mainly to intermittent generation issues are being addressed. The proposed project is currently under feasibility study and a Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) between the Government of Barbados and the World Bank is being negotiated with the Barbados Fair Trading Commission (FTC) and involves a mechanism for the project to be developed on concessional terms. However, this would require overcoming several factors through a capacity (availability) payment scheme. The concessional loan of GCF allows for the PPA that will BIFCs would pay the project to be lower. The impact of this lower tariff is then fully transferred to the end consumer. This is because the electricity generated from solar PV and battery storage is not ODA-eligible, concessional funding for the proposed project is difficult to come by, making GCF support unlikely. However, the cost of electrolyzers and fuel cells are expected to fall as their scale of production increases, which has happened in the solar PV and wind sector. The feasibility of renewable energy in Barbados is unique because the country does not have large petroleum reserves that would provide competing energy from fossil fuels with embedded subsidies. This is a particularly important consideration of concern to the government and the public.

Barbados has a tropical climate of high temperatures, which makes it an ideal location for wind and solar energy. Barbados averages 8.3 hours of sunshine per day and 5.6 kilowatts of solar irradiation per square meter. However, the intermittent nature of these resources is a challenge that must be addressed.

In recent years, there has been a significant increase in demand for electricity due to population growth and economic development. The current installed capacity of renewable energy sources in Barbados includes 10 MW of wind turbines, 73.5 MW gas turbines, and two 25 MW steam turbines, located in three sites near the coast in the south and southwest of the island. A 10 MW solar power plant is north in the first utility-sized photo-voltaic solar farm in Barbados.

The government has been working to increase the share of renewable energy in its electricity mix by establishing a new independent power producer (IPP) regime, which allows for private sector investment in renewable energy projects. The IPP regime was introduced as part of the Electricity Act 2015 and is designed to encourage private sector investment in renewable energy projects.

The Barbados Power Corporation Limited (BPLC), which operates the national electricity grid, has been working with the government to develop a new regulatory framework for independent power producers (IPPs). The IPP regime allows BPLC to enter into long-term contracts with IPPs and provides incentives for the development of renewable energy projects.

The RSB project scope includes the design, financing, construction, operation and maintenance under a PPA scheme of a renewable energy plant in the easternmost end of the island. The project site is in Horseshoe Bay, St. Andrew’s Parish, 17 km east of Bridgetown, the capital city of Barbados. The project will be based on a 73.4-hectare plot.

The plant consists of a system delivering a stable baseload and dispatchable energy through the following components:

- 50 MW solar power plant, as a primary source of electricity when the sun is shining
- 90 MW hydrogen storage system produced using electricity and a 15 MW electrolysis system during off-peak hours (7 pm to 9 pm)
- 3.4MWh fuel oil that transforms the stored hydrogen into firm electricity at night and when required
- 12MW-30MWh Li-ion battery storage system that provides peak power from 7 pm to 9 pm and, in combination with the hydrogen storage, ensures the stability of plant power service.

### Activity 1: Installation of a new source of decentralized renewable energy generation

The 50 MWp solar photovoltaics (PV) plant would enable the country to produce clean energy locally, and lower its dependence on imported fossil fuels. The solar PV plant is designed to sustain human needs and does not require fuels to run. The solar PV plant is an intermittent renewable source of energy. Its output varies with weather conditions and time of day. The battery stores excess power during periods when the output of the power station is dispatched into the grid in a stable way, while the balance is split to the electrolyzer and the battery following a linear optimization algorithm. The charging capacity of the combined storage (hydrogen and lithium-ion) is 10 MWp. The hydrogen storage system will be an integral part of the project that will act as the brain of the power plant and manage the optimal energy dispatch between different subcomponents of the hybrid power system at any given time. When the solar PV output is not sufficient to deliver the required power, the EMD will connect the battery or the fuel cell to provide power.

The plant can run on an island mode if necessary with the support of CCF concessional funding would have a competitive spot price of electricity over 25 years for renewable and dispatchable energy sources that can also replace baseload assets and peak-load generation. ## Activity 2: Installation of a short-term battery energy storage system (BESS) and a long-term green hydrogen storage system

The most mature technologies on the BESST market, but the current leading technology and most suitable for RBS is the Li-ion BESS. Electrolyzers are typically of two types: either they can be contained or which are pre-assembled and integrated in a building. Electrolyzers are expected to have an efficiency of 15 percent, with a degradation rate of around 10-15 percent per year. A reduction in the electrolyzer's operating temperature would result in a 10 to 15 percent inefficiency. The energy consumption of the electrolyzer is around 55 to 60 kWh per kg of hydrogen produced.

### Hydrogen Tank

The hydrogen storage will take place in large vessels (50 m³ to 110 m³) at "low pressure" (around 30 bar). These steel vessels, especially designed for hydrogen storage, are a technology widely used in the industry. The use of these vessels is preferred over other types because they provide the simplest and safest solution. The total capacity will be equivalent to the hydrogen needed for 90kWh of electrical energy generation.

### Fuel Cell

As with the electrolyzer, different technologies of fuel cell exist on the market: alkaline, PEM and solid oxide. The only technology suitable for RBS project is PEM. The fuel cell is a strategic brick because of the very few suppliers capable of building a multi-MW fuel cell. H2Ft Industry will be the supplier of the fuel cell. The fuel cell will have an output power of 150kW and will be installed in a dedicated container. It will be one MW fuel cell in Martinique that is currently in operation. Fuel cells are estimated to have a lifetime of 30,000 hours or around 2 years with a degradation rate of about 15 percent per year. A reduction in the operating temperature would result in a 15 percent inefficiency and a refurbishing of internal parts will be needed. The energy generation of the fuel cell is expected to be around 30kWh.

In addition to the main three components of the green hydrogen system, an overall balance of plant such as water treatment, compressors, pipelines, civil works, etc. will also be needed.

## Potential for scaling up and replication

The proposed project has a very high potential for engendering a paradigm shift given its prospects for scaling up and replicability in Barbados and in other small island economies or in off-grid rural areas.

# Independent Technical Advisory Panel's assessment of FP224

## Proposal name: Renewable Barbados Project

### Accredited entity: International Finance Corporation

### Project/programme size: Medium

## Assessment of the independent Technical Advisory Panel

### 1. Overview

RenewableBarbados is an innovative stable energy provision project based on a 53 Megawatt peak (MWp) solar photovoltaic (PV) system combined with a battery energy storage system (BESS) and green hydrogen storage. This combination will allow continuous and stable electricity supply at 10 MW for 24 hours, 9 MW for 72 hours, 6 MW for 96 hours, and 9 µW and 3 MW of other times. The core feature of the project is that the solar PV system will allow for a high degree of dispatchability, which can be achieved through both storage in the BESS to cater for short-term fluctuations during the day and by providing hydrogen as an energy storage medium. This allows for the production of green hydrogen which acts as long-term storage for provision of electricity during the night or as reserve power. Such systems are central to achieving high rates of renewables based on intermittent generation, such as solar PV, and provide a more flexible and robust dispatch option.

The project has opted for a hybrid storage system using lithium-ion (Li-Ion) batteries combined with hydrogen production and storage as an additional energy storage solution. The project is of high relevance for countries, especially small island developing States (SIDS), in terms of their ability to achieve renewable energy production and is one of the first large-scale systems of this kind worldwide.

The USD 169 million project has a finance source of EUR 41 million from GCF composed of (i) USD 20.5 million loan from the International Development Association (IDA), (ii) USD 8.5 million loan from the International Finance Corporation (IFC), and (iii) USD 3.5 million loan from the American Development Bank (IDB) and (iv) USD 66 million loan from a local commercial bank. The project is privately executed, with GCF providing co-financing in the form of equity as well as significant investment by a local commercial bank into an important climate change mitigation project. The private executing entity, RenewableBarbados, is composed of Barbadian Government (50%), GCF (24%) and IDB (26%). It should be noted that all equity needs are already committed by the existing shareholders. GCF in discussion with local credit unions to provide a loan facility for the project. The USD 169 million from IDA and IDB loans proceed are used to reduce the tariff for energy carriers and will not alter the financial internal rate of return (IRR) of the project sponsor. GCF grant proceeds are used for capacity building and knowledge-sharing.

The mitigation outcome is achieved by providing stable carbon-free base-load power to the grid. # Paradigm shift potential

## Scale: High

### 13. Barbados relies currently on over 90 per cent fossil fuel-based electricity generation.

The country is considering a shift to 100 per cent renewables by 2050. No large-scale storage and base-load plant from renewables currently exists in Barbados.

#### 18. The major paradigm shift is not so much towards renewable energy, although this alone is already a significant change. It is the combination of two technologies: solar photovoltaics (PV) as the change from solar PV being an intermittent source of energy that potentially creates grid stability challenges and battery storage as a means to address these challenges.

Barbados’ government has adopted a hybrid, combination of a BES with H2 storage. It is the first of its kind in the Caribbean and one of the only plants of this scale at this stage. A large challenge will have to be made in RE base-load generation and storage in order to achieve this goal. This project represents a critical role in assessing and choosing optimal technology solutions needed to enable this paradigm shift.

#### 10. The approach of combining H2 and a BES is one possible technological solution. Another option, already implemented in various countries, is to use only a BES. This system is more energy efficient than the hybrid approach but has higher capital costs due to the need for larger energy losses when using batteries. At current costs, a hybrid approach is also more expensive than an all-BES system.

The project will have to assess and choose optimal technology solutions related to flexibility and reduced impact of battery degradation over time. Trialling different technologies in Barbados will provide valuable lessons on how to best design, implement, scale-up and choosing optimal storage and base-load systems relative to country requirements and situations. The project is expected to be technology-neutral with respect to storage solutions but should not be exclusive or uniquely prefer the proposed hybrid solution.

#### 12. There are several critical opportunities in green hydrogen production and storage. Green hydrogen can play an important role to decarbonise short- and medium-haul maritime transport, as well as aviation. The project will assess whether Barbados could also decarbonize long-haul maritime and aviation services that are critical for islands. However, the independent TAP believes it is important that hydrogen is promoted only in areas where it has an environmental advantage over other technologies. For example, the production of green hydrogen from renewable energy sources in Barbados, battery electric vehicles which have a factor of three to four times higher efficiency in energy use than internal combustion engines and electric aircraft are all more environmentally friendly options for short-haul maritime transport and aviation.

The project will also assess whether electrolysis or fuel cells (e.g. PEM) or H2 or e-fuels based on green H2 are an option for maritime shipping and aviation. The independent TAP would recommend that Barbados should not be overly focused on the development of hydrogen technology.

#### 15. Knowledge-sharing is critical for a paradigm shift. The project includes measures for capacity-building and development of skills. Information, know-how and capacity-building should however be technology-neutral and should not be steered by the investor with vested interests in certain technologies.

The project is private sector with a private company taking the investment risk. Private sector capital is utilized for renewable energy storage solutions. This is considered as highly positive that private sector capital will be used to finance the development of RE base-load and storage provision can be delivered in a cost-effective manner with private capital.

#### 16. Overall, the independent TAP considers that the project has the potential to attract more private capital into renewable energy storage solutions. The combination of RE base-load energy storage and base-load provision are critical, and the proposed system creates a paradigm shift for Barbados. 1.4 Sustainable development potential Scale: Low to Medium

The project is aligned with several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs): SDG 3 (good health and well-being); SDG 7 (affordable and clean energy); SDG 8 (decent work and economic growth); and SDG 13 (climate action).

The Barbados electricity grid is vulnerable to hurricanes. Since 2010, hurricanes Tomas (2010), Ernesto (2012), Harvey (2017) and Elsa (2021) and the tropical storms Matthew, Maria, Kyle, and Fiona have caused extensive damage to the power infrastructure in Barbados with severe damage to the people of Barbados. 3 Key Gender Terms and Concepts

Gender analysis or assessment: Identifies, analyses and informs action to address inequalities that arise from these different roles of women and men, the unequal power relations between them, and the consequences of these inequalities on their well-being and development. This is a gender-sensitive approach. The term also means that women have less access to and control over resources, and are less likely to take part in decision-making.

Gender and energy nexus: Women and girls disproportionately bear the burden of energy poverty because of gender norms and traditions, which hinder equal access to modern energy services. Women and men have different energy needs and capacities to access energy services and appliances. This is a consequence of social gender inequalities. Gender-sensitive approaches are needed in order for energy sector development approaches further institutionalise and perpetuate existing inequalities. This is why we need to create a gender-responsive energy sector that enables women and men to equally benefit from energy services and supports their socio-economic potential.

Gender-inclusive term refers to initiatives, activities or programmes that are open to both women and men but are designed to overcome barriers to full inclusion of one group in order to achieve gender balance. In most cases, it targets the increased participation of women and girls. Implementing gender-inclusive programmes is a powerful way to promote gender equality and eradicate gender bias.

Gender-neutral: Something that is not specific to women or men. This can be policies, investments, projects or business models that are designed in such a way as to have no differential effect on women or men. Often, something is mistaken to be gender-neutral when it is "gender blind". This means that the policy, programme or business model did not take into account the ways in which it would affect women and men differently. In the end, gender-neutral programming often means that the needs of men are assumed to be the needs of everyone, which leads to women's needs being left out.

Gender-based violence (GBV): An umbrella term for any harmful act that is perpetrated against a person’s will and that is based on socially ascribed (gender) differences between females and males. The nature and extent of specific forms of GBV vary across different cultures and societies, including but not limited to: including sexual exploitation/abuse and forced prostitution, domestic violence, trafficking, forced/early marriage, harmful traditional practices such as female genital mutilation, honour killings and widow inheritance.

The Renewables* Barbados Project (referred to herein as "the Project" or "RBB") is an innovative and unique large-scale green energy project which combines the use of solar, hydrogen and lithium-ion battery technologies to construct and operate a hybrid solar photovoltaic (PV) energy facility with hydrogen storage. RSB will deliver non-intermittent renewable electricity power onto the national grid and will also accommodate a Blackbelly sheep farming facility within the solar farm site.

8 Barbados Country-Specific Gender Context

Barbados is globally recognized as a leading voice for small island developing states (SIDS) specifically in the championing of climate, climate finance and environmental issues. The country has also been consistently ranked among the top 10 countries with the highest gender equality index scores (countries with lowest corruption indexes). In 2021, the country formally became a Republic becoming the fifth Caribbean Community (CARICOM) country to do so. The country's current population is estimated to be 281,976 people. According to the World Population Prospects the country’s population growth is around 0.38%. In 2021, there were an estimated 45,000 males and 39,000 females in Barbados. This represents a male to female ratio of 1.14:1. The female population at that time was 51.57% compared against 48.43% males*. Barbados’ female population exceeded that of men by approximately 9,000 females and at the time (i.e., circa 2021) Barbados ranked 30th out of 102 countries in terms of gender equality.

Since the start of the 20th century Barbados has transitioned from a largely agrarian sugar producing nation-state to a diversified service sector-based economy where tourism, international business and finance - are among the country's main economic drivers. However, with this transition the traditional gender stereotypes that cast women and femininity as essentially different from and inferior to men and masculinity still remain intact. According to the Barbados Gender Gap Index (GGGI) published by the World Economic Forum in 2018, these gender binaries continue to affect the economic options and behaviours of women and men across the public/ private and professional/ domestic spheres. ### National Strategic Plan of Barbados 2006–2025
The National Strategic Plan of Barbados 2006-2025 was developed to enhance Barbadian society to become prosperous and globally competitive by 2025. The National Sustainable Development Policy attempted to encourage an integrated and holistic approach to sustainable development. The Sustainable Energy Framework is an ongoing technical assistance program with the Inter-American Development Bank. The objective of which is to promote renewable energy and energy efficiency to reduce fossil fuel dependency while enhancing energy security and environmental sustainability. The most recent installment of the program includes a focus on the development and modernization of the electricity framework in Barbados to support adoption of renewable energy technologies (Government of Barbados 2019).

10.2 Gender integration in The International Framework and Barbados National Climate Change Policy

### 10.3 Gender Considerations in the Updated NDC

# Conclusion

Although Barbados has and continues to put in place policy measures which aim to bridge the gender gap between females and males, the energy sector, and the renewable energy sector more specifically, is still very much a male dominated field. While women are well represented within the technical related side of the sector, they (men) hold the highest percentage of the decision-making and senior positions, with this being extended even among those highest paid in comparison to their female counterparts.

In this regard, the Renewable Barbados Project represents an opportunity for gender considerations to be appropriately addressed. The project will serve as a model for the renewable energy infrastructure project in Barbados. In this regard, the RB project would also become a practical demonstrable model for the integration of gender into future large-scale community-based renewable energy projects on small-island states. The Gender Action Plan developed by the Project Team will be used to ensure that all actions are gender-sensitive and will allow to improve women's participation and enhance their social and economic empowerment within the project.

An important component of the project would be the social and community-based activities and engagement to be outlined within the Community and Social Engagement Benefits Plan. The Plan would highlight all of the community-based activities and engagement that will be undertaken as part of the renewable energy services to the St. Philip community. Gender will be appropriately considered within the scope of the design and implementation of these actions to ensure both women and men are able to benefit, as well as the youth.
