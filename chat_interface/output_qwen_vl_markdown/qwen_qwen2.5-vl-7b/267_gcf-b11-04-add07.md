## A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

The outer islands of the Maldives experiences drinking water shortages during the dry season. These shortages have a significant impact on the population, with over 50% of the total population living below the poverty line of US$2 per day. The key problems pertaining to freshwater security relate to the increasing vulnerability of groundwater resources due to climate change and sea level rise induced decline of groundwater. As confirmed by the IPCC and RMES studies, a decrease in rainfall of up to 3.1mm/year is expected over the next century which will compound current water stress in the country. The Government faces constraints in responding to the challenge at hand without adequate resources. The lack of infrastructure for water supply and sanitation services, coupled with the high cost of desalination that confronts the Government limits the response options to this emerging crisis to largely reactive emergency measures. Long-term solutions are required to address the problem. The current situation is further exacerbated by the fact that the population on 193 islands prevents the possibility of economies of scale in providing water and sanitation services, including capital-intensive infrastructure.

In response to this climate challenge, the proposed project objective is to deliver safe and secure freshwater to 105,000 people in the islands of Maldives in the face of climate change risks. This will be achieved by delivering the following results:
- Scaling up an integrated water supply system to provide safe water to vulnerable households;
- Introduction of decentralized and cost-effective dry season water supply systems;
- Continuous quality improvement of existing water supply services.

The proposed solution is based on maximizing water production and scale-up of the use of an integrated water supply system that will bring three primary sources of water (rainwater, groundwater and desalinated water) into a least cost delivery system. The project will be implemented in 193 islands with a population of over 50% living below poverty line. This will be achieved by addressing the main barriers to implementing integrated water supply systems (cost recovery; management capacity; and institutional arrangements). The project is aligned with the policy direction. Resources are being considered for the pilot island to provide clean water in the 2008 Climate Change Strategy of the country. The project is based on national priorities and has been endorsed by the National Designated Authority for Maldives.

C1. Strategic Context

**Context: Poverty, Turmoil and Vulnerability**

The Maldives consists of a 1,190 small low-lying coral islands clustered in 26 ring atolls, spread over a North-South axis covering an area of approximately 300 km² with a population of about 389,000 spread over 194 islands. With high-end tourism as the main driver of economic growth, the country has made a significant development progress and graduated from being one of the world’s poorest countries to a middle-income economy in 2015. However, this progress is unevenly distributed. There is spatial disparity between the capital, Male, and other areas in human development indices. The poverty headcount ratio at national poverty line (NPL) for Male has fallen from 3% to less than 5%, while for the outer islands it has fallen at a lower rate, from 52% to 25% (UNDP, 2014). This is also reflected in the Human Development Index (HDI), which has increased by 6.7 points for Male and 3.8 points for the outer islands since 2000. The poor are disproportionately affected by unequal opportunities. On account of the spatial disparity, every 1 in 3 persons was living in the capital city of Male, where the most public services, including water supply are provided and their affordability is the most favourable.

The Maldives is a typical example of the “dollar paradox”. Its relative prosperity through domestically generated income contrasts with its vulnerability to external shocks. The country’s economy is highly exposed to price fluctuations in oil prices or contractions of the tourism sector – and high structural costs due to its particular geographical characteristics. In addition, the country has been facing a number of challenges related to climate change, including sea-level rise, small island economies including policy and legislative formulation, regulatory activities, education, social services, public health, water supply and sanitation, urbanization, and environmental degradation. These challenges are resource and constraint constrained. Longer-term impacts of urbanization are already happening including increasing water demand and pollution of naturally occurring, and very important, groundwater resources.

**Context: Policies and Institutions**

The legislative and policy basis for the provision of water supply and sanitation services in the Republic of Maldives is the Water Act (2014) and its implementing regulations (Constitution of the Republic of Maldives (2008), the Manifesto of the Progressive Party of the Maldives (2013-2017) and the Public Health Act (2012). Legislation for the entire water sector like the Water Act is still in draft stage.

The Constitution of the Republic of Maldives, Article 23 states that every citizen has a right to “adequate and nutritious food and clean water”, the establishment of a sewage system of a reasonably adequate standard on every inhabited island, and the provision of drinking water to achieve the progressive realisation of these rights by reasonable measures within its ability and resources.

The Manifesto of the Progressive Party of the Maldives (2013-2017) outlines a number of policy areas, aims and actions for the next five years. The manifesto includes the following: “To provide drinking water to every inhabitant living on an island by 2018; To ensure that all inhabitants have access to safe drinking water for all time”. The solutions include providing safe water to islands that face water shortages during the dry season, establishing a system of sewage disposal and waste management in urban areas and other inhabited islands with large populations; and establishing a faster system to provide water to islands in emergencies through regional distribution systems. In addition, it is proposed to establish a national water board to ensure the protection and conservation of water. The solutions for sewerage systems were therefore: ii) plan and implement sewerage projects in the islands; iii) introduce environmentally suitable models of sewerage to the Maldives; and iv) establish a system for sustainable maintenance of sewerage.

C2. Project / Programme Objective against Baseline

Baseline: Water security

13. Highly urbanized centres such as Male are predominantly reliant on desalinated water. Water supply is managed by a single utility company, MWSS. The majority of the population in the Maldives lives in low-lying areas and most of these regions are coastal. However, in rural outer islands, individual households are largely responsible for harvesting rainwater and abstracting groundwater from private dug wells at their own cost. Management of the limited water resources is complicated by the fact that the island communities have no control over the quality or quantity of water available during the seasons and the susceptibility of groundwater aquifers to pollution from poor sanitation on many islands and salinity encroachment in some areas. The population density in outer islands is generally low (less than 10 per square kilometer) but some islands (population densities of more than 300 per hectare are not uncommon in Maldives) with acute problems in groundwater quality and quantity have been identified. In addition, the water security situation on some islands has been applied in a diverse context of atolls and islands. Difference in geography (land availability), hydro-climatic characteristics (rainfall patterns, evaporation rates, temperature variations), soil type, aquifer thickness and depth (a type of aquifer basis) suggests the need for decentralized and fully customized approaches to water production and distribution systems on outer islands.

14. The freshwater groundwater lens underlying each island has historically been the most important water source for islands. The thickness of the freshwater lens, which typically floats atop the denser sea water, is controlled by island width, rainfall patterns, evaporation rates, temperature variations, soil type, aquifer thickness and depth (a type of aquifer basis). With a projected 60% increase in the number of households by 2035, the next 20 years will be critical for the water sector in the Maldives. The population growth rate is expected to be 1.8 percent per year over this period. The population of the Maldives will decline by 34 percent from the 2009 level of 103,000 people to 77,000 people by 2035. Population growth in urban areas is expected to be higher than that in rural areas. This rapid increase in population density (42%) will result in higher density communities with knock on impacts on pollution from waste disposal, particularly given the height of the islands and the lack of space for landfills unless a deliberate protection of catchment areas takes place.

The baseline scenario of increasing water stress is exacerbated by the climate change impacts. This calls for innovative and integrated solutions to water sector resilience.

Adaptation Scenario: According to IPPC and RMES reports sea level rise of 3.1 mm/year will have a profound impact on saltwater intrusion into the groundwater lenses, jeopardizing the main freshwater source for the country. In order to ensure that there is no over-exploitation of groundwater, the need to develop, protect, monitor and conserve ground water as a primary source of water as well as the long term sustainability of the project has been considered. The proposed system will not be designed to be overemphasized. A Managed Aquifer Recharge (MAR) system is planned to be developed as one of the results and outputs of this project, which will help in recharging groundwater aquifers.

158. The existing technically feasible options for producing a quality potable water supply to the inhabitants of the project area are outlined below: 1. Community based rainwater harvesting system; 2. Desalination Plant to secure minimum water requirement; and 3. Reverse Osmosis (RO) plant. In this project, the first two options will be pursued as these sources (rainwater and seawater) would be directly connected to the household to realize the ultimate goal of safe and sustainable drinking water supply. The third option is not feasible in the present context due to high cost and non-reliance desalination option due to unpredictable climate-induced rainfall pattern and the polluted ground water. Desalination plants are also not economically viable as they require a large amount of energy to produce potable water from other inexpensive sources. This economically viable approach will be environmental friendly and involve less risk of pollution in comparison with RO plants which may lead to high levels of salt concentration in the end users. In view of the contaminated ground water source, the larger section of the population within the supply area needs to have access to safe drinking water through a combination of rainwater harvesting and seawater desalination at 100% and the minimum per capita consumption would be limited to 20litres/day during extreme climatic conditions and emergency situations. The project will also include the construction of a reverse osmosis plant for producing potable water from sea and land based brackish water sources.
