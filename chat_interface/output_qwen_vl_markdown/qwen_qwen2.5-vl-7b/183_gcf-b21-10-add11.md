### Background
High population growth rates, land degradation and use pressures, changes in rainfall patterns, greater frequency and intensity of droughts, and conflicts over natural resources are undermining the resilience of Niger Basin communities—despite their efforts to adapt and mitigate these challenges.

To promote climate-resilient growth in the region, the African Development Bank, European Union, Global Environment Facility and the affected countries have allocated financing for the Programme for Integrated Development and Adaptation Climate Change in the Niger Basin (PIDACC/NB). Supplementary funding will help forge the required collaboration and provide additional complementary resources for urgent interventions.

Based on preliminary studies undertaken by the Niger Basin Authority (NBA), the PIDACC/NB builds on the Niger Basin Climate Resilience Investment Plan (CRIP). The CRIP aims to enhance climate resilience in Benin, Burkina Faso, Cameroon, Chad, Côte d'Ivoire, Mali, Mauritania, Niger, Nigeria, Senegal and Togo. It seeks to promote investments in reliable information systems, strengthened institutions, and sustainable infrastructure. PIDACC/NB also follows up on the CRIP by focusing on specific countries.

PIDACC/NB aims to contribute to improving the resilience of populations and ecosystems in the Basin through sustainable management of natural resources: reducing the silting process of the Niger River; enhancing the adaptability and capacity for reduced exposure to climate risks; raising awareness of climate threats and risk reduction processes; and minimizing land-use/land cover change emissions reductions.

GCF-related outcomes include improvements in:
- Management, restoration and protection of natural habitats from climate variability and change;
- Greater access to information on climate change in decision-making;
- Adaptive capacity and reduced exposure to climate risks;
- Awareness of climate threats and risk reduction processes; and
- Mitigation of land-use/land cover change emissions reductions.

The programme is scalable and contributes to transformational change. It is also structured to ensure long-term financial sustainability.

#### C.1.1. Status of the Niger Basin, including basin strategies

The Niger River Basin is one of Africa’s largest basins (hydrologically active). The basin cuts across the major climatic zones—which include: the Guinea Savanna vegetation belt in the Fouta Djallon Massif in Guinea, the semi and Sahel Savanna zone in Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger, Chad, Sudan, Central African Republic, and Cameroon. The population of the basin is expected to rise to more than 180 million by 2025. Heavy reliance on natural resources, combined with ongoing conflicts and external effects of climate change, make it one of the most fragile river basins in Africa.

Niger Basin Sharing Agreement (NBSA) was signed between the countries of the basin on February 2002 to lead a Shared Vision process for the sustainable management of the Basin. The Shared Vision is an expression of shared aspirations and values that promote a framework for enhancing cooperation and sharing benefits deriving from the Niger Basin’s resources. The Shared Vision is implemented through the Sustainable Development Action Plan (SDAP).

The SDAP with its Implementation Plan (2006-2027). The SDAP defines and guides the development processes in the Niger River Basin. It comprises 350 actions for a total amount of US $ 7.2 billion. An estimated 89.4% of the investments planned in the Operational Plan are to be financed by the countries.

Niger Basin Water Charter (2008). The charter is designed to ensure that NBA Member States share the river’s resources equitably. The Charter lays out a series of principles for equitable and responsible participation and use of the Niger River water.

Silt Control in the mid-Niger River Basin. The project financed by the African Development Bank ($ 30 million) was located in the mid-Niger River Basin, which is characterized by high sediment loads. The main objectives of this project were to: (i) include: (a) adoption of a Siting Control Master Plan and Investment Programme (SDLE); (ii) institutional strengthening of the NBA; (iii) capacity building for basin countries.

Climate adaptation initiatives in the Niger Basin. The initiatives by the COP21 relating to the Niger River Basin are numerous, particularly in terms of adaptation to climate change. The Intended Nationally Determined Contributions (INDC) of the countries include commitments on water resources and river basins. These commitments are based on a set of strategic documents specific to climate change specific to the water resource sector. These strategic documents define priority areas for intervention at national and regional level in order to enhance resilience to climate change. The CRIP was developed to test these measures selected as part of the COP21, particularly with regard to adaptation to climate change.

The Climate Resilience Investment Plan (CRIP), under the platform of the nBA. Niger Basin countries also collectively developed a Climate Resilience Investment Plan (CRIP) in 2015, which is aligned with the SDAP and the Shared Vision. The CRIP was developed with the support of the African Development Bank (ADB), World Bank Water and Energy Global Practices and World Meteorological Organization (WMO).

The CRIP (2015): is a strategic planning document that comprises 246 climate change adaptation actions for strengthening the resilience to climate change through the Niger Basin (see table 3). The CRIP’s priority actions were selected from existing plans, projects, and programs. The CRIP was developed in close collaboration with basin countries, civil society organizations, and with exercises to build local capacity. The CRIP took into account all the actions planned under the PIDACC. The Niger River Basin States collaborated on the Plan and pledged to advocate for technical and financial support to implement its priority actions.

The plan was presented at the UNFCCC COP21 in Paris to raise the profile of their climate adaptation needs. The confirmed submission of the CRIP to the Green Climate Fund (GCF) is expected by 2017, with a view to financing through the SDAP of the Basin over the nine years in three 5-year increments (2016-2018, 2019, 2021, 2022-2024). ### C-1.3: African Development Bank experience, lessons reflected in PIDACC programme design

The African Development Bank's experience from multilateral programmes in shared basins have informed PIDACC programming and project design. Lessons learned from the Niger Basin, Lake Chad, the Songo-Songo River Basin in Tanzania and the Songwe River Basin. Lessons include the following:

(i) The socio-economic and environmental conditions prevailing at the Niger Siting determine the achievement of programme targets. In activity design, emphasis should be placed on developing decentralized services to support programme implementation.

(ii) Long-term commitment is needed for achieving sustainable results and is important for institutional development programs in the basin.

(iii) Programmes must be firmly anchored to the local economy. Direct relations must be facilitated among farmers' organizations and other stakeholders involved in the project.

(iv) Communities should have greater freedom to choose their service providers, suppliers and construction enterprises.

(v) In support of PIDACC, the African Development Bank has provided a framework for basin-wide water resource management with its role to support the countries to manage the shared resource. Instrumental rainfall data in the Sahel show the strong variability of rainfall. In addition to the well-documented decline in rainfall since the late 1980s (Chappell and Agnew, 2004), there has been a shift from predominantly year-to-year variability to variability visible over longer periods of decades. The last 40 years since 1969 have witnessed relatively low rainfall levels compared with earlier decades. This is consistent with the findings of Hishiyabou (2007) and Chappell and Agnew (2004). From the mid-1990s, rainfall received somewhat in the Niger Basin, with rainfall in a number of sub-basins increasing significantly since 1995. This increase is attributed to El Niño events, in part due to rainfall increases (Ossion et al., 2005). Measures are required for strengthened resilience against rainfall variability.

In the Niger basin, droughts killed up to 250,000 people and decimated over two million livestock in the Sahel region (Kanji, Verchot, and Mackenney 2006). Benson and Clay (1989) assessed the impact of drought on the GDP of several African countries including Nigeria, Burkina Faso, and Niger. Other climate-related hazards of significance in the Niger basin include desertification, land degradation, and flooding.

Extreme climate events not only impact livelihoods through disaster shocks but also affect poor households' ability to adopt low-risk strategies that have low returns, and leave no prospect of rising above the poverty line (Hallegatte et al. 2016).

## C.2.3-1 Understanding with respect to climate risk?

In the Niger Basin, AGCMs project a general increase in temperatures, mostly between 1°C and PC for 2050 and 4°C by the end of the 21st century (RCP8.5, CMIP3 and CMIP5 Global Circulation Models). Under RCP6 and RCP8.5, AGCMs project increases in precipitation of 90% or more over the Niger Basin.

AGCMs do not agree on whether the Niger Basin is likely to become wetter or drier in the 21st Century. 50% of the models used by the IPCC (Christensen et al., 2007) predict increased rainfall, while the other half predict decreased rainfall. However a high degree of uncertainty exists regarding the projected changes in precipitation and temperature over West Africa at different timescales, to the scale that resulted in the severe droughts of the 1970s and 1980s. This underscores the importance of climate change adaptation strategies that are designed with an understanding of the intended benefits under a wide range of future climates at various time-scales. The PISDAC will factor this into the project design.

# C-2.2 Climate change and land degradation

## Unsustainable land use practices and watershed degradation.

The Sahelian part of the Niger basin has widespread land degradation due to unsustainable land uses, triggered by increased droughts and increasing desertification. Slash-and-burn farming, forest fires, deforestation, and mining concessions within selected watersheds contribute to severe degradation in the basin. The Sahara Desert is also experiencing a significant increase in desertification, with climate change being one of additional stressors that reduce water availability and contribute to land degradation (USAID 2017). These degraded environments are characterized by soil erosion, reduced vegetation cover, and increased runoff, which leads to soil degradation in the Sahel is characterized by soil degradation, mainly due to wind erosion. This is caused by climatic factors such as increasing temperatures, decreasing precipitation, and more frequent droughts, coupled with human activities like overgrazing and overfarming. These factors result in the reduction of vegetation cover, decrease in fallow periods and a reduction in the basin's water resources.

## C-2.3 Water resources scarcity and increasing environmental degradation in the Niger Basin

The Niger’s water supply is unevenly distributed, poorly accessible due to undeveloped hydraulic systems and complex topography. The basin experiences high seasonal and often unpredictable rainfall; a lack of adequate water storage already causes large and unreliable economic losses. Climate change will likely increase this trend. In addition, climate change will likely reduce the frequency of rainfall patterns and other conditions will amplify future drought severity and temperature extremes, increasing resource scarcity and environmental degradation in the Niger basin (IPCC 2013). The combination of increased water demand from population growth and diversification of sources of income resulting in benefits for agricultural production and land degradation.

## C-2.4 Water quality deterioration

Water quality deterioration. Precipitation and associated river discharge are major drivers of water pollution in the form of excess nutrients, sediments, and pathogens. These pollutants can enter rivers through point source discharges or non-point source disposal in the Niger basin in several ways. Elevated stream temperatures, combined with lower flows, may require wastewater treatment plants to be upgraded or replaced. In addition, increased sediment loads will increase the costs associated with waste disposal. The water quality of the river may suffer because of reduced dilution of pollutant concentrations, with potential health impacts. More intense precipitation and flooding events will also lead to more frequent overflows from combined sewer systems. The combination of contaminated overflow flow or combined sewer over flows contributes in declining water quality in those areas. If flood frequencies increase, human health may be impacted by sewage contamination during flood events.

## Navigation constraints

Navigation on the Niger River will become increasingly vulnerable to more varied precipitation patterns, the increased frequency and intensity of floods, and droughts. In addition, lower river levels, less sediment load, and low flows and other climatic conditions are affecting the reliability and capacity of navigation in the Middle and Lower Niger. Drought often lowers water levels on the Niger River, which can reduce the depth of the channel and increase the duration of the navigation period in Cameroon. In Chad, the Niger River carries a heavy sediment load, making navigation difficult during high flow periods. The river is also subject to seasonal flooding from tributaries and branches; the drought that dried these channels has not allowed the annual "rinsing" of the seasonal barriers created by deposits of sediments.

## C-2.5 Barriers to climate adaptation in the Niger basin

### Policy and institutional barriers
Implementing a climate resilient program in the Niger basin requires supportive policies, as well as coordination across processes and institutions responsible for climate change to avoid inconsistencies. Issues of climate change are often not addressed by existing institutions or programs that have been established to reduce agro-climate risks. Institutional barriers contribute to weak coordination between those working on climate change development and environmental issues. The lack of a clear institutional framework for addressing climate change is exacerbated by climate change, and how these relate to development priorities. Government departments responsible for poverty reduction and disaster risk reduction have limited means of coordination, which undermines adaptation efforts.

Candidate Activities implemented by the project will follow the basic principles of participatory natural resource management (PNRM). PNRM means that: i) the request for the activity must emanate from the beneficiaries; ii) the activity is consistent with Sub-basin master basins to be developed at country level and located within the Pidac area of jurisdiction; iii) contribution towards biodiversity conservation, water resource management, sustainable land use and climate change adaptation (S.C.1-4) will be in compliance with environmental safeguards (category 3 or 2 projects); iv) it be led by groups of communities (producers' groups, women's groups, etc.) or other stakeholders; v) the project activities must be consistent with the capacity of the beneficiary group to ensure a sustainable management and maintenance of the results of the project.

In addition, no activity will be approved that involves any of the following: i) location in nature reserves; ii) significant loss of primary forests, mangroves or sensitive wetland; iii) any permanent negative effect on known rare or endangered species; (iv) any adverse impact on cultural heritage sites; and iv) any other activities that are not consistent with PNRM principles added during the development of the community interventions implementation manual taking into account the local realities.

# D1.Value Added for GCF Involvement

Presenting the value added of GCF involvement as critical for the project/programme, in consideration of other alternatives.

GCF involvement in the PIDACC is critical as it will contribute towards strengthened resilience of human and natural systems in the Niger Basin to the impacts of climate variability and change and related risks. Without GCF investments that improve management capacity, there would be no sustainable development in the basin.

GCF will contribute to the achievement of Basin country commitments to the UNFCCC conventions—more specifically, the NDCs as committed under the Paris Agreement and the NAPs, NAPA, national climate change strategies, and the national adaptation plans. The project will also support the implementation of the PIDACC in line with the basin countries’ national climate goals and help mainstream climate infrastructure needs into broader plan within the Niger Basin. By supporting the development of a sustainable financing mechanism for ecosystem services (PES) and by ensuring the contribution of GCF is also essential for the development of a sustainable funding mechanism for the PES to preserve natural resources.

# D2.Exit Strategy

The approach for sustainability is drawn on elements from the success of multi country climate adaptation programs like the Bank financed Silt Control project in the Sahelien Zone of the Niger River Basin (Burkina Faso, Mali and Niger) and community based management of water resources in the Upper Tana basin in Kenya. The strategy consists of three main pillars: (i) participatory approach—emphasizing demand driven interventions by the governments and the beneficiary communities to address their own needs; (ii) strengthening ownership, sustainability and replication of project results through 50% of the project costs respectively—which strengthens ownership, sustainability and replication and (iii) setting up community based infrastructure management committees, which are trained and equipped for B & M of infrastructure. Other dimensions include: (iv) building capacity to document and disseminate best practices and (v) setting up mechanisms for payment for Ecosystem services (PES) and other related payments. The project will also contribute towards the development of a sustainable financing mechanism for PES in the basin countries.

The Bank will ensure that agreements for loans and grants to the recipient agencies include commitments to an exit strategy which ensures the sustainability of project outcomes. Details of the elements are illustrated below.

### E.1.1 Expected assessment of impact potential

Specify the mitigation and/or adaptation impact, taking into account the relevant and applicable sub-criteria and assessment factors in the Fund's framework presentation.

The project/programme is expected to reduce vulnerability and shared prosperity in the Niger basin. Climate change projections point to higher temperatures, increased frequency and intensity of rainfall and associated flooding, along with more frequent and prolonged droughts. Climate variability, in particular, poses a significant risk to vulnerable communities. Projected impacts will be felt across the Niger basin countries (Igando and Ségou in particular).

Expected total number of direct and indirect beneficiaries: numerous beneficiaries relative to total population (PM-A Core 1), particularly the most vulnerable groups. The GCF-financed activities under PIDAC2 are expected to contribute directly to the achievement of the Fund’s objectives and results areas by reducing vulnerability, increasing resilience and potentially benefit 4 million people directly and 10 million indirectly (-50% female). The vulnerability of people in the Basin is exacerbated by political instability, notably recent conflicts in Mali and Niger. ## Conflicts over shared natural resources

Some of the biggest threats facing agro-pastoral systems that could undermine food security in the Niger Basin include herder-conflict and land tenure and rights. The pastoral systems are trans-boundary by nature, and pastoralists migrate across borders as a coping strategy against droughts and floods. However, the lack of harmonized policies on livestock movement, grazing areas, nomad range management and settlement systems have infirmed conflicts among pastoralists (like in Fulani) and between pastoralists and farmers. The conflict has led to the closure of some transhumance routes, the creation of other forms of instability, make traditional transhumance routes less accessible, marketing corridors more dangerous, and livestock production more risky. Strengthening resilience means working on a continuum of actions that combine protection of natural resources with sustainable livelihoods.

The lack of access to education, health services, credit facilities, land tenure, basic social services, unequal property rights and persistent gender gaps in the labour market and in the public sphere.

In addition to this, it should be noted that women have on increasing responsibilities from a number of standpoints. The increased role played by women has also been reflected in increased power within the community, indeed, because they have only a subsidiary role in relation to household production and reproduction, with consequences for household food security and nutritional well-being. Investments are required to ensure that women’s access to productive resources is not limited by present formidable barriers to women wanting to adopt new technologies and practices and actively participate in markets.

Sources of data for feeding the M&E system will include: (a) administrative data collected through the project MIS, such as programmatic and financial reports; (b) specially designed qualitative and quantitative household survey instruments; (c) existing and newly collected geo-referenced data (including global datasets); and (d) scientifically collected baseline and follow-up health data.
