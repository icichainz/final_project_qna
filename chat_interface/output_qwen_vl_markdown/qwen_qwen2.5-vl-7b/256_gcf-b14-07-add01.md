## A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

### 1. The melting of the Hindu Kush, Karakoram, and Himalayan glaciers in Northern Pakistan due to rising temperatures has created 3,044 glacial lakes in the federally-administered territory of Gilgit-Baltistan (GB) and the province of Khayber Pakhtunkhwa (KP). These lakes are at risk of breaching and forming Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs). Such outbursts have occurred in the past and when they do, millions of cubic metres of water and ice can be released from these lakes. The GLOF hazard is a significant threat to communities living downstream as it causes damage to livelihoods in some of the most remote areas of Pakistan. Currently 710,000 people remain at risk in GB and KP. Most recently, in July 2015, over 280,000 people in GB and KP were affected, a combination of heavy rains and GLOFs.

### 2. At present, the country faces a critical gap in technical and technological capacity to monitor the status of glaciers through hydrological monitoring and early warning systems (EWS) do not have the capacity to预警and respond to GLOF events caused by rising levels at the lakes. The project will address this issue and provide services to communities. The design and implementation of medium- and long-term disaster management policies and risk reduction measures are also critical in order to reduce the vulnerability of communities.

### 3. The Government of Pakistan has recognized the threat from GLOFs in its National Climate Change Policy and in its National Determined Contribution to monitor changes in glacier volumes and related GLOFs. The Government of Pakistan is committed to implementing a range of interventions to protect communities living downstream of glacial infrastructure to protect communities from GLOF risks. The interventions proposed for scale up by this project will be integrated into the national disaster risk management framework, including the development of flood warning systems and engineering structures (i.e. gaiton walls) have been constructed; automatic weather stations, rain gauge and discharge measurement equipment are also being installed.

### 4. As the project is focused on delivering a public good, the Government of Pakistan is requesting 100% borrowing from the Green Climate Fund. The project will be implemented in Gilgit-Baltistan (GB) where over half of the rural population and 18 per cent of urban households below the poverty line. In Khayber Pakhtunkhwa, the poverty rate is 25%. The project will also benefit the people living downstream from the GLOF hazard. The Government of Pakistan needs financial support from international donors. Flood hazards are already greater than what national public finance can manage. During the project preparation phase, communities including civil society in the targeted districts were fully consulted and the GBH has issued a Letter of No Objection.

# C.1. Strategic Context

## Institutional and Political Context

It is important to note that Pakistan’s Economic Growth (2011) developed by the Ministry of Planning, Development and Reforms, highlights the role of climate change in its growth model, proposing a “climate-proof economic growth from the impacts of climate change, paying particular attention to the agricultural, water and energy sectors.” Enabling climateresilient development is one of the key objectives of Pakistan’s National Climate Change Policy (2013). The policy also calls for delivering these policy objectives. Though Pakistan has undertaken efforts to advance policy and actions on climate change, there are gaps in implementation at both federal and provincial levels. This is due to a lack of institutional capacity and coordination between line ministries at both the federal and provincial levels. To this end, the proposed project will support strengthening the architecture for delivering climate change adaptation policies and actions.

### Key Reforms

9. A major change in governance took place in 2010 in the form of the 18th Amendment to the Constitution of Pakistan, which mandated the devolution of 47 federal items to the provincial level, including “environmental pollution and ecology.” The Ministry of Climate Change (MCC) was established as a separate ministry under the 18th Amendment. Prior to this reform, governance through the Ministry of Environment (now, Ministry of Climate Change [MCC]) has taken the lead on implementing climate change policies and actions at both federal and provincial levels. Under the 18th Amendment, the MCC maintains responsibility for national and international policy and obligations. The split responsibilities between the federal government and provinces have created challenges in delivering climate change adaptation policies and actions.

### Re-establishment of MCA

10. The re-establishment of the MCA as a ministerial entity in 2015, following its downgrading to a division in 2013, has given it greater prominence at the federal level. The MCA is now responsible for climate change and environmental protection functions. The National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) is an attached department of the MCC and administratively works under the Ministry. This administrative linkage between climate and disaster management means, better coordination in terms of policy development and implementation. The NDMA has been managing all natural disasters and extreme weather events since 2013. Moreover, the Ministry has been managing other climate change and environmental projects and their capacity has been built up over time. The re-establishment of MCA as a ministerial entity will further strengthen the capacity of the Ministry and take adaptation to climate change into livelihood level in the provinces.

### National Climate Change Policy

11. The National Climate Change Policy (NCCP), approved by the Cabinet in 2012 and central to the MCC, focuses on adaptation, in light of Pakistan’s vulnerability to extreme weather events and other adverse impacts of climate change. The NCCP is a living document that has been updated several times since its approval. A particularly relevant aspect of the NCCP is the recommendation for the development of plans of action by the federal and provincial governments. The MCC has developed a National Climate Change Action Plan (NCCAP) in 2013, which is a provincial and local adaptation action plan, but this has not been the case so far. Further, because of the devolution following the 18th Amendment, the provinces have not implemented the federal-level NCCP at the sub-national level in Pakistan.

### Policy and Institutional Setup at KP Provinces

12. The Environmental Protection Act (EPA) of 2014 is an attached entity of the provincial Environment Department, policy formulation on climate change in KP is under the purview of the EPA’s Climate Change Division. The division formulates strategies and actions. The KP Integrated Development Strategy and the KP Green Growth Initiative help link the environment with development planning at both federal and provincial levels. The KP Environmental Protection Act includes a decision support process for strategic environmental assessments (SEAs) for certain areas. While the SEA is not mandatory, it is recommended that the Provincial Environmental Protection Council notification may be used as a vehicle to make climate change explicit in the SEAs.

### D.1 Value Added for GCF Involvement

**98.** With a debt of about 62% of its GDP (IMF, 2015), Pakistan is among the most indebted countries in the world. The country has been ranked as the second most vulnerable to climate change by the Climate Risk Index but has upgraded to B3 which shows an improvement in the government effectiveness. This very high level of indebtedness prevents the government from borrowing to invest in climate change adaptation.

However, Pakistan is also one of the countries where the occurrence of GLOFs in Pakistan is made manifest following the impacts of the previous floods and the obvious lack of preparedness of the affected populations. Indeed, urgent action is required to address this issue. The country has a very high vulnerability index (Vulnerability Index mainly invested in disaster recovery rather than prevention). Pakistan, therefore, needs GCF funding to operate a framework for early warning systems and to provide financial support to communities most vulnerable facing the consequences of GLOFs. This investment is urgent considering the expected increasing occurrence of these disasters.

**91.** The intervention aims to reduce the vulnerability of the local community to climate-induced changes (especially the threat of glacial lake outburst floods) thus reduces the financial stress on government coffers in terms of the financial burden of disaster response and reconstruction. This will also provide a long-term economic benefit through return for the Government of Pakistan or the implementing agency. In addition, GCF funding will not further indebted the country but rather will help to reduce the debt burden by providing an alternative source of financing that can stimulate local economy in the medium to long term. The government of GB has estimated an annual savings of US$ 1.2 million from this intervention.

**92.** GCF funding will not crowd out local funding as the project was as proposed here, i.e., one that focuses on long run climate change adaptation, is not something that is traditionally financed by local private and public sector entities. This is a very important point to note because the GCF is not intended to finance projects that generate revenues (non-purely economic), rather economic benefits that do not accrue as revenues; GCF funding is necessary in this situation.

**93.** Finally, the risk premium has been quantified in this case. However, given that interventions have already been successfully piloted, the Government of Pakistan is certain of its ability to scale up (as proposed here) without significant additional risks. The pilot project was implemented by a local NGO and was funded through GCF resources; therefore, there is no risk premium in excess of what has been previously tolerated. Also, access to GCF fund gives the government the capacity to continue and scale up the public support that has been provided to the vulnerable communities in GB and KP.

**94.** GCF resources to address GLOFs not only impulsive sustainable development for communities in rural areas, more importantly send a strong political signal in other GLOF-prone countries in the Himalayas and Arges that replicate this model of intervention. This will also provide an opportunity to scale up the intervention in Pakistan. In addition, all the information and experiences generated will provide knowledge and best practices to other communities in GLOF-prone areas.
