## In Senegal, agriculture’s economic viability is heavily contingent on climatic conditions, with decreasing rainfall and rising temperatures posing significant challenges.

Despite agriculture's substantial contribution to the Gross Domestic Product and its role as a primary source of livelihood for much of the population, smallholder farmers face numerous constraints in accessing credit and other financial services while developing agricultural value chains and enhancing the resilience of the food system. This challenge is exacerbated by limited access to climate-smart agricultural (CSA) practices, which are crucial for increasing productivity and improving adaptation to climate change.

The Green Finance Facility aims to tackle the increasing need for concessional finance to bolster climate smart agriculture in Senegal through a dedicated line of credit for Climate-Smart Agriculture (CSA) investments. This initiative is supported by La Banque Agricole (LBA) and BaoBab AgriFinance.

### Key Objectives:

- Establishment of a dedicated line of credit for Climate-Smart Agriculture (CSA) Investments, facilitated by La Banque Agricole (LBA) and BaoBab AgriFinance.
- Improvement in access to finance for micro, small, and medium enterprises (MSMEs) engaged in agriculture.
- Promotion of the adoption and scaling up of CSA practices, with a focus on reducing greenhouse gas emissions and enhancing climate resilience.
- Establishment of an enabling environment for CSA through knowledge sharing, learning successes, lessons learned, and best practices in CSA, fostering an enabling environment for sustainable agriculture.

### Climate Results:

The Programme aims to achieve significant outcomes:
- Reduced Emissions and Enhanced Resilience: By promoting low-emission technologies and climate-smart practices including the use of renewable energy (primarily biogas), the Programme seeks to reduce greenhouse gas emissions while enhancing resilience against the adverse impacts of climate change.
- Increased Access to finance, financial inclusion, and improved risk management in agricultural lending. The Programme seeks to improve access to finance by providing a concessional line of credit for CSA investments, including through traditional at the bank branch, or digitally via the digital tools the Programme seeks to develop.
- Strengthened policy frameworks, knowledge dissemination, and enhanced institutional capacity to support the adoption of CSA. This will encompass creating an enabling environment and dissemination platform for CSA.

### Benefits of the Programme:

- Job creation: By engaging MSMEs, service providers, asset financiers, and large corporates in programme activities is expected to drive job creation within the financial sector.
- Improved livelihoods: The anticipated increase in productivity from the adoption of CSA practices is anticipated to enhance food security by boosting agricultural productivity and household incomes, particularly for women smallholder farmers and women-led MSMEs.
- Increased access to climate finance for women smallholder farmers and women-led MSMEs. The Programme will include a dedicated financial assistance component that targets women, increasing their capacity in accessing financial products.

The Green Finance Facility for Climate-Smart Agriculture in Senegal addresses the dual challenges posed by climate change and limited agricultural financing. Through innovative financial mechanisms, technical assistance, and knowledge sharing, it aims to promote the adoption of CSA practices, foster resilience, and advance sustainable development amidst climate uncertainties.

### B.1. Context (max. 1000 words, approximately 2 pages)

#### 1. Senegal
- **Location**: Senegal is located on the west coast of the African continent.
- **Area and Population**: It spans a total area of 196,722 square kilometers and has an estimated population of 18,126,390 (2023).
- **Development Index**: With a Human Development Index (HDI) of 0.517 (2022), it is ranked among the least developed countries.
- **Economic Structure**:
  - Agriculture accounts for about 44% of Senegal's GDP, with livestock contributing approximately 17% and fishing around 3%.
  - The agricultural sector employs over 65% of the population, making it a significant part of the economy.

#### 2. Climate Change
- **Agriculture**: Agriculture is crucial to Senegal’s economy and employment but is increasingly threatened by climate change.
- **Climate Risks**:
  - The study uses a combination of observational data, reanalysis data, and Global Climate Model (GCM) outputs from the NASA Earth Exchange Global Downscaled Climate Projections.
  - Key climate parameters assessed include temperature and precipitation trends.

#### 3. Observed and Projected Impacts of Climate Change
- **Observed Climate Trends**:
  - Increased rainfall in June-August over the Casamance region since 1980.
  - Higher maximum temperature values observed annually from May to September, especially in the Ferlo regions.

- **Projected Climate Trends** (2025–2044 and 2045–2064):
  - Temperature increase of about 0.37°C per decade in the Niayes region.
  - Precipitation decrease by approximately 18% over the same period.

#### 4. Climate Change Impacts on Agriculture
- **Agricultural Activities**:
  - Senegal’s economy is heavily dependent on agriculture, which employs a large portion of its workforce and contributes significantly to GDP.
  - The sector is highly vulnerable to weather shocks due to climate change, affecting crop yields and livestock production.

#### 5. Conclusion
- Climate change poses significant challenges for Senegal's agricultural sector, impacting food security and economic stability.

- Invest in irrigation infrastructure and techniques to ensure adequate water supply for crops during periods of water stress caused by climate change.

- Research and select varieties that are more resilient to the anticipated changes to temperature, rainfall patterns, and soil conditions.

19. Smallholder farmers in Senegal face increasing challenges due to the uncertainty and variability of weather patterns driven by climate change (i). With most crops being rainfed, yields are heavily dependent on rainfall. In recent years, the frequency and intensity of heavy rains during the peak period of the rainy season have become increasingly unpredictable, and access to irrigation remains limited. Currently, only 10% of Senegal's irrigated land is used for agriculture (ii). The majority of irrigation potential are utilized and equipped for irrigation (iii). Barriers to implementing adaptation strategies include lack of information on climate change and credit, insufficient market support, insecure land tenure, and a lack of productive resources.

20. Even with substantial food assistance efforts over the past decades, food security continues to be a major challenge in Senegal. The country is particularly vulnerable due to its high population density, livelihoods, exacerbating environmental degradation, poverty, and food insecurity (iv). Given Senegal's high exposure and vulnerability to climate change, and in response to the Paris Agreement, the country is implementing several strategies aimed at improving resilience of smallholder farmers to climate change agriculture (v). For example, the Senegalese government initiated the Senegal Emerging Plan (PSE) in 2014. The PSE aims to improve food security by increasing agricultural productivity and promoting development policies to bolster the resilience of the country's production systems against the impacts of climate change. Furthermore, Senegal has recently completed sector-specific National Adaptation Plans (NAPs) for agriculture, forestry, fisheries, and water resources in order to reduce vulnerability and enhance health.

21. Senegambia is working to build economic resilience in the agriculture and livestock sectors to mitigate the impact of climate shocks and stresses on communities while increasing revenues and safeguarding livelihoods(vi). Over the past seven years, USAID, in collaboration with IRRI/Columbia University, has assisted smallholder farmers by providing training workshops, establishing farmer field schools, and disseminating weather and climate information services (WCIS) to bolster the resilience of farmers, fishers, and pastoralists. The WCIS initiative is supported through a multi-stakeholder partnership that increased investment in the Weather and Climate Services (WCS) value chain, expanding access to pay-for-service WCS products, and advancing work on hydrometeorological services for flood control and climate change adaptation. In addition, USAID has provided support under the Feed the Future initiative, smallholders in Senegal are supported in high-potential horticulture and small ruminant production systems through the development of a value chain approach to agricultural extension that includes scaling up of the WCIS, developing index insurance products, and implementing ICT-enabled farmer subscription systems linked to mobile money payments and contracting models that incorporate enhanced climate risk mitigation through financial mechanisms.

## Risks and barriers analysis:

The proposed Programme aims to overcome significant risks that currently hinder the scale-up of CSA practices and in Senegal. These represent significant obstacles to achieving sustainable agricultural development and climate change resilience in the country.

### Economic risks may limit the ability of financial institutions to offer concessional credit:
- The Senegalese economy continues to face headwinds as it recovers from the COVID-19 pandemic.
- The country is also facing geopolitical tensions, including (i) the ongoing conflict between Russia and Ukraine; (ii) the imposition of restrictions on rice exports by India; (iii) the appreciation of the US dollar against the Senegalese CFA franc; and (iv) the global food price crisis, which have raised concerns about potential constraints on funding availability.
- Instability in some neighboring countries has created additional uncertainty and weighed on economic activity.

### The prevalent perception of high financial risks associated with lending to the agricultural sector among financial institutions:
- This perception is based on the historical experience that Senegal has had with low agricultural yields and income, which LFs view as risky propositions.
- This perception limits their willingness to lend to farmers and hinders investment in CSA technologies and practices.

### The challenging risk-return profile of agricultural lending worsens the situation:
- High borrowing costs and short-term loans discourage both lenders and borrowers from participating in agricultural activities that require long-term investments, such as CSA technologies and practices.
- The existing financial environment and offerings (high capital costs and interest rate, short maturity of credit, and collateral requirements) create barriers that hinder substantial investment in agricultural resilience.

### Operational challenges further compound these issues:
- Particularly the Additional high transaction costs of implementing CSA technologies and practices.
- High transaction costs and regulatory burdens restrict access to essential funding needed to implement CSA technologies and practices effectively.

### A critical barrier is the lack of understanding regarding climate risks and vulnerabilities among farmers, as well as a general lack of awareness about smart strategies for reducing risk exposure and the adoption of CSA practices that could enhance agricultural resilience.
- Without comprehensive awareness campaigns, it is difficult to adopt smart strategies, reduce risk exposure, and address climate challenges effectively.

### Institutional weaknesses and policy gaps:
- There is a general lack of coordination among key stakeholders in Senegal, such as the national government ministries and civil society organizations. This often leads to fragmented implementation efforts, duplicative activities, and inefficiencies.

## I. Assessment of the Independent Technical Advisory Panel

### 1.1 Overview

1. **Agriculture is central to Senegal's economy, providing livelihoods for 70 per cent of the population. However, Senegal’s reliance on food imports leaves the country vulnerable to food security risks, exacerbated by climate change. The GCF facility for Fostering Climate-Smart Agriculture (GCF) aims to address these challenges by increasing agricultural productivity, building resilience to climate impacts and reducing greenhouse gas emissions through the adoption of climate-smart agriculture practices.**

2. **The project will support smallholder farmers in Senegal, who will receive approximately twenty-five thousand loans for crop production, livestock rearing, and post-harvest management. These loans are expected to be provided by micro, small and medium-sized enterprises (MSMEs), service providers, financial and larger agricultural institutions.**

3. **Strategies for crop production, agricultural leasing and post-harvest management; the introduction of agroforestry practices and forage crop cultivation in order to improve livestock feed quality; and the promotion of energy-efficient stoves to convert agricultural waste into bio-energy, offering a sustainable energy source.**

4. **The programme’s accredited entity (AE), La Banque Agricole (LBA), is a first-time direct access entity that will support Senegal's agricultural sector. Majority government owned, LBA is a leading provider of agricultural working capital with extensive experience in call-loan facilities and microfinance.**

5. **GCF is structured as a EUR 39.7 million local currency on-lending facility. GCF is expected to provide EUR 18.7 million in concessional loans, matched by EUR 15 million in funds from LMA (LBA Microfinance Agency) and EUR 6 million in co-financing for the project loan facilitated by the Government of Senegal. Additionally, GCF will contribute EUR 4.7 million in grants to support the development of a climate-smart agriculture strategy and policy dialogue. LBA will provide EUR 8 million in-kind for project management costs under this facility.**

5. As part of GCF’s LBA will allocate EUR 4.5 million of GCF loan proceeds to Banque Microfinancière Sénégal, a leading microfinance institution. Banque Microfinancière Sénégal will extend credit to underserved borrowers, complementing the traditional banking channels of LBA. Banque Microfinancière Sénégal is expected to mobilize EUR 10 million in additional funds raised through a local currency green bond (totalling EUR 30.5 million) listed on the Abidjan stock exchange.

The overarching strategy of GCF aligns with Senegal’s nationally determined contribution (NDC) by addressing financing barriers that hinder CSA adoption, particularly among MMEI, cooperatives and smallholder farmers. Key objectives include:
(a) Promoting CSA practices through concessional credit facilities;

(d) Incorporating climate risk appraisal into lending operations using tools such as climate risk assessment and early warning mechanisms;

(f) Strengthening institutional frameworks, building institutional capacity and expanding knowledge dissemination in order to enable CSA scale.

### 1.2 Impact potential

The GCF-funded proposal projects that the programme will eliminate 3.79 million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent of greenhouse gas emissions over its lifetime while benefiting more than 680,000 people. These outcomes are to be achieved through the integration of climate-smart agriculture (CSA) practices into existing agricultural systems in agriculture, improved livestock management and the implementation of sustainable land-use strategies.

9. From an adaptation standpoint, GCF intends to promote CSA solutions such as drip irrigation systems, climate-resilient seed varieties and agrotechnology farmers, enabling them to better adapt their farming practices to changing climatic conditions while maintaining stable agricultural productivity. The programme places a particular emphasis on supporting vulnerable groups, including women, youth and smallholder farmers with adaptive capacities of these vulnerable groups and address their socioeconomic vulnerabilities.

10. Advancing CSA practices is a priority for Senegal, a least developed country in which agriculture accounts for approximately 25 per cent of the gross domestic product (GDP) and provides employment for a significant majority of its population. The agricultural sector is highly dependent on rainfed systems susceptible to climate change, which threaten both food security and economic stability.

11. Transitioning to a resilient and low-emission agricultural sector offers important opportunities to drive economic growth and improve food security. The adoption of CSA

## Needs of the recipient Scale: High

Senegal faces significant vulnerability to climate change impacts, particularly in the form of droughts and floods. The country’s NDC sets ambitious targets for addressing challenges in agriculture and water resources, identified as the two most at-risk sectors. Despite the urgency, the agricultural sector remains underfunded.

### Human Development Index (HDI)

With a Human Development Index of 0.517 or 82nd place, Senegal ranks among the world’s least developed countries, at 166th out of 189 nations. This reflects persistently low levels of human development and poverty. The country is one of the most vulnerable to climate change; over half of the population is classified as multidimensionally poor. From 2014 to 2018, around 1.8 million Senegalese were affected by droughts, with 35 per cent of the population in need of food assistance.

### Agricultural Sector

The agricultural sector employs over half of the population and contributes 17 per cent to GDP, of which 36 per cent comes from livestock farming. However, only about one-third of arable land is irrigated, and less than a quarter is classified as arable. The sector is dominated by small family farms, which make up 95 per cent of agricultural holdings.

### Challenges in Agriculture

Agriculture is the backbone of Senegal’s economy, yet it is critically underfunded. Despite contributing over 15 per cent to GDP, the sector receives just 3 per cent of commercial bank lending and only 0.26 per cent of rural residents access microfinance. These vulnerabilities make climate change a high-risk sector for financial institutions.

The program aims to improve agricultural productivity by increasing access to credit for CSA investments, targeting various segments of the agriculture value chain.


### Agricultural Sector’s Reliance on Rainfed Crops

The reliance on rainfed crops makes it particularly susceptible to climate change impacts. Limited rainfall, prolonged dry seasons, and limited access to irrigation exacerbate these vulnerabilities. Currently, only 2.1 per cent of commercial bank lending is directed towards the agricultural sector.

### Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW)

The Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) has been adopted in 1979 by the United Nations General Assembly and is often described as an international bill of rights for women. Consisting of a preamble and 30 articles, it sets out what constitutes discrimination against women and sets up an agenda for national action to end such discrimination.

By accepting the Convention, Senegal commits to undertake a series of measures to end discrimination against women in all forms, including:

- Incorporate the principle of equality of men and women in their legal system.
- Abolish all discriminatory laws and adopt appropriate ones prohibiting discrimination against women.
- Establish tribunals and other public institutions to ensure the effective protection of women against discrimination; and
- Ensure the elimination of all acts of discrimination against women by persons, organizations, or enterprises.

Senegal ratified CEDAW in 1985, and the Optional Protocol on violence against women in 2000. It is also committed to submit national reports at least every four years, on measures it has taken to comply with treaty obligations. Senegal submitted its last report in 2019[1].

### Introduction

Agriculture remains a cornerstone of Senegal’s economy. Senegal's agricultural landscape is characterized by its critical contribution to the national economy; it represents approximately 17% of the country's GDP (20), making it one of the most important sectors in terms of economic impact. Additionally, agriculture is the primary source of employment for about 70% of the population, further emphasizing its importance in the daily lives of Senegalese citizens. However, within this sector, there exists a complex interplay of gender roles and dynamics that significantly influence the agricultural landscape.

The agricultural landscape in Senegal is characterized by rain-fed and seasonal practices both cash crops and subsistence farming. One of the defining features of Senegal's agricultural sector is its diversity. It encompasses various activities, including crop cultivation, livestock farming, and fishing. These activities collectively contribute to the production of essential food items, raw materials for industries, and export commodities.

Senegal’s agriculture is deeply rooted in traditional norms and practices, which have assigned specific roles to men and women within agricultural activities. Traditionally, farmers' fields have been divided along gender lines, with men primarily responsible for land preparation, plowing, and certain cash crops, while women have played a central role in subsistence farming, food crop cultivation, and processing of agricultural products.

Women's contributions to Senegal’s agriculture are substantial and often underrecognized. They play a vital role in food production, with women contributing to approximately 50% of the country’s food production. This includes cultivation of staple crops like millet, maize, and groundnuts, as well as the management of small livestock. Women are also actively involved in post-harvest activities such as food processing and marketing.

The gender dynamics within Senegalese agriculture have significant implications for food security in the country. Given that women are responsible for a substantial portion of food production, their access to resources and support directly affects food availability and affordability. Therefore, addressing gender disparities in agriculture is not only a matter of gender equality but also a critical component of broader food security efforts.

# Conclusion

The gender assessment report provides a comprehensive overview of the challenges, initiatives, and interventions shaping gender dynamics in Senegal’s agricultural sector. It is evident that while progress has been made, significant gender disparities persist, hindering the full potential of women in agriculture and jeopardizing food security.

Land tenure reforms, financial inclusion programs, and gender-responsive policies are commendable steps taken by both the government and non-governmental organizations. These initiatives aim to empower women, enhance their economic independence, and address historical disparities in land ownership. However, effective implementation and impact assessment remain ongoing challenges.

International support and funding from organizations like the United Nations underscore the global recognition of Senegal’s commitment to climate change adaptation, food security, and gender equality. Yet, the need for improved gender-disaggregated data and ongoing research is emphasized to ensure the targeted success of these initiatives.

Climate-smart agriculture (CSA) practices emerge as a promising avenue for advancing gender equality in Senegalese agriculture through equitable access to production bases. A gender-responsive approach to CSA, encompassing women’s leadership, training, and improved access to resources, can significantly contribute to sustainable development for female farmers. The potential economic, environmental, and social benefits of sustainable agriculture practices are highlighted, particularly in promoting diverse and nutritious food access, preserving biodiversity, and building community resilience.

However, the report underscores the existing gender data gaps, revealing the inadequacy in monitoring key SDGs from a gender perspective. Achieving gender-related SDG commitments requires closing these gaps in areas such as unpaid care work, the gender pay gap, women’s representation in leadership roles, and access to land.

In conclusion, the path to gender equality in Senegalese agriculture demands continued commitment, collaboration, and a steadfast focus on closing data gaps. The integration of gender-responsive approaches in policies and practices, coupled with robust monitoring and evaluation mechanisms, will be instrumental in realizing the vision of inclusive, resilient, and sustainable agricultural development in Senegal.
