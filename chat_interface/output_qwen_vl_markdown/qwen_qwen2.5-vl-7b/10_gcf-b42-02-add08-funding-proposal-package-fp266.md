## B.1 Climate Change (see pages 108-115 and annex approximately two pages)

Climate change is profoundly impacting Mauritian communities, particularly those living along the Sahel-Saharan boundary that extends from the northwest to the southeast of the country. A noticeable impact of climate change includes increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events such as heavy rainfall, flooding, and droughts. These phenomena are becoming increasingly soiled as a result of sand inundation, reducing access to and the availability of already strained water resources. The impacts on agriculture have been significant, with changes in precipitation patterns affecting both the agricultural (see Figure 27 in Annex 2) and livestock practices in the oasis. In addition, sand inundation threatens coastal areas and the livelihoods of communities living along these coasts. In some cases, entire villages or even dunes have disappeared entirely within villages, in some cases only once (see Figure 15 in Annex 3). The ongoing decline in water resources has also led to a decrease in agricultural production, particularly in the oasis region known as the transhumance²⁰. Combined with the climate change-induced loss of vegetation and pasture, water scarcity has contributed to the migration of communities from rural areas to urban areas where access to clean drinking water is more reliable. However, sedimentation increases pressure on limited resources, creating a source of conflict – particularly between pastoralists and farmers.

Four regional hubs have been identified along the boundary where desertification processes are most severe and the need for adaptation is greatest: these hubs are: A) in the wadiya of Abidj; B) in the wadiya of Tagant; C) in the oasis region known as the transhumance²⁰; D) in the oasis region known as the transhumance²¹. Each hub covers an area of 10,000 km², spans several administrative regions, and is characterized by rural households living under similar climatic conditions. The four hubs are: Abidj, Tagant, Tamechet and Nema. Abidj is surrounded by rural households with a total population of approximately 25,000 people; Tagant has around 13,000 people; Tamechet has around 48,000 people; and Nema has around 43,000 inhabitants²¹. Nema hub encompasses ten communes with a total population of approximately 25,000.

## Climate science basis³

### Observed climate change

Observations of historical climate trends show that the mean annual temperature in Mauritania has increased by 0.7°C per decade since 1961. The most significant increase is observed across all major climatic zones in the country; temperature increases have been greatest over the Sahara Desert and Sahel-Saharan boundary, where temperatures have risen by approximately 2.5°C on average (see Table 34 in Annex 2 for year weather data since 1981-2010) indicate that, in general, temperatures have also increased with the focal hubs of Abidj, Tagant, Tamechet and Nema (see Table 27 in Annex 2 for observed temperature changes in the four focal hubs).

In addition to shifting temperatures, climate change in Mauritania has induced: i) an increase in the frequency of extreme rainfall events; ii) a decrease in the number of days with precipitation across all major climatic zones (see Table 32 in Annex 2 for observed precipitation trends in the four focal hubs). Consequently, while heavy rainfall events have become more frequent and intense, the overall amount of rainfall has decreased. This shift has resulted in increased rainfall irregularity and prolonged dry spells across the region³¹. Precipitation has declined by approximately 10% since 1981-2010 (see Table 34 in Annex 2 for observed precipitation trends). The hub under discussion has a small but statistically significant increase in its overall precipitation. Despite the overall decline in rainfall, there is an increase in extreme daily rainfall, which often results in flash floods, as been observed in all four hubs over both time frames.

### Projected climate changes

All four target hubs are projected to experience an increase in both minimum and maximum temperatures under future RCP4.5 (intermediate) and RCP8.5 (high) greenhouse gas emissions scenarios, with larger temperature increases projected for RCP8.5 than RCP4.5. The projected increase in mean annual precipitation is expected to be small or negligible across all four hubs under both the RCP4.5 and RCP8.5 emission scenarios.

With increasing temperatures, the frequency of heat waves is predicted to increase across all four target hubs by 2050 (but not beyond). Similarly, the number of days with heavy precipitation is expected to increase in all four hubs during this decade are presented in Table B-6 in Annex 2.

The projected evolution of precipitation trends varies along hubs. For example, no significant change in precipitation is expected for Namaqua under both RCP4.5 and RCP8.5 emission scenarios. In contrast, a decrease is expected to significantly decrease in Rachel, Tomakomai and Nekka, relative to the reference period (1981–2010). Similarly, an increase in precipitation is projected for all four hubs under both RCP4.5 and RCP8.5 emission scenarios compared with the reference period. However, by 2050, mean annual precipitation is expected to decrease in Rachel, Tomakomai and Nekka, relative to the reference period.

In line with increasing hub-level temperature trends and decreasing precipitation potentials, potential evapotranspiration (ETP) is projected to increase across all four hubs by 2050 under both the RCP4.5 and RCP8.5 emission scenarios (see Table B-7 in Annex 2). The projected increase of ETP for Namaqua is expected to be small or negligible, while that for all other hubs is also projected to increase significantly (p<0.1), by 35% as shown in Table B-7, Annex 2.

The combination of projected temperature rise, ETI and declining rainfall will increase the severity and extent of droughts across all four target hubs under both RCP4.5 and RCP8.5 emission scenarios. The frequency of dry spells is predicted to be between 210 and 230, compared to the historical value of 216 days in 1995. Moreover, by 2100, annual rainfall will decrease from its current level of approximately 700 millimeters (mm) across all four hubs.

Another indicator of future drought conditions: the Standardized Precipitation-Evaporation Index (SPEI). The SPEI is a standardized index that combines precipitation and evapotranspiration to assess water availability. Pathways S3/S4 scenarios predict SPEI values below zero in the hub, indicating an increase in dryness by 2100 compared with current conditions.

Moreover, by 2100, 90% of the country will experience a decrease in near-surface wind speed, signifying an increasing in extreme dry years.

# Problem statement

Climate change will continue to exacerbate the drivers of desertification in Mauritania, threatening the structural integrity of the Sahara Desert and the natural resource-based livelihoods of rural communities. Drivers of desertification include changes in the spatial and temporal patterns of rainfall and increases in temperatures and ET that, when combined with human activities such as overgrazing, deforestation, and inappropriate land use practices, accelerate desertification processes; and iv) siltation of dams, boreholes, wells and rivers, which reduces the accessibility and availability of water resources. The impacts of climate change on desertification are already being felt in Mauritania: these impacts, increasing frequency, severity and time span of droughts are particularly damaging, as they already compromise the livelihoods of poor communities and a significant share of the rural population (see Figure 140 in Annex 2).

Without urgent intervention, these processes will worsen in the future. The Sahara Desert is expected to spread southward by about 50 km over the next century, with associated impacts on desertification, water resources, food security and coordination capacity to slow the rate of desertification along the Sahel-Saharan boundary and improve water security.

## Preferred adaptation solution

An integrated multi-sectoral approach within the target hubs is the preferred option for responding to the impacts of climate-induced changed desertification from the four target hubs because it is cost-effective, easily implemented and has a high potential for co-benefits. The proposed project will support the implementation of this approach on the cases (for an example, see Figure 145 in Annex 2). This approach includes establishing 2,122 ha of green-grey dryland restoration sites, which are expected to provide livelihoods and income opportunities for communities living in degraded landscapes and enhance ecosystem services related to dune stabilisation and the supply of natural resources.

To support the adaptive capacity of the communities in the target hubs, the proposed project will develop or strengthen sustainable agriculture-based livelihoods. By providing an alternative source of income to current extraction-based livelihoods, this approach will reduce pressure on degraded landscapes and green-grey infrastructure approaches by mitigating the risk of communities resorting to maladaptive coping mechanisms.

## Barriers to the preferred solution

Barrier 1: Limited financial capacity to implement adaptation interventions at the community level. Implementing entities, as well as government and non-government stakeholders in Mauritania, have limited financial resources for implementing adaptation interventions at the community level within the four target hubs. Government officials at national-, regional- and local levels, as well as staff from crucial sectors at the national level (e.g., Ministry of Environment, Ministry of Agriculture), are often not aware or do not engage with stakeholders on the forefront of managing climate change impacts, such as community leaders, often have inadequate technical capacity to implement adaptation interventions, including decision-making or designing and implementing climate-resilient natural resource management and land use planning.

Barrier 2: Limited financial capacity to invest in climate-resilient land and natural resources management at the community level. Most urban centers in Mauritania are characterized by predominantly informal trade, limited infrastructure and public service coverage, high levels of self-employment and low human capital. These characteristics generally discourage private sector investment in climate-resilient land and natural resource management projects. The government of Mauritania (Gom) has introduced a number of regulatory and legislative reforms in an attempt to boost the private sector by providing incentives for private sector investment in sustainable development, but these reforms have not persisted, impeding local and foreign investment in sustainable development. These obstacles include: difficulty in accessing credit; lack of access to information on climate change impacts and adaptation options; low levels of technical and professional training; or limited governance within the financial services sector; and/or limited private sector capacity for long-term planning and investment.

Barrier 3: Limited knowledge of the climate change impacts, risks, vulnerabilities and adaptation options across the four target areas.

Communities in rural areas – particularly farmers and pastoralists – are not well informed about current and expected climate change impacts, risks and vulnerabilities related to water availability, agricultural production and other land use practices. This lack of awareness is compounded by a general low level of education among these communities and increasing climate resilience. To date, attempts to stabilise durums in the four target hubs have rarely been consistent, either because of poor knowledge or limited access to inputs such as seeds and fertilisers. As a result, national-level stakeholders seldom pass on information at regional or local levels. There is limited inter-sectoral collaboration and knowledge-sharing between ministries, departments and stakeholders.

Barrier 4: Limited integration of climate change adaptation into land use planning and natural resource management strategies at the regional and local levels

Although urban plans developed for Nerat and Rashid incorporate some measures for climate risk reduction, most rural areas in Mauritania are not covered by such plans. In addition, the importance of pastoralism is often overlooked. In the past, failure to recognise the importance of a participatory approach has resulted in weak coordination between different stakeholders involved in land use planning and natural resource management.

Land-use planning and natural resource management plans exist within the target region, pasture- and rangeland-management plans that consider climate change are not yet fully integrated into these plans. This is due to limited knowledge about climate change impacts on ecosystems and communities at local levels.

Project strategy for overcoming barriers to adaptation and achieving a paradigm shift in Mauritania

The goal of the proposed project is to enhance the resilience of communities and ecosystems in northern Mauritania against the adverse effects of climate change by integrating climate change considerations into land use planning, water resources management, climate-resilient agriculture and livelihood diversification in four rural-urban hubs. Aquit (a small town) and Hadi (a village) are located on the western coast of Mauritania; while Ksar (a small town) and Hodh (a Chingu village) is located to the east.

To ensure the success of this approach, the project will focus on two main outcomes:

Outcome 1. Increased implementation and upscaling of Ecosystem-based Adaptation (EbA) measures across the four target hubs in northern Mauritania by establishing a conducive enabling environment for EbA.

Outcome 2. Communities in four rural-urban hubs along the Sahara-Sahel boundary in Mauritania have increased their capacity to adapt to climate change through improved access to information, knowledge and natural resources management practices.

Outcomes 1 and 2 will be achieved through adaptation to established barriers by establishing communication platforms to share technical capacity; facilitate knowledge sharing on natural resource management and sustainable land-use planning among stakeholders at the regional, national and local levels. By creating and disseminating knowledge on EbA, Outcome 1 will contribute to addressing barrier 1 and barrier 3.

Under Output 2.1 - 2.123% of high-green infrastructure will be established across the four target hubs to facilitate the implementation of climate-resilient agriculture and livelihood diversification in communities through improved access to land and the supply of natural resources. Co-developing commune-level rehabilitation plans will support the integration of climate change considerations into land use planning at the regional, national and local levels.

Investment in climate-resilient land management will contribute to overcoming barriers 2 and 3.

Under Output 2.2, community-managed Water User Groups (WUGs) will be established. Commune-level water management plans will be developed with the participation of commune council members, agricultural extension officers and representatives from commune-level technical committees (CTCs) to practice climate-resilient crop farming in communities. This will contribute to overcoming barriers 2 and 3.

Output 2.3 will facilitate the adoption of climate-smart agricultural practices and sustainable diversified livelihoods for farmers and pastoralists through training, capacity building and knowledge sharing among commune council members, extension officers and representatives from commune-level technical committees (CTCs) to practice climate-resilient crop farming in communities. This will contribute to overcoming barriers 2 and 3.

Output 2.4 will provide climate-resilient seed varieties to newly established nurseries – for distribution to 500 farmers and sale

# G.2. Gender assessment and action plan (max. 500 words, approximately 1 page)

A gender analysis and Action Plan (AP) for this project provides an overview of gender-disaggregated challenges, specifically those related to i) women's rights; ii) the gendered division of labour; iii) political participation; iv) access to land; v) access to justice; vii) gender-based violence (GBV); and viii) climate change impacts.

Access to productive resources is a major challenge for women in Mauritius, as well as for other socio-economically marginalised groups in Mauritius as a result of unequal access to productive resources.105 Although the Community Empowerment and Development Assistance Fund (CEDAW)106 has been established since 2003 to assist economically disadvantaged women and other socio-economically marginalised groups, the GfM has not yet lifted restrictions applying to articles 13(a) and 16 of CEDAW, which relate to i) eliminating discrimination in family benefits; and ii) ensuring equality in marriage and family matters.

The Political Stability Council (PSC)107 was established by the Constitution of Mauritius (2004) as a body established in 2001, serving as the primary codified law governing marriage and family relations in Mauritius. The PSC provides a framework for the regulation of marriage and family relations, including divorce, adoption, guardianship, and heads of their respective households. However, ~50% of households in the focal hub of Aubejot, Raichur and Tamarind are headed by women, mainly resulting from divorces, widowedness, and male migration to urban areas for work.108 Statistics show that 62% of women in Mauritius are married or cohabiting with a partner. Additionally, PSC states that men hold decision-making authority, while women are expected to assist in managing the family. The PSC also recognises that women are more likely than men to be employed in low-paying jobs and informal occupations considered dangerous, arduous or immoral.109 Although the GfM has ratified International Labour Organization (ILO) Convention 189 on Domestic Workers' Rights, only ~35% of domestic workers in Mauritius participate remuneration rate are 31%, and they face a considerable gender pay gap.

Educational disparities exist, with lower primary school completion rates for girls (~56%) compared to boys (~44%). Women also have lower secondary school completion rates than men (70%) compared to men (78%). Gender disparities in access to finance limit women's capacities to start and expand their businesses. Women are less likely to be employed as managers, directors or owners of credit and register businesses; cultural norms and gender roles limit women's participation. Women also face time pressures due to the division of household responsibilities between men and women, reducing their time available to diversify their livelihoods or participate in projects designed to improve their resilience.

In accordance with the GCF Updated Gender Policy and Gender Action Plan 2020-2023 (GCP/GAP.2416), the proposed project will address gender equality by achieving its target figures by incorporating the following measures into activities designed under the proposed project:

* The establishment of a gender-balanced project implementation unit (PMU);
* The appointment of at least one female PMU member to support and monitor the implementation of the GAPP;
* Compulsory gender training for the PMU;
* The inclusion of gender considerations in all relevant consultancy and partnership TUs;
* The identification and strengthening of at least six women-led civil society organisations (CSOs), to assist with knowledge sharing, capacity building and advocacy.

1. Climate change risk and impacts. Observations of historical climate trends show that the mean annual temperature in Mauritania has increased by 0.3°C per decade over the past 70 years - from 26.1°C in 1950 to 28.46°C in 2020. In addition to shifting temperatures, climate change is also affecting precipitation patterns and frequency of extreme weather events (with associated flash floods, and a decline in the low-intensity, long-duration rainfall that infiltrates into the ground), increased rainfall irregularity and more prolonged dry spells.* Precipitation has decreased by 15% over the past century. The most significant decrease is observed for the Agadez hub, with a reduction of 20%. In contrast, the Tanezrouft hub has experienced a small but statistically significant increase in precipitation (about +3%). This increase is mainly due to an increase in one daily rainfall, which often results in flash floods. The observed potential evapotranspiration (PET) year-to-year variability is strongly correlated with the inter-annual variability of precipitation between precipitation and PET, indicating that water loss via PET exceeds water gained via precipitation. These trends are consistent across all four hubs and are also observed for the rest of Mauritania, including both the north-eastern trade winds and the Harmattan - a dry wind from the Sahara desert to mid-March. Together, these winds carry large amounts of dust and sand across the country.

# Adaptation Impact

The adaptation impact potential of the project is considerable, focusing on three key areas: (1) reducing vulnerability to drought and flood through Drought Zone 2.1; (2) improving water availability for rural communities in Drought Zones 3-5; and (3) enhancing resilience against climate change.

This will be achieved through enhanced ecosystem services generated by physical-dune fixation using a variety of indigenous species, including shrubs, grasses, trees, and other vegetation. These will reduce soil erosion, improve water infiltration rates, and increase the availability of groundwater for livestock watering points. The project will also introduce improved agricultural production and human respiratory health to sand encroachment and dust storms.

A set of greening-adaptation measures will be implemented in Drought Zones 2-5:

* (a) Groundwater dams: to improve aquifer recharge;
* (b) Gabions, dykes and stone walls: to reduce flood risk; and
* (c) Sand dune stabilization systems: to increase water retention and storage.

## Access to and control over resources

In Mauritania, considerable gender disparities exist in men's and women's access to and control over resources, particularly in the context of entrenched socio-cultural norms and legal frameworks. Women in rural areas have restricted access to land, water, and credit, despite legal provisions recognizing their rights.

## Gender-based violence (GBV)

Although there are no specific laws criminalizing domestic violence in Mauritania, the government has initiated several actions to address it. The government has developed a National Action Plan on Gender Based Violence (2014–2018) and a National Strategy for the Promotion of Women (2015–2019), both of which aim to combat GBV through various measures, including awareness-raising campaigns in cooperation with civil society and establishing family dispute units in all regional capitals. The government has also launched several awareness-raising campaigns in cooperation with civil society and established family dispute units in all regional capitals. However, there is a lack of comprehensive legal framework for addressing domestic violence, and there is limited police intervention on reported cases. Instead, women often seek help from their families or community leaders.

### Domestic violence

Domestic violence is a pervasive problem throughout the country. Under the Penal Code, rape is a criminal offense, with perpetrators subject to penalties ranging from forced labour to death by stoning (Art. 309). However, there are significant challenges in enforcing this law due to its strict interpretation and the omission of marital rape. In practice, rape cases often go unreported or are not prosecuted because victims fear social stigma, lack access to justice, or are intimidated by a judge, who may, under Sharia law, accuse the woman of adultery or sexual intercourse outside of marriage (Qadi, 2014). The government has enacted various awareness-raising campaigns to encourage women victims of rape to file a formal complaint with police. However, these campaigns have not significantly reduced the incidence of violence between rape victims and those who have committed rape; nonetheless, impunity for rape continues to be a problem in Mauritania.

To date, there is no law prohibiting sexual harassment, which occurs often, especially in the workplace and public spaces. The government has launched several awareness-raising campaigns on this issue but has not yet enacted specific legislation. Regarding female genital mutilation (FGM), there are no specific legislative prohibitions; however, the government has developed a National Strategy for Female Genital Mutilation in 2008 and a draft law prohibiting FGM by imprisonment as well as fines ranging from 120,000 to 360,000 CFA francs. The government has also launched several awareness-raising campaigns on this issue.

The local government continues to work with international organizations to address the health outcomes and social consequences surrounding the practice. However, there is no specific law criminalizing FGM in Mauritania; it was only prohibited by a draft bill criminalizing FGM that was submitted for approval of the National Assembly before the 2019 elections.

FGM is deeply rooted in Mauritanian society, particularly among ethnic groups and performed on young girls, sometimes even before birth. The practice is seen as a rite of passage or religious requirement, providing social acceptance or higher social recognition within communities, and increasing marriageability.

# I.13. Gender and Minorities

Minority groups in Mauritania include sub-Saharan ethnic groups such as Peuhl and Toucouleur. Some of these groups together make up 25% of the population, and Hararine who constitute 40% of the population. It is difficult to provide transparent data on the ethnic composition of the population since the government does not collect this information in its census or other official statistics.

In addition, the government considers that Hararines are part of the wider Moorish society, forming part of the majority group. The Moors (or Bedouins) represent 50% of the population and are divided into two groups: the Tukulor and the Soninke. The Tukulor are descendants of black ethnic groups who were captured by the white Moors in the 17th century, while the Soninke are descendants of the original inhabitants of Mauritania.

The Hararines are descendants of Black ethnic groups that were captured by the majority white Moors and enslaved. They were later freed from slavery but still retain their African cultural heritage. Slavery, and in particular slavery of Hararine women, underpinned by deep-seated discrimination as well as poverty, is a major issue for this group.

Mauritanian adults and children continue to be sold and bought as slaves; children are enslaved by their parents or guardians who sell them to other families or individuals. The sale of children often results in the complete dispossession of their ancestral property by their masters or masters of their parents upon their parents' death.

Although Arabic is the official language, some minorities speak Hausa—i.e., a dialect of Arabic (90%), Peuhl or Pular and Toucouleur (5%), Soninke (1%), and Wolof (2.0%). All minority groups in Mauritania share the same religion: Sunni Islam.

Both men and women receive training on gender-sensitive approaches, including understanding the dynamics of cultural settings and power relations. The project recognises the intersectional systems of advantage and disadvantage that shape access to resources, opportunities, and decision-making processes and spaces. Women's needs, concerns, and expertise frequently become marginalised in development projects and are often not adequately represented or considered. Specifically, the project will actively involve women in activities, ensuring that their needs, insights, and interests are represented throughout all gender-sensitive communication materials in terms of content, design, and dissemination.

### 2.3.3 Project expected outcomes—gender equality

The project will provide tangible benefits for women and promote a shift in gender dynamics by adopting a gender-sensitive and responsive strategy. Involving women in various decision-making processes is necessary to ensure that their needs are met and that the project’s activities are sustainable over time. The following steps and activities to ensure women's participation in decision-making processes and project activities:

By building on existing structures, such as Water User Groups (WUGs), women will be empowered to make decisions at both household and community levels. This impact is anticipated to continue beyond the project's lifespan.

Women’s access to natural resources will fulfill their numerous economic and domestic responsibilities. Both men and women engage in agricultural activities; by ensuring women have access to agricultural training, they can better manage their land and livestock. Women’s participation in decision-making processes to present and future climate change impacts will be strengthened. Moreover, by targeting 30–50% of women in WUGs, the project will ensure that women are involved in all aspects of water resource management. Their economic empowerment and adaptive capacity will be enhanced. Land use planning in the target hub communes will be improved to ensure that women have access to land for agricultural production. Better managed natural resources which will substantially reduce women’s work burden as less time will be spent on water collection, firewood gathering, and other domestic chores. Improved water resource management—including water source conservation and the establishment of water management infrastructure—will improve access to water for domestic and productive purposes, easing the load on women who are responsible for collecting water from distant sources. The distance that women travel will also be reduced as they will no longer be compelled to travel far from their households in search of natural resources such as water and wood.
