A.21. Executive summary (max. 750 words, approximately 1.5 pages)

The energy sector contributes to 35% of Ghana's GHG emissions yet the country's RE installed capacity currently stands below 20% of its 2030 target. The proposed Program aims to mobilize concessional financing from the GCF to support the Government in scaling up renewable energy (RE) and expanding access to this technology for Micro, Small & Medium Enterprises (MSMEs) and Households, to contribute to the reduction of Ghana's Green House Gas (GHG) emissions and the creation of a more favourable environment for sustainable energy financing over the long term.

The expected total cost of the ASAP program is USD37.57 million. By offering concessional financing terms, the ASAP program aims to provide a financial incentive in the industry within the country, which will not only improve the heavily fossil-fuel reliant energy mix but also help to increase the share of renewable energy by 10% during the 25-year life of the solar systems installed.

The loan can be used for the purchase and installation of distributed Solar PV Systems. The bank can broaden the impact of ASAP by reaching untapped market segments, which were unable to access financing in the past due to lack of collateral or lack of awareness. This will enable it to meet stringent collateral requirements of commercial banks, or for lack of awareness about financial products, to develop competitive solutions that will inevitably increase the overall market acceptance.

It will also allow the creation of a secondary market for solar panels that will encompass both residential and commercial sectors, which currently have access to fossil energy.

The Program is expected to result in 1,095,378 kWh of renewable electricity generated during the years of production of the distributed Solar PV systems through a forecasted total installed capacity of 30.59 MWp. The ASAP program will increase the share of renewable energy by 30.59 MWp to 26% of the total installed capacity in the country, which is currently at 18.47 MWp (a growth rate of 78.61%) to a target of 109 MWp by the end of the implementation of the program, which represents an increase from current levels.

The ASAP total annual capacity will result in 517 installations with an average power output of 4kW for households with energy use between 300 kWh/month and 600 kWh/month, and 793 installations with an average power output of 30kW for MSMEs with electricity use above 600 kWh/month.

The total energy produced will allow the reduction of 417,452 tonnes of CO2 equivalent over the 25-year lifetime of the solar systems installed, reaching a total of 2,382 household and MSME beneficiaries (including the special subset of irrigation Cooperatives), and create 1,794 jobs in the clean energy sector.

The Program is expected to play an important role in filling the long-term financing gap and mobilize funding for renewable energy projects. It will also provide a financial incentive for renewable energy investment projects unable to obtain suitable financing in the existing market.

Electricity is a significant portion of MSME operating expenses and a major expense for households. The concessional nature of the loan under the ASAP program will enable beneficiaries to reduce their electricity costs by up to 50% compared with the current market price with a flexible repayment term. This would enhance the competitiveness of the MSMEs.

### B.1 Context and Background (1000 words, approximately 2 pages)

#### Ghana’s climate profile
12.Ghana has a tropical climate that follows the country's varied topography. Annual rainfall ranges from about 500 mm in the north to over 3,000 mm in the south. The northern part of the country has one rainy season that extends from May to September; the south has two rainy seasons—the first lasts from April to June and the second from August to October. The dry season (December to March) brings the arid and dusty harmattan winds that blow from the Sahara Desert, which can last for up to 10 weeks in some parts of Ghana. Temperatures are highest around December with temperatures averaging at about 26°C, with higher temperatures in the north during the dry season. The area between the forest in the south and the savanna in the north is ideal for domestic food production, due to more rainfall and fertile soil.

#### The main climate changes being experienced in Ghana
13.The main climate changes being experienced in Ghana are temperature increases and shifting rainfall patterns, resulting in floods and droughts becoming more prevalent. Climate change impacts will continue to be felt across all sectors of the economy, with significant implications for agriculture, one of many, including small-scale farmers, being placed at risk. In terms of agriculture, decreased productivity is expected due to increased temperatures and reduced rainfall. The increase in temperature will exacerbate the increased occurrence of plant pests under drought conditions. The occurrence of vector-borne diseases such as malaria and dengue fever are also likely to increase with resultant depopulation of non-urban regions, primarily in northern Ghana. The country will also be affected by sea-level rise due to rising sea level rise, resulting increases in coastal erosion and the salinification of coastal aquifers.

Market Gap Assessment

## I. Assessment of the independent Technical Advisory Panel

### 1.1 Overview

In its assessment, the Independent Technical Advisory Panel (ITAP) is guided by decision B33/12 adopted by the Board, which explains the principles for demonstrating the impact potential of GCF-supported activities as well as other appraisal and assessment guidance in compliance with the GCF’s policies.

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change has concluded that Ghana is already experiencing the impacts of climate change, and these impacts are expected to worsen in the coming decades. The country faces significant challenges related to vulnerability to climate change due to its reliance on rain-fed agriculture, limited adaptive capacity and high levels of poverty.

Aside from the negative impact that climate change already had on the country, Ghana is also on a pathway of significantly increasing its own greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in the coming decades. The government’s current GHG emission trajectory projects a 2030 business-as-usual scenario; Ghana's emission could rise from 28 Mt CO2eq in 2021 to over 496 Mt CO2eq by 2050, driven primarily by population growth and urbanization, the transport, industrial and power sectors, driven by population growth, gross domestic product per capita growth and increasing vehicle ownership. While the government has ambitious plans to reduce GHG emissions in line with its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), significant challenges remain, including a complex macroeconomic environment characterized by unattractive fiscal conditions.

Against this background, Ecowash Ghana Limited, the accredited entity (AE), proposes the implementation of a project entitled "the Accelerating Solar Action Program (ASAP)". The ASAP is an initiative to promote the deployment of off-grid and distributed solar photovoltaic (PV) installations for households and micro, small and medium-sized enterprises in Ghana. The target beneficiaries are low-income households and SMEs. The objective of the programme is to provide affordable financing solutions to these target clients to address current energy access challenges. The project will be implemented through a public-private partnership with private sector participation and blended finance leveraging GCF and other sources of financing in the Ghanaian market. Through the programme, the AE seeks to demonstrate the viability of off-grid PV and, through associated technical assistance as well as advocacy work, lay the groundwork for a subsequent study on the market for distributed solar PV in Ghana and the wider region.

## 1.3 Paradigm shift potential Scale: Low-Medium

14. The funding proposal notes (correctly in the view of the independent TAP) that the current financing environment in Ghana is the key bottleneck for scaled investments into distributed solar PV installations by households, MSMEs and others. One major challenge in this context to the proposed project is the lack of a dedicated solar PV financing mechanism, which underpins macroeconomic fundamentals. The availability of concessional capital is thus considered key to the success of the proposed project. It would be important for the funding proposal to highlight prospective clients that otherwise would not be able to afford such borrowings.

15. In that context, it is unclear to the independent TAP how the proposed project will trigger financial inclusion in a meaningful way (i.e., beyond the immediate beneficiaries) after GCF resources have been exhausted. While the independent TAP agrees that some of the other programmatic measures included in the funding proposal could help to address this issue and may help in moving the market forward and in facilitating scale, the independent TAP would expect that without a dedicated solar PV financing mechanism, the proposed project will not be able to fully leverage GCF resources. The independent TAP notes that the 14-year tenor and high levels of interest rates on concessional loans are currently not attractive for most borrowers. These are not close to being commercially viable as a lending sector. Thus, it is in our view not realistic to believe that other financiers will see this as an opportunity without high levels of concessionality. The independent TAP notes that the funding proposal contemplates the use of a dedicated distributed solar PV financing mechanism, and to utilize GCF resources as first loss capital for such a mechanism would be a good way forward in order to make it commercially viable over time. It is unclear whether this approach would reduce lending rates by sufficiently large margins or whether it could be used to finance other types of projects that would provide a more sustainable and scalable lending model that could also be opened to other participating parties.

15. This challenge is compounded by the reality that for a proper scaling of the market for distributed solar PV, there needs to be a dedicated financing mechanism that can successfully target less affluent clients. # INTRODUCTION

## Background and Rationale

The Accelerating Solar Action Programme (ASAP) proposed by EcoBank Ghana aims at mobilizing concessional finance to promote the use of Solar PV systems in Ghana, with a focus on MSMEs, households, and public institutions. The programme seeks to contribute towards energy access for all Ghanaians and the creation of a more favourable environment for sustainable energy financing over the long term. The programme is in line with Government of Ghana’s GHG emissions reduction targets, as set out in its Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) submitted under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), and in line with the Government's strong focus on renewable energy and solar power development. It also aligns with the National Development Agenda (NDA) at the Ministry of Finance as well as the Energy Commission (EC). The proposed programme has national coverage, specifically, the intervention targets peri-urban areas across all sixteen regions of Ghana.

The Green Climate Fund is committed to gender and social inclusion and centres on gender-responsive climate change policies. The Gender Assessment for the ASAP programme in Ghana study has been found to fulfil the gender safeguard policy of the Fund to ensure that women and other vulnerable groups will equally and meaningfully share the programme's benefits.

## Specific Objective of the Assessment

The purpose of this assessment is to support the Implementing Entity (IE) to produce a comprehensive gender assessment, following the GCF policy. The study builds on an initial assessment carried out during the earlier stage of the formulation, and on comments from GCF. The assessment will feed the ASAP Gender Analysis Report (to be incorporated volume).

## Assessment Methodology

The assessment involved a literature review; formulating an assessment design/ approach; determination of key stakeholders and programme affected persons (PAPs); preparation of field instruments, data collection tools.

- Document review
  - To contextualize the assignment, we reviewed relevant national policy documents, ASAP documentation, and the initial draft of the Gender Analysis Report prepared during the early stages of the formulation, reports from relevant government ministries, agencies, departments, and international organizations.

### Assessment design/ approach

The methodology combined quantitative and qualitative approaches (mixed methods) in addition to the triangulation of information between primary and secondary sources.
- **Secondary data collection:** We used a purposeful sampling technique to select the sample respondents. At the national level, the following institutions was sampled in addition to the Accredited Entity: nine (9) government ministries and agencies; four (4) private sector companies; five (5) civil society organizations; two (2) trade associations; four (4) ongoing and past solar PV projects; and two (2) NGOs. ### 2.1 Legal Status of Women and Girls in Ghana

The Government of Ghana has made a strong political commitment to gender equality and is a signatory to several international conventions, protocols, and agreements. These are aimed at ending violence against women and girls to achieve economic growth and development.

Ghana has several national laws protecting women's rights and violence against women and girls including the Criminal Code Act (1960) which criminalizes discrimination based on sex, social or economic status among others; the 1998 Criminal Code Amendment Act and legal amendments criminalizing certain sexual offenses such as rape, forced marriage, child marriage, female genital mutilation (FGM) (1995) and child abuse (1998). ### 2.3.4 Key challenges to achieving gender equality in Ghana

The main barriers to attaining gender equality in the country include low political prioritization of gender issues, limited data on women and girls, and implementation gaps between the statements contained in the formal framework and their actual implementation. Some institutions have gender policies, others are still developing them. There is also a challenge with the collection of gender-disaggregated data across all sectors. The lack of disaggregation makes it difficult to identify the number of women and men in rural persons, their gender and inclusion expertise is limited and those who are not in management positions, and those who are not in decision-making roles.

Other challenges are the patriarchal social structure and discriminatory customary practices where male domination is persistent. Socio-cultural discriminatory practices, norms, and prejudiced institutions continue to limit women’s access to resources such as land, and also access to credit and educational opportunities, among others. In addition, systemic gender-based violence continues to be a major challenge in the country, including domestic violence, denial of education, and cultural servitude. Another barrier which runs through all the sectors is that of a woman’s limited access to resources, including technical support, finance, and productive time. Finally, women have poor access to justice for women; limited logistics for monitoring the gender equality aspects of policies.

### 5.4 Commonly held beliefs, perceptions, and stereotypes

Our field assessment confirmed well established general perceptions of gender roles and biases. The PAPs had beliefs related to loans, technology adoption and farming. ### Recommendations

Based on the assessment's findings, we propose the following recommendations for the ASAP programme:

1. The differential needs of men and women with respect to the provision of loans and installation of solar PV systems for PAPs as indicated section 6.3 of this report should be taken into consideration when disbursing loans and providing technical assistance.
2. Beyond the provision of long-term and short-term loans for PAPs, the ASAP programme should invest in capacity building to ensure that all stakeholders involved are adequately trained. Training workshops would be organized for ASAP staff and selected staff of the key implementing institutions (ECA and EPA) on how to implement the ASAP programme. In addition, training workshops would also be organized for the ASAP programme staff; additional training would be provided on how to implement the ASAP.
3. In the assessment and certification of solar PV vendors, conscious effort should be made to include not only women but also men who are female-headed households (FHHs) and male female owned HHs.
4. Considering that there are different categories of PAPs (male and female-headed households, male and female own HHs), and WUAs to be engaged by the ASAP, it is important for the programme to ensure that all these groups have access to solar PV systems which would usually disadvantaged. The bank should provide business technical assistance (non-financial support) to the vendors and families who benefit from the loans. This would ensure business

5. In the implementation of all project activities for PAPs, a quota of 50% women participation should be set as part of the gender action plan. This means that at least half of the women's membership in groups and at least two women in leadership positions in the WUA should be a requirement for group members to access loans. Training and advisory services should be done at a time when women are free to participate.

6. Adequate financial resources should be allocated for the implementation of the Gender Action Plan (GAP). A mid-term review on gender equity achievement of ASAP should be undertaken. # Introduction

This document presents the Gender Action Plan (GAP) for the Accelerating Solar Access Programme (ASAP), developed by the Gender Assessment Study carried out from May to July 2023 to assess the baseline conditions of the sector and intervention.

This gender action plan provides a strategic approach to how gender equality and social inclusion issues identified in the study will be addressed. The GAP outlines the strategies for addressing these issues, including the targets of the plan; actions to address identified gender inequalities and vulnerabilities; GAP implementation mechanism and monitoring and evaluation aspects of the programme.

The GAP is structured around three components, each with a set of sub-components and indicators to monitor the progress towards the achievement of effective gender and inclusion safeguards. This GAP is focused on women considering their disadvantaged position. In certain elements of the plan, the target group includes men as well. The plan takes into consideration the diversity among ASMEI and financial WUA members. Intersectionality categories such as race, class, income, education, age, ability, sexual orientation, ethnicity, indigency, and geography were taken into consideration in the development of the GAP.
