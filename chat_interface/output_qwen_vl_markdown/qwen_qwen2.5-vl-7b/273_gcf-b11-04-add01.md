### A.2. Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

The project seeks to enhance the resilience capacity of the indigenous communities living in the rich carbon stock wetland ecosystem in the Province of Datem del Marañón (PDM) in the Region of Loreto, Peru, improve their livelihoods and to reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions through deforestation. The target population is a primary low-income group with limited access to social services and education. The project will address social and economic issues to entrust the management of the natural resource base to the indigenous communities.

The proposed activities seek to: (a) facilitate the participatory preparation of land-use and operational management plans in accordance with national law, including the empowerment of women and power women in the decision-making process; and (c) strengthen and expand commercially viable and sustainable bio-businesses of non-timber forest products, agroforestry, and aquaculture. The project will also promote community-based enterprises for high opportunities, and consolidate indigenous peoples' basic rights. All the proposed activities are community-based adaptation and climate change mitigation good practices, which are complemented by strong monitoring and evaluation systems to ensure their effectiveness and sustainability.

The project is based on a solid scientific knowledge base in order to guide future activities aimed at preserving the carbon stock and enhancing community well-being.

## C.1. Strategic Context

The project is located in the western middle portion of the Amazon Basin, in the Province of Datem del Marañón, Loreto Region, Peru. The main feature of the project area is the presence of the alluvial cone of the Pastaza–Marañon Rivers. This area has been inhabited by indigenous communities for centuries and is one of the most biodiverse areas in the Amazon region.

Associated with these landforms are rich organic soils, better classified as peat. Most of the area is covered by forest, with some areas dedicated to subsistence farming and cattle ranching.

San Lorenzo is the seat of the provincial government, with a population of 6,034 – 12 per cent of the provincial population (as of 2007 census). All other towns and villages are smaller, with populations of fewer than 2,000.

Datem del Marañón's population is distributed in over 284 populated areas. Poverty affects over 75 per cent of the population living in rural areas (the poorest district), while 13 per cent live in urban areas (the second-poorest district). It is estimated that 26,329 people speak Spanish as their main language; the remainder of the population – well under 40% – speaks one of the indigenous languages. The most common are Quechua, Aymara, Shipibo-Conibo, Kandezo, Chocó, Queswachaka, Wampis and Shawi, each characterized as having its own territory, culture and language. Each of these indigenous peoples traditionally belongs to an ethnic group. The ultimate authority of native or indigenous communities is the community council.

It should be noted that the mestizo (mixed race) population is growing faster than that of indigenous communities, and includes in-migration from other regions. This creates tensions over access to land and other natural resources, forests, fisheries, mining and oil extraction.

Droughts In recent years, the Amazon River has experienced extreme drought conditions, which have had major social and environmental consequences. As indicated in Figure C.1.3, during the last 40 years the Amazon River has shown a dramatic negative trend in multi-year discharges.

The very intense droughts in 2005 and 2010 are part of the spectrum of Amazon die-back, a scenario in which the humid tropical forest is converted to dry savanna due to changes in temperature, rainfall and the onset of climate feedbacks. ### E2.3. Contribution to the creation of an enabling environment

Consensus building among key stakeholders, including government, indigenous groups and private-sector actors, is a difficult, largely neglected task due to its high transaction costs. Nonetheless, many experiences confirm that sharing a common vision is a very efficient and effective mechanism through which to achieve social goals. The Building Operational Land-Use Plans (BOLUP) project in the province of San Lorenzo, Peru, has developed a pragmatic mechanism through which to build consensus in the operational management of wetlands and associated lands. The project has selected several planning instruments to be formulated through a thorough and open participatory process. These include: land-use plans, zoning maps, buffer zones, environmental impact assessments, technical assistance training, logistical support and coordination are the responsibilities of the project, together with the facilitation of public participation in the development of these instruments. The project will also facilitate the signing of tenure agreements for the benefit of all participants. It is hoped that after several similar participatory processes, community leaders, public officials and leaders from the private sector will learn that such an approach is valid and worth the additional time and costs, and that reaching agreements is an effective means of managing relationships with other social actors.

The project will strengthen private-sector participation through its support for the definition of clear land-use plans and sustainable development policies. Both instruments are expected to reduce the transaction costs of setting up new businesses and increase the efficiency of government licensing entities.

### Economic and social development level of the country and the affected population

Peru is one of Latin America's fastest-growing economies. Between 2002 and 2013, the average growth rate was 6.1 per cent in a context of low inflation (2.5 per cent on average). The effects of strong growth on employment and income have been positive for most people. However, despite this economic progress, poverty rates fell by more than half, from approximately 55.6 per cent to 27.7 per cent of the population (National Institute of Statistics and Informatics 2013). The country has made significant progress in reducing extreme poverty, with the number of people escaping poverty in the country. In addition, the share of the population living below the official extreme poverty line declined dramatically, from 15.8 per cent in 2005 to 4.7 per cent in 2013. Extreme poverty is highly rural and concentrated among indigenous populations.

### Datem del Marañón is the country's third-poorest district, with its population distributed in 284 communities located in the Amazon region of Peru

Poverty affects over 75 per cent of the population, while extreme poverty affects 42 per cent, reaching 61 per cent in Moquegua. The average income for a family is US$300 per month (National Institute of Statistics and Informatics 2013). The local population suffers from a strong persistence of endemic diseases such as hepatitis B, dengue, and malaria. The health services provided do not yet meet the needs and culture of the people they serve. Education is provided through 203 educational centres at initial, primary and secondary levels, with a ratio of one teacher per three or more grades. ### Needs for strengthening institutions and implementing capacity

The current national environmental authority in Peru is the Ministerio del Ambiente (Ministerio del Ambiente – MINAM), which has a General Directorate for Climate Change, Desertification and Water Resources (Dirección General de Cambio Climático, Desierto y Recursos Hídricos). The National Commission on Climate Change (Comisión Nacional sobre Cambio Climático – CONAC) is the focal point of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) secretariat. MINAM chairs the National Commission on Climate Change and coordinates the implementation of the UNFCCC in various sectors and to design and promote the National Climate-Change Strategy (Estrategia Nacional sobre Cambio Climático – ENCC). Adopted in 2003 and currently under revision, it is a strategic document that sets out the country's climate change policy.

The Organic Law of Regional Governments, which established the obligation to draft Regional Climate Change Strategies, was adopted in 2002. Three regions already have a Regional Climate Change Strategy; there are five Regional Climate Change Strategies in preparation and three more under study (MINAM 2013). MINAM has developed adaptation and mitigation projects under the National System of Public Investment (Sistema Nacional de Inversión Pública – SNIP). The National Centre for Strategic Planning (Centro Nacional de Planeamiento Estratégico - CENPLAN) is responsible for the implementation of the ENCC. Although men carry out extractive activities, the processing and conservation activities will incorporate women. According to the experience of the Pastaza Morona-First Stage Project with bio-business implementation, women were responsible for the production of handicrafts (e.g., ceramics) and in the production of fish (e.g., cooking fish), and in the production of handicrafts and ceramics. Taking these experiences into account, the project will create spaces for the gradual participation of women in bio-businesses and will train them in economic development issues, and promote their access to financial products as part of the component to support indigenous peoples' request for access to finance.

Currently, most women in the seven native communities only speak their native language. In that sense, the project will be respectful of their culture and will seek to achieve the gradual participation of women in community meetings, enabling them to participate more effectively in decision-making processes.

There is a considerable gap in socioeconomic and family composition data and information on the ethnic groups in PDM. It will take a long time to build trust before such information can be collected. The proposed M&E system will seek to address this issue.

PROFONANPE will ensure balanced participation of women in the field office. If feasible, no less than 50 per cent of the project's personnel in Datem del Marañón shall be women.

# Risk Assessment Summary

The following risks have been identified:

- **Financial risk:** No relevant risk has been identified in this category. - **Operational risks:**
  - Development of a negative perception of project activities or personnel by key stakeholders.
  - Delays in obtaining permits for construction and operation due to work in Datem del Marañón for the duration of the project. Other operational risks include: market failure and lack of local skills related to the maintenance of the bio-business equipment.

As indicated in the analysis of threats to the area of interest, very strong external forces are exerting pressure on Datem del Marañón. These include illegal forest harvesting and cutting and/or the search for highly paid jobs.
- **Environmental risks:** Although the project's environmental category is "C" (very low environmental impact), other external environmental events could disrupt the project's performance: severe droughts and associated fires, floods, landslides, and deforestation. These events could lead to soil erosion, damage to vegetation and reducing production of inputs for bio-businesses. Such events could force the communities to seek other areas to harvest; this could have potential social, economic and environmental consequences.
- **Political risks:** Elections for the executive and legislative branches are to be held in 2016. Political shifts could reduce the project's performance.

No survey was conducted to develop a baseline of the number of males and females and respective income and expenditure levels. However, the project team has access to information on this topic. MINAM has agreed to provide detailed satellite data and information on forest cover changes in the intervention area. The project reporting frequency and project level evaluations will be semi-annual and annual respectively. The reports will include an overview of the project's progress against expected and actual levels of project activities, counterpart behaviours, and indicator values. The reports will include a summary of lessons learned from the project implementation process as well as recommendations and insights for future activities during the life span of the project. PMU will explicitly link the monitoring plan with lessons learned during the project’s activities, especially the innovation pilots. This information will be uploaded in the project created database and shared with MINAM and other stakeholders to ensure that lessons are captured and disseminated.

Dynamic implementation-monitoring of project activities
Monitoring of project activities will be achieved through management actions, including monthly briefings with the GCF staff meetings, and review of staff and adviser inputs to quarterly reports. A system will be established to channel feedback from project activity feedback forms, and the findings will be incorporated in recommendations for changes as needed.

Specific issues addressed in these feedback forms will include the quality of technical assistance provided by the project, the accuracy and timeliness of the implementation data provided by the project, and the recipients' response to project assistance.

### Dynamic implementation-monitoring results
Performance against identified tasks: (i) analyzing realized indicator values; and (ii) evaluating the overall results and impacts of the project. Realized (actual) indicator values will be analyzed, focusing primarily—but not exclusively—on those indicators whose values differ significantly from proposed levels. The reason "Why" the difference occurred and what findings will be incorporated in recommendations for changes in indicator values.

Specific issues to be addressed include the quality of reported data, such as timing and data classification, operation of the data collection system, and whether the project has responded to assistance as contrasted with expectations, and how closely indicator values are a function of identified tasks.

Progress reporting
The M&E system will provide information regarding project execution: progress towards achieving key targets; and, possibly, towards impacts and outcomes. The Project Coordinator will prepare reports for each regular meeting of the PAC. An outside independent third party will conduct the mid-term review and the end-of-project evaluation. The mid-term review will be conducted in accordance with the terms agreed upon by the GCF, the Government of Papua New Guinea (GDP), and the PMU. Lessons learned will be documented and communicated to the GCF, the GDP, and the community of development practitioners. Dissemination of lessons learned will include national and international meetings on REDD and climate change.

Monitoring of capacity-building
The project will provide support for capacity-building to increase the sustainability of project investments. During implementation, the project will create various indicators (e.g., number of people trained, number of government offices supported, etc.) to monitor progress towards achieving key targets. The project will also monitor the PMU's capacity to develop plans to manage natural resources and implement activities for the creation of sustainable livelihoods in order to promote trade, investment and private-sector development.

Monitoring implementation of pilots
The project will conduct various pilot programmes to test innovative technologies that could produce tangible impacts. A number of indicators will be created (e.g., baseline studies for pilot programmes; M&E to ascertain the impact of pilot programmes).

### Annex VIII: Environmental impact assessment summary (The full assessment can be found as a separate document in Spanish)

This project is focused on promoting participatory bio-business initiatives by indigenous communities, based on sustainable use of natural resources and the conservation of cultural heritage. It also supports the consolidation of the proper zoning and territorial management of the Province of Datem del Marañón (Loreto Region), which provides the legal and technical framework.

The purpose of the environmental assessment of this proposal is to ensure that the project pays due consideration to the potential positive or negative impacts on, and/or minimizing potential environmental damage due to project activities. On the other hand, the assessment also identifies opportunities to maximize the potential positive impacts. The environmental safeguards are also tools and means by which communities can participate in decision-making processes by expressing their views and asking for modifications or improvements so their rights are not affected.

The methodology used is based on guidelines established by PROFONADNE and the Green Climate Fund for the application of performance standards (PSs), which are also those used by the International Financial Corporation. Among the eight PSs considered by IFC, the following two are most relevant to the project in terms of the environment:

- **PS1:** Assessment and management of environmental and social risks and impacts;
- **PS2:** Resource efficiency and pollution prevention;
- **PS3:** Biodiversity conservation and sustainable management of living natural resources; and
- **PS7:** Indigenous peoples.

PS4: Community health, safety, and security is partly applied to promote safety and health safeguards to project activities. In addition, PS5 Cultural heritage is applied to avoid any harm to ancient ceramic art found within Achuar communities and other ethnic groups.

The impacts were characterized to identify their positive or negative potential effects on the following environmental elements: water, soil, air, aquatic fauna, terrestrial fauna and forests/aguajales (palm swamps), cultural heritage, and indigenous peoples. The assessment was conducted in such a way that the final environmental risk could be classified as significant, moderate or minor/non-significant.

The evaluation results were grouped by project component and are shown as follows:

### Component 1: Strengthening institutional capacity in government organizations

The activities under this component are focused on providing training, technical assistance and coordination support so that the official institutions and providers can provide their mandated services and fulfill their responsibilities. These services include strengthening the institutional structure and legislation. These services are related to the protection of conservation units, preparation and dissemination of zoning plans, and the development of a territorial management plan for zoning and coordination between sectors and government levels, in order to achieve the effective and sustainable use of the province's natural resources. In that sense, these indirect impacts are all of a positive nature.

Component 2 is focused on working closely with communities and their institutions, such as production associations, environmental monitoring committees (from the environmental conservation areas) and indigenous peoples' organizations. The objective is to strengthen the capacity of these institutions by equipping them institutionally by facilitating relevant technical and legal information, training and technical assistance for those tasks within community responsibility that provide sustainability to project activities. These tasks include the establishment of a community-based monitoring system; the strengthening of management, accountability, the establishment of community land boundaries, providing technical assistance to bio- business development, the promotion of sustainable use of natural resources, promoting knowledge-sharing opportunities, among other activities. Through these actions, institutions will be strengthened, as will the sustainability of natural resource management. The indirect impacts on the province's natural resources will be minimized.

The project's operational actions will depend largely on coordination among project personnel, community institutions and government officers. Constant visits, meetings, workshops, studies and training activities will be carried out in order to promote the use of fossil fuel motor boats by communities. These are being used as motor boats that use fossil fuels and emit carbon dioxide. Many of the meetings will involve taking packaged food and beverages for attendees. These packages are usually disposed of in the environment and pollute water bodies.

Component 3. Resilience built through sustainable bio-businesses in natural resources management areas

This component promotes bio-businesses through the sustainable use of locally managed natural resources according to community choices and opportunities. Emphasis is placed on the sustainable use of natural resources and subsequent income generation through processing, value adding and marketing. Component 3 represents the economic development of the project.

Bio-business choices are diverse, some of which are already in operation since they started during the previous phase. These include activities such as ice-making and palm oil production from agave and other palm trees, fresh and processed fish, sago de drago (dragon's blood) latex, managed wildlife meat, ancient medicinal herbs (like clary), and handicrafts using seeds, leaves, and bark.

When the products are to be sold, an officially approved management plan is required. This ensures the sustainability of the natural resource base. Conservation through sustainable use results in a highly positive impact on the environment by reducing anthropogenic pressure in rivers, cochas, aguajales and forests.

The promotion of new associations and the increased economic drive generated by the bio-businesses will have an important effect on the conservation of the natural resources and their associated biotic and abiotic components. Moreover, more meetings, training activities and income may result in the increased consumption of goods and consequently the corresponding solid waste produced, causing localized impacts on soil or water.

On the other hand, some of the bio-businesses, such as ice-making, palm oil production and saemilving, require externally generated energy from fossil fuels. Pollution of soil, water and air is a risk—evaluated as minor—which can be minimized. The following mitigating measures are being proposed for Component 3:

a) Regarding the generation of energy for processing (bio-businesses), coordinates with local authorities to partially use the public energy already being produced in order to connect to the lower voltage grid.

b) Analyse the possibility of partially replacing fossil fuel produced energy for bio-businesses with renewable energy sources.

c) Carry out regular maintenance of energy generators and avoid contamination of the liquid or solid waste produced;

d) Strictly follow management plans for the sustainable use of natural resources;

e) Plan the visits, meetings, workshops and training activities in advance in order to minimize the distance traveled by motor buses used by communities, and government or project personnel; and

f) In conjunction with communities and municipal governments, promote activities to strengthen public awareness on the importance of recycling and reusing materials such as plastic bags, bottles and used batteries, in soil and water. Activities should include the promotion of best practices to properly dispose of solid waste, especially which is non-biodegradable.

## PS2: Labour and working conditions

PROFONDE has high standards when hiring staff and rigorously applies all of Peru's legal guidelines, in addition to those set by the Ministry of Cooperation agencies. All staff recruitment takes place in a competitive and transparent manner, without discrimination, and is particularly respectful of the local sensitivities.

The project does not involve the permanent recruitment of indigenous staff. It seeks to empower communities to implement the actions identified as a result of the enactment of the land-use plan and natural resources management plans. In the case of hiring administrative, operational, technical and technical staff, the project may give preference to those who are from the region and are of indigenous origin.

The project will not promote or force child labour. (In indigenous communities, underage children are traditionally involved in productive activities as apprentices and perform tasks suited to their abilities under adult supervision.)

## PS3: Land acquisition and involuntary resettlement

The project will not generate the displacement of indigenous communities. Instead, it will strengthen legal security by promoting land titling and land-use planning.

The project seeks the sustainable use of, and unrestricted access to, natural resources in the province, without generating conflicts with other users. The project will seek to ensure that any actions that benefit indigenous peoples and persons from outside the territory may initiate the same territory as project beneficiaries. In addition, the project will promote a policy of intercultural interaction as is currently being implemented in the environmental conservation area.

The project will promote alternative activities through the development of bio-businesses, such as the commercialization of dried, salted and frozen fish, and aguaje harvesting (without cutting down palm trees) and its transformation into oil, flour and oils.

## PS4: Indigenous peoples

The project's design includes a process for the free and informed consent of indigenous peoples, as a result of which the social licensing of the project was obtained. This process included an intercultural approach to ensure that all aspects were understood by the communities involved.

The project will develop a process of information and permanent dialogue with indigenous peoples so that all decisions regarding the project will be made with consensus as the preferred method.

Gender

Based on cultural aspects, members of indigenous families have well-defined roles. Men are responsible for hunting, fishing and forest resource gathering; while women are in charge of food preparation and childcare. The work in the farm (chacra), where yuca (Manihot esculenta) is the principal product, is an activity performed by both men and women.

Although it is true that extractive activities are mainly carried out by men, the activities of processing and wetland conservation will incorporate women. According to the Pastaza Morona-first stage project's experience regarding the incorporation of women in the conservation process (salting dried fish) and in the production of handicrafts and ceramics. Taking into account this experience, the project will create spaces for the gradual participation of women in the activities related to wetland conservation and the processing of yuca. In addition, it will promote the sharing of experiences with women leaders of other indigenous groups and respect their traditional knowledge and practices. This will allow the incorporation of women in the decision-making process, leading to gradual and negotiated incorporation without overloading their occupations, which would mean an increased workload and potential conflicts in their homes.

Currently, most of the communities involved speak in their native languages. In that sense, the project will be respectful of their culture and will seek to achieve gradual participation of women in community meetings and will enable the conduct of meetings in each population's language.

### Annex XI: Project sustainability: an innovative response by PROFONADNE

A recurring question emerges from almost all projects funded mainly by international sources, no matter if they deal with conservation, sustainable development, climate change or infrastructure: how sustainable are positive impacts? The answer is not always clear. It is difficult to find indicators that objectively demonstrates the degree of sustainability after a project has come to the end of its life span.

Partly this is due to the fact that projects are often designed and implemented without considering their long-term viability; in certain cases there are insufficient provisions to ensure the future sustainability of actions (c) the design of certain works are poorly adjusted to the real needs of beneficiaries, (d) very few financial resources are allocated for the maintenance of infrastructure and services after a project has ended. This is often due to lack of political will or in government staff tend to erode the memory of commitment given. Some of these causes by themselves, or a combination thereof, can lead to the failure of projects.

PROFONADNE has almost 23 years of experience in funding projects from bilateral, multilateral, corporate and international foundations sources; and two decades facing the same question. However, its institutions have been able to find an innovative solution that allows for the sustainability of projects in most developing countries: the allocation of trust funds. These can be of two types: sinking funds and endowments.

Sinking funds are a capital invested in the short term market the annual returns from which are used for project funding. The resulting capital is granted to PROFONADNE in perpetuity.

Thanks to this trust fund mechanism, PROFONADNE has managed to apply a useful answer to the uncertainty of sustainability of projects. This is because the amount of resources allocated to implement such a project (the years) is insufficient within which to consolidate operations achieved during that period. To date, PROFONADNE has set up 10 endowments, the purpose of which is to facilitate the sustainability of project achievements in the long run.

A formula derived from this mechanism is the generation of an endowment from the annual income gained by the investment of a certain amount of resources during a specific implementation period. By means of the proper management of investments it is possible to obtain capital in perpetuity at the end of the project, thus making possible the application of this modality "mixed trust fund" and it is the first institution of its kind that has applied in Latin America.

The purpose of such a mechanism is not to replace the potential sustainability of certain investments or to simply support them. Rather, it is an innovative financing strategy for projects that can assist some initiatives during a certain period in order to achieve full sustainability, especially those with little prior experience or facing long periods of maturity.
