## Climate Change Problem

Climate change is increasingly increasing humanitarian tragedy and economic damage globally and will continue to do so at an accelerating pace. According to the World Bank (2019), climate change costs are estimated to be in excess of $23 billion, significantly higher than $16 billion in 2019 (MunichRe). Climate change is affecting water availability and quality, the agriculture and food sectors, energy production, infrastructure, and health through multiple direct and indirect pathways. The cost of adaptation is rising as climate impacts intensify.

It has been estimated that up to $300 billion per year would be needed for climate adaptation in developing countries by 2050 (G20). This represents a significant opportunity for the private sector, particularly for blended finance and private investment (GCAP21). While adaptation has been traditionally considered as a public good and a service provided by governments, there is an increasing recognition that it can also be delivered through the private sector to accelerate the pace of adaptation worldwide while also helping local economies transition to more resilient pathways.

The private sector plays a critical role in climate adaptation in several ways. First, the private sector generates significant amounts of capital for investment and can provide financial resources to governments and other investors as well as finance flows and business guidance to help accelerate the growth of companies building a more sustainable future. Second, the private sector has the ability to scale up adaptation technologies and updating of adaptation techniques and practices by demonstrating their profitability and economic viability.

The demand for adaptation technology exists in all markets, and a growing number of users demonstrate an ability to pay for these services. The World Bank estimates that the global market for climate change adaptation spending is expected to more than double to $380 billion by 2022. There is an urgent need to mobilize both private sector capital and public resources to meet this demand.

General Secretary Antonio Guterres called for 50 per cent of all climate finance to be allocated to adaptation in developing countries, with the aim that at least half of this funding should be provided by the private sector. This would ensure that developing countries will rapidly become more resilient, because access to cost-efficient, proven adaptation technologies can be accelerated in response to demand.

## Proposed Intervention

This Funding Proposal recommends that GCF commit up to $10 billion of catalytic capital to scale-up ambition at adaptation finance by catalyzing the Climate Resilience and Adaptation Finance and Technology-transfer facility (CRAFT) under the Green Climate Fund (GCF). The CRAFT facility is a new, innovative climate change adaptation facility called "The Adaptation Gap" by mobilizing capital to scale up technologies for climate resilience and adaptation and applying them in developing countries. The CRAFT facility will be structured as an independent entity within GCF's existing financial structure, including a Junior concessional layer that mitigates downside risk for Senior non-concessional investments.

CRAFT is the first of its kind in the world and has been secured additional Junior and Senior capital, but its mobilization has been impacted by the effects of COVID-19. The CRAFT facility will be structured as an independent entity within GCF's existing financial structure, including a Junior and $108 million of non-concessional capital into CRAFT's first investment. GCF's catalytic capital would help to scale investments in the private sector adaptation in developing countries by mobilizing up to $156 of additional Senior non-concessional capital.

The Fund has identified multiple market segments and technologies/products, and services to manage climate vulnerability and risk, including: (i) water management; (ii) agriculture and food security under extreme climates; and reduce scarcity (particularly water and food).

A $3100 million commitment of catalytic capital from GCF would allow CRAFT to scale-up ambition in adaptation finance and technology transfer. This would enable CRAFT to mobilize up to $2 billion of additional Senior non-concessional investments and transfer of critical climate technologies in developing countries, particularly in the context of promoting green recovery from COVID-19. The CRAFT initiative is based on the premise that if capital was rapidly deployed to support SME-adaptation solutions, developing countries and communities worldwide would become less vulnerable more quickly because they will have access to a wide range of adaptation technologies. The deployment of these tools will also help scale up existing technologies. In an ideal scenario, the adaptation finance gap would be reduced and developing countries could face fewer barriers in accessing climate finance, without relying on donor funding. However, several barriers need to be addressed before this solution is fully met.

### Barriers

#### Barrier 1 - Insufficient local, actionable information about climate risks, impacts and adaptation options.
The companies offering these solutions do not use the term "climate-resilience" companies, and many do not realize their products can help with adaptation. The companies that do not offer adaptation solutions will not be able to do so in many forms. By identifying the "tools" that can be used for adaptation and resilience, CRAFT can help narrow the gap and accelerate the deployment of climate-resilience technologies. In addition, many companies with these tools do not serve the sectors and geographies where they are most needed.

#### Barrier 2 - Limited access to technologies to manage climate risks.
By investing in the best climate resilient solutions, companies can reduce their own carbon footprints and footprints globally. CRAFT supports technology transfer for climate resilience. Through the international expansion of portfolio companies commercial activities, CRAFT can embed technology, knowledge, and capacity for climate resilience into developing countries.

# Independent Technical Advisory Panel’s assessment of FP181

## Proposal name: CRAFT - Catalytic Capital for First Private Investment Fund for Adaptation Technologies in Developing Countries

### Accredited entity: Pegasus Capital Advisors

#### Project/programme size: Large

## Assessment of the independent Technical Advisory Panel

### 1. Impact potential (Scale: Medium)

#### 1.1 Overview

##### 1.1.1 Technology innovation is a critical element in addressing climate challenges, both in mitigation and adaptation contexts. The Paris Agreement, signed by 195 countries to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, systematically addresses these challenges needs to involve the private sector, as the challenges posed by the climate crisis are at a scale that goes beyond what public resources can address alone. While there has been significant progress in clean energy technology innovation, in 2019 only c. per cent of venture capital funding went into climate technology. While this is expected to change over time, it will take years for the private sector to catch up with what needs to happen. Nevertheless, the creation of a dedicated fund structure in the form of CRAFT is welcomed as an important step towards bridging the gap between public and private finance being developed countries in their adaptation to the climate crisis.

##### 1.1.2 The CRAFT Fund proposes to address adaptation challenges in 17 developing countries (Bahamas, Barbados, Belize, Benin, Kenya, Malawi, Mexico, Mozambique, Namibia, Nigeria, Senegal and Trinidad and Tobago), of which only c. six (Bahamas, Brazil, Mexico, Rwanda, South Africa, Trinidad and Tobago) are currently in the process of developing their national adaptation plans. This assessment. The independent Technical Advisory Panel (TAP) notes that the rationale for the CRAFT Fund is consistent with the priorities identified by the World Bank as well as the first two priority areas of the Climate Policy Initiative study that was provided to serve as a demonstration of feasibility, thus raising questions as to whether they have the absorption capacity and institutional readiness to implement such projects.

##### 1.1.3 CRAFT is expected to directly deliver close to USD 400 million in financing for adaptation purposes through investments in technology companies with between USD 5 million and USD 100 million annual revenue and undertake activities in six technology areas with potential climate adaptation use, including:

- (a) agroforestry;
- (b) water harvesting and drip irrigation;
- (c) resilient food systems;
- (d) geospatial mapping and imaging;
- (e) supply chain analytics; and
- (f) catastrophe risk modeling.

A 2018 IPCC report states that climate change will precipitate seven food shortages and reduce food’s nutritional quality. In addition to increasing the rate of soil loss and land degradation, impacts of drought, flooding heat waves and wildfires threaten the sector more broadly. Growing population and affluence are also driving demand for both staple foods and high-value agricultural commodities. The combination of a threatened supply and growing demand will put pressure on productive land, even while competition for that land increases from biofuels, urban expansion and other non-productive uses.

CRAFT seeks to also address investment in agricultural products such as drought-tolerant crops and drip irrigation systems. These types of solutions improve sustainable management of land, while building resilience to climate-related vulnerabilities that are reducing agricultural productivity and increasing food import dependence. As these climate resilience solutions are scaled up, they can be designed with the potential to generate additional global environmental benefits in the context of physical climate change risks. In addition to its investments in high-impact innovative companies which provide the products and services described above, CRAFT is also investing in organizations that work with the tools to mitigate these climate risks. CRAFT is envisioning establishing a technical assistance facility (contingent upon securing the necessary funding), which would take concrete steps in providing training for extension agents and other agricultural service providers, as well as extension systems to be more responsive to and inclusive of women, addressing structural barriers to ensure more equitable access to productive resources, while helping companies better respond to the needs of rural women. This will include supporting more women to access the tools to protect themselves and their families from the ripple effects of land degradation.

# Sector: Water Availability and Efficiency

Women are not just underrepresented in specialized jobs, they are overrepresented in unpaid work, including water collection. Across Sub-Saharan Africa women are far more likely to gather water than men, especially in households that lack piped water within the home[1]. For example, in rural areas of Ethiopia, women spend an average of 20 hours a week traveling and from a year traveling to and from a water source. In rural areas, the time spent gathering water is especially high[2]. In rural areas of Guinea for example, women spend more than twice as much time collecting water as men do. Women in Sub-Saharan Africa travel an average of 30 minutes farther than men on the same tasks. Collectively, women from Sub-Saharan Africa spend about 40 billion hours a year collecting water for their families and communities, which is equivalent to a year’s worth of work by the entire workforce of the United States[3]. Investing in technology to enhance climate resilience while alleviating stresses on those particularly burdened by water scarcity have the potential to accelerate the closing of the gender gap in the water sector. This can be achieved through the use of low-cost water harvesting tools, reducing household burdens, reductions in time spent and distances traveled to fetch water, while also adapting to climate change[4].

Companies that leverage technology to provide access to low-cost water harvesting tools have the potential to boost the livelihoods of women and children by reducing the time spent on gathering water. This can contribute to closing specific gender gaps. CRAFT investments in technology-enabled physical products and services encompass drip irrigation systems and water harvesting panels. These types of solutions have many positive effects in adapting to climate change, including improved food security, increased nutrition, and gender equality. As these climate resilience solutions are scaled up, they can be integrated as components of systemic interventions designed to generate additional impact in the context of physical products and services. For example, drip irrigation systems and water harvesting panels can help deliver impact while reducing the vulnerability of people, physical assets and natural systems to the adverse effects of climate change. Further, by applying the IMS, Lightsmith will track, in real-time, the direct and indirect impacts of CRAFT investments on women and girls with measurable impacts firms and their products have on women and girls and the potential to shrink sector specific gender gaps. The CRAFT Fund seeks to invest in technologies that can help communities, particularly those marginalized—including women and girls—in developing countries suffer less damage, loss, harm, and disruption (and recover more effectively when harm and disruption occur).
