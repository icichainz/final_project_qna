# PROJECT / PROGRAMME EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (max 300 words)

The objective of the proposed Project is to promote investments in energy efficiency (EE) measures by small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in El Salvador. The IDB will provide a sovereign-guaranteed loan*, funded with GCF resources, for up to US$25 million. This loan will be provided on concessional terms and conditions and will be maintained in a dedicated revolving fund, will be blended with BANDESAL’s own resources in order to provide a concessional loan facility of up to US$40 million. The loan will be structured as a combination of standard instruments at adequate terms and conditions to SME firms interested in adopting eligible EE measures*. The GCF loan will be complemented by the use of innovative financial mechanisms, including blended finance, green bonds, carbon credits, and risk-sharing instruments required to support the structuring of technically robust, bankable, private sector investments in energy efficiency. These financial mechanisms will be developed with the support of the GCF grant included in this proposal.

The standardization of non-financial mechanisms and risk sharing instruments to be developed with the support of the GCF grant includes the development of a technical assistance program for SMEs interested in EE measures, including energy savings, and energy savings insurance/revenue products (ES). The implementation of the financial strategy, with respect to the loan facility provided by the IDB, will be based on the following principles: 1) ensuring that SME access to financing at adequate terms and conditions for EE projects (source of financing), to develop a robust pipeline of EE projects; 2) building a project development pipeline; 3) providing technical assistance to support the development of the aforementioned financial mechanisms and instruments, to create an enabling environment for the promotion of climate change mitigation investments through the adoption of EE measures by SME firms in the country. This proposed Project is expected to generate significant energy savings and reduce greenhouse gas emissions via private sector investments in energy efficiency, as demonstrated by the Global Climate Finance Innovation Lab.

## B.I. Description of Financial Elements of the Project / Programme

Under the proposed project, GCF grant resources (USD 17 million) will be used to support the structuring of the risk-in-projects and/or projects that are expected to generate energy savings through the use of technologies eligible under the EEE investment projects, but also to develop a pipeline of technically robust, bankable, EEE projects. GCF grant resources (USD 20 million) will be used for the development of a dedicated revolving fund in Bandalé, which will provide project-level operating guarantees from the Republic of El Salvador. The proceeds of the GCF loan resources, which will be maintained in a dedicated revolving fund by Bandalé, will be blended with an equivalent amount of BANDÉS’s own resources (USD 20 million) to support the structuring and development of EEE projects that are eligible under the EEE investment projects in El Salvador. Any loan recapitalizations and re-payments by third-party LFs will be used to finance eligible EEE projects.

In particular, GCF resources will support BANDÉS in:

(i) Developing a financing structure, including tailored credit and risk sharing instruments (concessional credit line and/or equity investment, as well as debt financing), as well as non-financial, such as standardized instruments to support the deployment of the insurance product and the demand for finance EEE investments.

(ii) Engaging LFs in the deployment of the new financial, non-financial, and risk mitigation products;

(iii) Identifying and engaging technology providers and other key market stakeholders to support the demand for financing and investment in energy efficiency and renewable energy projects;

(iv) Developing standards and mechanisms for adequate monitoring, evaluation, and verification of energy savings resulting from supported SME EEE investment projects;

(v) Deploying the credit risk mitigation product that is available in the market, which together with the risk-sharing instruments and the non-financial mechanisms form an integrated strategy to promote LFs further engagement and financing of eligible EEE private sector investment projects.

Activities will be implemented through a combination of financial and non-financial instruments, including long-term funding at adequate conditions for BANDÉS to:

* Address real or perceived risks and barriers that are currently preventing EE investments by SMEs. The funds will be used by BANDÉS, so they are interested and engage to in turn lend to SMEs interested in financing EEE eligible investment projects.

### 4. Validation and verification mechanisms
A set of technology-specific, independent validation procedures will be carried out by a third party with strong credentials on EE normalization and certification. Its role will include: a) validation of the ability of the project to achieve energy savings; b) validation that the equipment has been installed according to the proposal and that the old equipment has been properly decommissioned and disposed of to avoid undue environmental impacts and ensure the expected energy saving effects; c) verification that the equipment has been installed in accordance with the case of confirming the SME and the LFI on the expected energy savings. The validation mechanisms provide the investing SME as well as to the LFI with the assurance and trust that all EE investments will be properly implemented, and that the project will achieve its expected energy saving effects.

### 5. Capacity building
The proposed Project is expected to develop the capacity of ESPTs to develop a new line of business - the sale guaranteed energy savings rather than just energy efficient technologies. Also, it should support the development of SMEs' capacity in EE investments and financing through training on EE investment planning, monitoring, reporting and verification methodologies, and EES products, among relevant stakeholders. These efforts will be carried out by ESPTs and LFI. The capacity building efforts are expected to help both parties to better understand the experience of the risks and returns associated with this type of project. Together, these capacity building efforts should lead to an increased awareness of EE investments as a viable business opportunity for SMEs and ESPTs, which will actively promote the adoption and financing of EE measures to their potential SME clients, respectively. The capacity building will include gender perspectives that will enable women-owned and -led SMEs benefit from loans offered by the Project while ensuring the commitment of LFI to provide insurance compensation for specific EE projects by women-owned and -led SMEs and promotion of these types of projects and beneficiaries with LFIs.

One salient feature of the proposed project is that the risks associated with each sub-project are shared by the different players involved in its design and implementation.

SMEs take out loans with LFs against their balance sheets to finance their EE projects. However, unlike alternative financing schemes, part of the risk of the projects is now assumed by the ESF through a performance contract which stipulates both the environmental and financial performance criteria for the project. The ESF has decided that it will not be able to continue its policy to be contracted by ESTPs in favor of SME clients investing in EE eligible projects. In addition, for an SSE owner who is not an established expert, the ESF will require a second opinion on the technical quality and the environmental impact of the project structured by experts: i) both the technical quality of the projects and of the experts who structured them are being validated by an independent entity specializing in the normalization and certification of standards in the energy sector; ii) if there is any conflict arises between an SME and an ESTP regarding energy savings attained, an independent entity specialising in the normalization and certification of standards in the energy sector will rule on the case.

Apart from the real guarantees demanded to grant a loan to SMEs, LFs would be reassured on several grounds that the loan is likely to be repaid. First, the projects were structured by experts in EE, and both the technical quality of the projects and of the experts who structured them are being validated by an independent entity specializing in the normalization and certification of standards in the energy sector. Second, if actual energy savings do not materialize, the shortfall will be covered by additional financial guarantees and it is not possible to default through calling upon the ESF-surety policy.

The insurance or surety company that provides the ESF with this guarantee is likely to be reminded that the ESTP policy will not be called due to any of the following reasons: First, if the ESF is based on a surety, the surety company is prohibited from calling upon the ESF-surety real guarantees in order to issue the policy, discouraging those players from letting the policy to be called up. Second, the insurance or surety company is aware that the projects are being structured by experts in EE and both the technical quality of the projects and of the experts who structured them are validated by an independent entity specializing in the normalization and certification of standards in the energy sector; and if a conflict arises between an SME and an ESTP regarding energy savings attained, an independent entity specialising in the normalization and certification of standards in the energy sector will rule on the case.

Finally, ESFPA, apart from benefitting from increased demand for its products and services, have the assurance that their performance contracts are being structured by experts in EE and fully comply with the environmental impact assessment criteria. In addition, if actual energy savings exceed contracted energy savings, the excess is shared by the SME and the ESF-surety.

### Structure of the Salvadoran Financial System

The Salvadoran financial system consists of a total of 43 entities, including bank deposits, cooperative banks, and savings and loans associations. All of them are supervised and controlled by the Superintendence of the Financial System of El Salvador.

These entities consist with the other types of entities: (i) savings and loans cooperative societies (credit unions / workers banks); and savings and credit cooperative associations; and (ii) federations of cooperative banks. The savings and loans cooperative societies are supervised by the Superintendence of Credit Unions and Workers Banks – Federación de Crédito Cooperativo, and are first-tier clients of BANDESAL. It is very important to note that all the work of these entities is supervised by the Superintendence of the Financial System.

According to June 2013 figures, the total assets (TA) of the Salvadoran financial system amounted to USD15,229 million, equivalent to 81.6% of GDP at current prices. Within this total, the banking system accounted for 94% of TA, equivalent to 77.1% of GDP; savings and loans cooperative societies accounted for 3.0% of TA, equivalent to 2.5% of GDP; and credit unions accounted for 3.4%, equivalent to 2.8% of GDP.

The banking system is the largest component of the financial system with USD13,016 million, concentrated mainly in deposits from the public, which reached USD9,334 million (90% of TA). Finally, the consolidated patrimony of the banking system totaled USD1,876 million.

Regarding non-banking financial entities, their capital amounted to USD281 million in 2013; they held deposits of USD575 Million and their loan portfolio reached USD1,059 million, distributed over 205,421 loans. These figures show that such entities are focused primarily on granting small loans to its members.

### Recent Performance of the Salvadoran Financial System

Recent data shows that the system remains well capitalized and continues to enjoy comfortable levels of profitability and liquidity. The return on assets (ROA) has been stable at 0.8% in recent years, with a slight increase from -125 basis points over the regulatory minimum of 12%. A relationship between the value of its liabilities and its assets of 64%, represents a good level of solvency for the system.

The high level of liquidity has been associated with an increase in deposits. The return on equity has exceeded 18% since 2013, reflecting the profitability of the financial intermediation process. In addition, the value of the gross portfolio that fell from the 4.01% in December of this year as a result of the international financial crisis, to 2.94%, in March 2013, has recovered and reached 5.78%. The return on equity has also increased from -6.2% in January 2013 to +20% of total investments and in conjunction with cash balances represents 18.4% of TA; and a recent trend towards the increase in profitability is observed, as evidenced by the ROE of 5.7%, which has reached 11%. The return on equity reached 41% by December 2013, an increase of 500 basis points in relation to the low level registered in March 2011 in response to the consequences of the international financial crisis. El Salvador, from this perspective, has little financial depth.

Not only is the financial depth of El Salvador low for a country at its level of economic development, but most of the credit granted by banks is concentrated in short-term instruments. The average term of loans granted by credit institutions is 28 months according to data from the SSF. This is due in part to most of its liabilities being concentrated in very short-term instruments. ## C1. Strategic Context

El Salvador has a small, open, and dollarized economy with a productive structure geared toward services and consumption, and a heavy dependence on the United States economy. Following a 3.1% contraction in gross domestic product (GDP) of El Salvador in 2009 due to the global financial crisis, GDP growth rebounded strongly in 2010.

Although its annual growth rate is still relatively low compared to both its own historical averages and those of other countries of similar development level in Central America and the Caribbean, the economy is expected to grow at annual rates of between 3.5% and 4%.

Saldavarian firms generally have a limited ability to innovate owing to the obsolescence of their equipment and the prevailing lack of investment in research and development (R&D). The adoption of more productive and efficient business models is due largely to low levels of investment, resulting from limited access to medium-term credit. In addition, there are also high costs associated with obtaining financing for long-term investments, which limits the possibilities for expansion. Irrespective of the various factors that may undermine their productivity and competitiveness, access to investment credit could ease their transition to technologically more efficient and competitive business models, as well as help them reduce their dependence on imported inputs and energy.

As regards the financial constraints that firms confront the same, additional investment credit restrictions with other more traditional types of investments. In this context, the blending of GCF resources with BANDESAL’s own resources will contribute to improving access to credit at adequate terms and conditions by clients to undertake investment projects in eligible EE technologies.

Regarding its alignment with existing national and sectorial policies and strategies, the proposed project is aligned with the National Energy Policy (2013-2025), which aims to promote energy efficiency and renewable energy resources in the public and private sectors by improving the existing normative and regulatory framework, providing incentives for EE investments, and implementing awareness campaigns on the need of more rational use of limited energy resources.

It is worth pointing out that within the context of EE (EE) 2035 has already been working to improve the legislative and regulatory framework in India. In March 2014, the Ministry of Power launched a draft Energy Efficiency Bill which is a parliament that seeks to promote the efficient use of energy in the public and private sectors through, among other things, the adoption of EE standards, the establishment of an EE certification system, and the creation of an Energy Efficiency Development Bank (IDB) and the German Agency for International Cooperation (GIZ).

More recently, the Ministry of Industry and Trade has launched a National Action Plan on Energy Efficiency (for Technical Regulation), aimed to work on the EE standards of several technologies (refrigeration, air conditioners, and electrical motors) which were found to be responsible for the major consumption of energy in El Salvador: improving, regulating, and enforcing EE standards will contribute to reducing energy consumption by 10% over a period of five years. The plan also includes terms and conditions available for individuals and firms to adapt them. Thus, the eventual establishment of EE standards for providing incentives for EE investments is expected to be one of the most important actions that should be expected in terms of EE in the different technologies. The existence of EE standards, together with the associated certification system, will contribute to reducing energy consumption by 10% over a period of five years.

C2. Project / Programme Objective against Baseline

Despite high relative energy costs and SMEs' interest in renewing old technology with newer, more efficient energy technologies, there is a significant barrier to EE/SME investments hindered by constraints and perceived risks by SMEs and financial institutions. From the customer/firm perspective, the aforementioned market study showed that the perceived barriers are: (i) lack of trust in EE equipment suppliers; (ii) concerns about the reliability of EE performance, their unavailability on the local market, and uncertainty over the reliability of their installation by ESTPs (see also page 36-39 of market assessment in Annex Z).

EE/SMEs would benefit significantly from EE—but the market is currently underserved. The market study on EE barriers highlighted that the preparation of this project identified that one of the most important barriers for firms' investments in EE equipment was the lack of trust by potential beneficiaries. There is the belief that EE investments are not likely to result in significant energy savings, and that the payback period will be too long. In addition, many SMEs surveyed were concerned about the equipment performance and quality of service of ESTPs (see in particular responses in Annex Z, Figure 19 page 29).

As a critical component, LFs need to apply a traditional "asset-based" lending approach when financing EE projects, limiting the loan amount to a maximum of $60k of the value of the assets financed. Those institutions give little or no collateral value to EE equipment and, in spite of the cash flow generated by EE investment projects to invest firms' money, most LFs are reluctant to lend to SMEs because they do not have sufficient information on how to repay their loans or increase their borrowing capacity. Furthermore, even if they would accept to consider such value, the inability to provide collateral is a major barrier for SMEs in accessing financing from LFs. This is especially true when viable financial business opportunities. Consequently, LFs tend assign little or no value to the cash flow generated by EE investment projects, and this requires firms to incur their internal credit capacity to finance such projects.

The lack of trust by beneficiaries towards EE equipment suppliers is a major barrier for SMEs' investments in EE, and the perceived risks are: (i) low performance of EE equipment; (ii) high costs of EE equipment; (iii) need to secure external guarantees of a certain level of energy savings (impact in the performance of EE projects and ESTPs are typical barriers to investments in EE projects in El Salvador as well as in many other countries); (iv) lack of trust in the quality of service provided by ESTPs. These risks can be mitigated by specialized Energy Service Companies (ESCOs) under a performance contract. However, in El Salvador there are neither ESCO firms, nor standardized performance contracts for EE investment projects.

The measures proposed by this project aim to address these barriers by providing a concessional line of financing to kick start the promotion of this type of investment and by creating risk-sharing instruments and non-financial mechanisms that could help to build trust among investors and their financiers, paving the way for future investments. If LFs and other financial institutions are convinced that SMEs and their investors and their financiers are aware of the low risks and high returns of these projects, they would continue to invest in and finance them, regardless of whether or not there is a performance contract with public sector entities. This summary examines how the proposed project addresses those barriers and risks that hinder EE investments by SMEs in El Salvador, relating those constraints to the proposed instruments and mechanisms of the Project and their specific outputs.

### D.1. Value Added for GCF Involvement

By financing the design and implementation of BANDESAL EE Financing Strategy (ESI scheme), the GCF will significantly contribute to the market development and increase of private sector investments in EE. 2. ### D.2. Exit Strategy

Sustainability of this Project is ensured by the fact that the financial and non-financial instruments promoted by the ESI Scheme are expected, over time, to allow different market players (SMEs & LFs) to reduce their EE investment risk perception and increase their willingness to invest in EE projects.

The proposed standardized instruments (performance contract, validation methodology, and insurance policy), resulting from the activities in the grant component, ensure that risks are allocated to parties best able to manage these risks. The performance contracts will provide a framework for the interaction between LFs and SMEs, including processes of energy savings calculation, validation, and loan provision for EE projects, and establish a method to address lower than expected savings. The instruments create trust and clear procedures for the interaction between actors in the EE market.

The high energy price in El Salvador makes EE investments economically viable without GCF support at current interest rates, while the current lack of long-term credit to firms constitutes a real barrier as cash flows saving from EE projects are not sufficient to cover the cost of financing. The ESI model is designed with two main goals: aiming at increasing the financing experience of LFs with EE investments, allowing for the provision of long-term credit, and providing a framework for the interaction between LFs and SMEs. The combination of high energy prices and IRR of EE technologies suggest that the ESI model favors the transition towards a purely private market, after initial GCF support is phased out.

In summary, once the Project (once the GCF financing is repaid), should allow for LFs to be engaged in financing these types of projects even without the dedicated second tier credit line from BANDESAL, as they would have acquired sufficient experience in financing EE projects and accounting the energy savings in cash flows.

### E2.3 Contribution to the creation of an enabling environment

A key element of the new business model is an insurance product covering projected energy savings for specifically defined EE investments, which would be underwritten by a standard contract between firms and technology solution providers. It seeks to overcome barriers that businesses and technology solution providers encounter when making EE investments, such as lack of access to financing, lack of information on EE measures, and the perception that the bank or the bank that energy savings will be sufficient to pay back the financing, while also building the capacity and credibility of technology solution and energy service providers. The enabling environment created through the GCF funds will allow for a more efficient and effective market.

Despite the high energy prices in El Salvador, and the resulting high profitability of EE investments, these investments are not materializing. The creation of standardized instruments (performance contract, validation methods, and financing) is expected to increase the demand for EE investments and in the long-term promote EE investment demand and financing supply without public support.

The ESI model emphasizes the use of standardized approaches that minimize transaction costs. This is enabled by local standards and regulations, which are currently not aligned with international best practices. To date include: air-conditioning, electric motors, industrial boilers, refrigeration, and refrigerator systems. The use of standardized approaches is expected to allow the familiarization of the market with these investments and the standardization of transaction costs.

The enabling environment that the Project will generate will have such a transformational impact that, once the GCF contribution finalizes, LFs would be engaged in financing this type of projects even without the second tier credit line being available.

## The Economic and Financial Analysis

For the loan portion assessed the impact of the financing line as well during its estimated life. The economic analysis includes an assessment of the expected value of the GCF participation with concessional funding.10 As explained in section E.3 above and in more detail in annex 3, GCF funds would further reduce the final market interest rates offered by the LFs to the SMEs. The estimated current market LFI rate is 8.4% (calculated based on the current market LFI SPREAD of 150bps) and the interest rates offered by BANDESAL, with the Project GCF intervention, would be 6.9%.

Most importantly, beyond reducing the interest rate for financing EE projects, the GCF intervention will have two key impacts: (i) it will allow the LFs to borrow at longer-maturity terms higher than 4 years, which is necessary for EE investments with payback periods usually longer than four years and decreases the payback period of EE projects; and (ii) it will allow the LFs to borrow at lower interest rates.

EE investments considered for the Project are financially viable, based on IRR and NPV, without the GCF intervention. The expected IRR, without the GCF intervention, varies from 30.1% (for boilers) to 36% (for electric motors). The net present value (NPV), without the GCF intervention, is positive for all EE technologies considered (except for air conditioning equipment). Despite the high IRR and NPV, these investments currently do not occur in the Salvadoran market due to financial and non-financial barriers that the GCF intervention seeks to address. In particular, at the current market rates, the payback period of between 3.1 years (for electric motors) and 5.7 years (for boilers) inhibits the ability of firms to fund these investments.

With the GCF intervention, the expected IRR for all technologies is a range of 32% (for boilers) to 38.4% (for motors), with an air conditioning technology showing no improvement in return on investment by technology, but the payback period is shorter for each technology, with the largest impact on motors.

The GCF grant funding will allow the structuring of the risk transfer mechanism which will remove the non-financial barriers. As explained in section E.1., not addressing these barriers would lead to limited success of the EE credit line.

# F2. Technical Evaluation

The feasibility study and market analysis, which were completed to assess the business opportunities to finance EE in SMEs in El Salvador, identified high EE investment potential for the replacement of four technologies: air-conditioning, motors, refrigeration, and boilers.

Efficient technologies to replace the current used air-conditioning, motors, refrigeration, and boilers are proven, well developed and available in the local market through import. They require a deployment of technology, rather than a change in production processes, and their efficiency and savings potential assessment is simple compared to complex multi-measure activities.

The proposed EE intervention aims to finance EE equipment applications that will be evaluated by a recognized third-party validation entity in order to be included in the BANDESAL Project and access the credit line of the Project. The following specifications will be evaluated and included in the validation procedure:

* A minimum energy saving threshold for each technology.
* For example, a new EE industrial boiler would have to deliver at least 10% more efficiency than the old one.
* The EE equipment that will replace the old equipment should be new and from a recognized manufacturer, that is able to provide a warranty.
* The new EE equipment must also provide at least the same service and capacity relative to the replaced system.
* The EE calculations and baseline estimations have to be in accordance with a predefined methodology based on ISO 5001 standards.

The replaced equipment has to be disposed of and disabled to avoid its further use (see paragraph 13 in Annex 6 for details).

# RISK ASSESSMENT AND MANAGEMENT

## GREEN CLIMATE FUND FUNDING PROPOSAL | PAGE 57 OF 72

### Mitigation Measures

A key goal of the Project and its activities is to demonstrate the profitability of EE and address the currently high risk perception of LFs towards EE investments through an ESI. This demonstration is expected to lead to transformative change in the financing market, as LFs would start to finance EE investments from their own resources.

To mitigate the risk that this goal is not achieved, the Project design contemplates two measures:

1. **Lfs gain experience with EE investments using loan resources and risk-sharing mechanisms** which let them experiment with new business models and instruments. This will help LFs to replace the lack of internal capacity to evaluate risks. Outsourcing this task to the validator, and covering the costs through a performance contract, will allow LFs to focus on their core business while developing and performance contract concerns. These instruments will be developed and immediately usable for the private market. The outsourcing effect will be to lower the entry cost and barriers for LFs for EE investments than the ESI model. This will create new opportunities for LFs to finance EE projects independently during Project support. It is expected that this experience will create internal mechanisms and incentives within the LFs to continue financing EE projects after Project completion.

2. **The key barrier to EE investment is not the interest rate, as most EE projects are profitable without concessional support**. Due to high industrial energy costs in El Salvador and the underlying high demand for EE, it would be difficult to finance EE projects at market rates with only a small amount of concessional and GCF support after the Project end. Importantly, the concessional financing provides a crucial initial and temporary incentive for the LFs to maintain their lending rates at the same level as for traditional investment projects (thus avoiding moral hazard). The GCF also provides an important source of funding for EE investments and to provide long-term maturities for credit. In the long-term, LFs are aware of the business opportunity and will compete for clients; the GCF concessional support becomes increasingly irrelevant and can thus be phased out.

The combination of risk-sharing instruments and their implementation is thus expected to lower the risk perception of LFs as EE’s realize the benefits and low entry costs of the ESI business model.

This EnPi is then used to build the Baseline and the Estimated and Actual energy uses models over a given time period.

For example: If we built the model for an electric motor it would be necessary to estimate the number of operating hours per year etc. If the equipment is used for 10 hours, per day, 5 days a week all year, and if the outcome is 15 HP per day then the Baseline can be modelled as follows:

| 10 h * 5 days/week * 52 weeks/year = 2,600 h/year. |
| --- |

Baseline = 22.1 kWh/HP = 15 HP * 2,600 h/year

Baseline = 861.900 kWh/year

The Estimated energy use would be calculated from the EnPi provided by the manufacturer. An example for the electric motor could be EiPi (estimated) = 15 kWh/HP.

Estimated energy consumption = 15 kWh/HP * 15 HP * 2,600 h/year.

Estimated energy consumption = 585,000 kWh

Which means that the Expected energy savings would be:

Estimated Energy Savings = 861.900 kWh – 585,000 kWh

Estimated Energy Savings = 276,900 kWh

To define the Energy Savings in percentage:

Estimated energy savings (%) = Baseline - Estimated Baseline

Estimated Energy Savings = 32.12%

To calculate the Actual energy savings it is necessary to register the energy use and the output in HP of the new equipment. Normally, the technology provider would install a permanent metering system that facilitates the measurement and instantaneous calculation of energy (kWh).
