### A.2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

Please provide a brief description of the proposed project/programme, including the objectives and primary measurable benefits to be achieved. The summary should not exceed 300 words and will be elaborated in pages B,C.

The Water Sector Resilience Nexus for Sustainability in Barbados (WSRN-S Barbados)’s project will result in a paradigm shift that will enable Barbados to achieve multiple co-benefits, including: reducing greenhouse gas emissions from its drinking water supply; create resilience to severe weather impacts; reduce greenhouse gas emission; reduce consumption of fossil fuels and increase the use of renewable energy sources; improve access to safe drinking water and water sector resilience. This will be achieved by employing renewable energy technology, creating a Revolving Adaptation Fund (RAF), increasing capacity building, supporting public-private partnerships (PPPs) in climate change capacity, helping to shape policies and legislations related to climate change, raising greater awareness about climate variability and change by the population of Barbados and the wider region, and making the project scalable and replicable within other Caribbean countries.

The main objectives of the project are to:

1. Utilize renewable energy technology in response to drought conditions by utilizing clean energy resources, decentralizing water storage, promote rainwater harvesting at the household and community level, and improve the efficiency of water supply systems.
2. further advance adaptation and mitigation initiatives in the water sector of Barbados by redirecting and mobilising local funds through a revolving adaptation fund.
3. reduce greenhouse gas emissions from the production of water provision by integrating renewable energy with back-up natural gas turbines and sustainable Water Loss Reduction (WLR) initiatives.
4. contribute to climate change resilience and adaptation by creating relevant platforms within communities, educational organizations, private sector, civil society, BWA and the Government of Barbados to manage and monitor climate change impacts on water security.
5. support the review and development of a legislative framework to supports climate smart development and water security.
6. collate and disseminate lessons learnt for use in developing further adaptation and mitigation initiatives and raising greater awareness about the effects of climate variability and change by the population of Barbados and the wider region.

The project will produce four main outcomes: (1) increased/reduced reliance to fossil fuels; (2) BWA’s carbon footprint reduced; (3) improved water security, access to safe drinking water and food security; (4) swift and immediate adaptation and mitigation actions by mobilizing localised funds for climate action.

The project will also contribute towards the following Sustainable Development Goals: (1) increased use of locally generated renewable energy; (2) improved water and food security; (3) greater awareness about the effects of climate variability and change by the population of Barbados and the wider region; (4) increased capacity building, public-private-partnerships (PPP) and innovative financing in the water sector of Barbados.

The resulting impacts of this project are:

- reduced greenhouse gas emissions and the intensity of greenhouse gas emissions in the provision of water.
- improved access to safe drinking water and food security.
- swift and immediate adaptation and mitigation actions by mobilizing localised funds for climate action.
- increased use of locally generated renewable energy; (2) improved water and food security.

The major beneficiaries are the population of Barbados, which in 2016 was estimated by World bank at 284,215.

Barbados has already begun to take steps to green its economy by incorporating renewable energy into its energy mix. The recognition and production of renewable energy is growing; in 2016, a 10 megawatt solar panel system was installed at the Barbados Power Corporation (BPC) headquarters.

Going forward the engines of growth will be tourism services and construction related to tourism, infrastructure, energy and housing. Critical to realising its growth potential is a water sector, which is resilient in the face of climate change.

Barbados is ranked the 18th most densely populated country in the world, with a population density averaging 862 people/km² and a population growth rate of 0.3%. At end-2016 Barbados freshwater supply is primarily a function of rainfall and groundwater. The island’s water resources are predominantly groundwaters in considering the size of the island’s water resources; it must be borne in mind that Barbados is ranked among the world's 15 most water scarce countries. Rainfall is predominately seasonal, with two distinct wet seasons (the Northwest Monsoon season from November to April and the Dry Season (December to May). Given the impeding and increasing impacts of climate change, it is pertinent that the BWA does its Non-Return Water (NRW) and promote conservation, recycling and reuse.

The BWA obtains its water supply from twenty-four (24) groundwater wells; two (2) spring water sources and one (1) privately owned reverse osmosis desalination plant, augmented by a smaller contained reservoir. The majority of the wells are located in the western part of the island. The depth of the well varies greatly with some being as shallow as 3 meters while others extend to depths greater than 200 meters. #### Actions
Barbados strives to achieve an economy-wide reduction in GHG emissions by 44% compared to its business-as-usual scenario, which is equivalent to reducing emissions by 259 ktCO2e per year from the baseline year 2006. As an interim target, the intention will be to achieve an economy-wide reduction of 37% compared to its business as usual scenario, which is equivalent to reducing emissions by 214 ktCO2e per year from the baseline year 2006.

In order to meet these targets and reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in the energy sector, Barbados has made attempts to adapt to the effects of climate change. To address reduction in rainfall, increased drought periods are expected to occur more frequently. In response to this, Barbados is implementing desalination plants that have provided additional water on the island nation's dependence on traditional energy sources (fossil fuels). The technology also provides a means for reducing GHG emissions by up to 74% compared with fossil fuel-based power generation and can be used in conjunction with renewable energy sources such as increased water reuse (0.1-2.4 kgCO2e/kWh) (Comrie et al., 2014). Desalination technology is to become an integral part of the tool kit for combating climate change, tackling mainly the issue of water scarcity, the calming of droughts and ensuring that there will be sufficient water available in times of need. The main aim of the operations of the desalination technology is to implement and power the RO plant using renewable energy sources. Besides reducing GHG emissions, it also provides a means for increasing water security and improving the production of water in Barbados.

Due to the cost associated with maintaining and operating desalination plants, the project proposes two alternative actions to reduce this expenditure. This involves: (1) implementing water loss reduction initiatives, which includes replacing mains and implementing pressure management and leak detection technologies; and (2) decentralised collection systems for potable water supply in rural communities, schools, health facilities, government residences and critical community essential services and other localised support centers. The 2 MW systems for Belle and Hamilton will be made up of approximately 7,700 PV modules each and the 0.5MW system at Bowesman will be made up of approximately 3,000 modules. The number of modules will depend on the type of roof available and the orientation of the roof. The systems are designed to be durable and efficient in the capture of irradiance. It is also proposed that a decentralized approach be adopted for the electricity generation at the pumping stations. This involves the installation of multiple inverters, which can be connected to a centralized system that uses only one large inverter. The decentralized system has the advantage of greater reliability and mitigates the risk associated with a single point failure. In contrast, the centralized system, if an inverter fails, only a small percentage of the system and energy production is affected.

The PV systems will be ground-mounted on the adjoining land for the aforementioned pumping stations—see Annex for diagram [not provided]. At each pumping station, the decentralized approach to electricity generation involves the construction of an interior room with appropriate switch gear and the installation of electricity metering and monitoring equipment. AC power from the grid or from a solar well will be fed into the switch gear at the pumping station. The PV system will be connected to the main bus bar system for the pumping stations via a switching device called a switch (ATS), which will be coupled either with the GENESET or with the Power Utility System. Approvals are required from the Government of Barbados and the Electricity Authority of Barbados for this approach.

Further details on these approvals are given in Section C.6.

Since all three systems are grid-fed and the battery technologies associated with such systems are expensive and still evolving, it is proposed that a small amount of diesel fuel (cleaner fuel) Microbioline be used as the back-up energy supply where there is currently no alternative power supply (in the cases of Belle and Bowesman). This is to ensure the BWA continues supplying water to its customers. The battery technology will only be used in conjunction with the power utility for extended periods. The proposed design is such that once there is a failure on the utility grid, the PV system will automatically switch over to the diesel generator. This will allow the Microbioline fuelled diesel generator BWA to continue supplying water to its customers. The same will happen when the sun sets. See Annex for the electrical interaction/interconnection diagram and visual engineering design for the PV systems and the electrical interaction/interconnection with the pumping station.

As battery technology becomes more reliable and affordable, more battery backup provision will be relied on to ensure the continued advancement of resilience at pumping stations across Barbados.
