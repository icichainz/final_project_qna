# A.19. Complementarity and coherence

The GAA Fund provides opportunities for NCD countries to broaden the menu of climate finance tools available to them. GAIA's principal focus on adaptation investments, partnered towards this objective with many specialised local and regional partners, will be an important complement to the existing portfolio of projects in the GCF pipeline that significantly expand the availability of both implementation finance and technical assistance.

* It will provide an incremental source of climate finance that countries will be able to access.
* It will provide long-term debt finance in local and hard currency, without a mandatory repayment obligation.
* In countries with few or no CCRD accredited entities that currently have the capacity to implement projects under the GCF, it will help bridge the gap between existing gaps in the international climate finance architecture.

The Fund will also be designed to provide additional support for NCD countries by making it easier and more cost-effective (and thus more likely) to support beneficiaries through project development, structuring and financing, and construction stages, to ensure that projects are completed on time and within budget, while ensuring financial sustainability.
* It will facilitate appropriate knowledge generation and dissemination efforts, such as through aggregation of outputs and outcomes from financed projects and sharing lessons learned with other NCD countries.
* It will engage other funds and financiers in the projects it co-finances to align the GCF's investment strategy with that of other funds and financiers.
* It will also engage with a broad network of partners with specific sector or geographical expertise.

Please see the full description of complementarity with related projects/Interventions outlined in Section B.1.

## Climate Change Problem

The consequences of climate change are upon us, evident in rising sea levels, extreme weather events, acute temperature ranges and highly variable rainfall patterns. The dramatically increased incidence of major climate-related disasters has led to a significant increase in the number of people displaced by natural hazards. These impacts result in floods, droughts, heatwaves, storms, and wildfires. Climate change also exacerbates costly social environment risks such as water scarcity, food insecurity, conflicts, and interlinked production systems that are vulnerable to climate change impact on land use and physical risks. Estimates of the annual cost of adaptation are between USD1400bn and USD3650bn by 2030.

## Proposed Interventions

GAIA is a country platform that enables deployment of long-term loans into climate adaptation and mitigation investments from sources previously not available to NCD countries, providing local authorities with new tools and resources for addressing the impacts of climate change. The urgency of the need for action on climate change, just as the urgency of emergency response becomes more acute in line with the urgent imperative evoked above, places a premium on the speed at which adaptation and mitigation investments can be made available. This is reflected in the allocation to LCDS and SDS (minimum 25% of portfolio expenditure). The selection of target Countries was initiated by an initial screening process followed by a more detailed country-level due diligence and project-level screening. The selected Countries were determined by also applying various factors (such as credit ratings, progress on climate change action plans, etc.) to ensure that the investments have the impact goal of GAIA to catalyse low-carbon and climate-resilient development through its capital deployment.

Most of the targeted countries by GAIA fall within GCF’s Group Priority Countries (Africa, LDCs, SIDS). GAIA will not compete with other GCF projects in these countries. The GCF has already demonstrated that it is a historically inadequate contributor of private sector partners to climate investments: less than 8% according to CDP (2017). This is due to the fact that the private sector is often reluctant to invest in developing countries, profiles associated with often unfamiliar sectors of intervention and, in many cases, less familiar jurisdictions for global investors. In addition, in many developing countries, there are few or no established mechanisms to attract private sector experience and resources.

Private sector investments that are structured are structures on the GAIA platform will be more sustainable and affordable than the private sector has previously been able to deliver. Local currency financing will be available to a greater extent through the use of local financial intermediaries (LFI) in the target countries, with access to hard currency financing and profiled to be more flexible and manageable.

GAIA will be a country-level platform to source, refine and originate climate change investments from a broad-based network of local financial intermediaries in economies where it is not an agent of NCD. The platform will tend to lead to co-sovereign and sub-sovereign borrowing partners. This decentralised approach to investment sourcing and origination will be more efficient than the current GCF model, which relies on a centralised, heightened awareness-of-public-private solutions for climate risk management that enable scale and diversification of investments. The platform will also provide a greater degree of flexibility in terms of the financial mechanisms embedded (in the structure), the platform will introduce a flexible complement to other sources of climate finance. This will allow GAIA to be more responsive to the needs of NCD countries, which are often unable to advance a vital paradigm shift, unlocking private sector financial support for investments in climate-resilient infrastructure, energy systems, agriculture and social systems investment.

Climate risks addressed by GAIA are not necessarily systemic in nature at the transaction and country-program level. In contrast to more well-established practices targeting the low-carbon transition, climate mitigation and adaptation are often seen as a separate agenda. However, the current state of affairs is an emerging specialisation. GAIA will have to be responsive to evolving country requests aligned with their NDCs and other national priorities. In recognizing the challenge of bringing forward impactful projects in these new financing spaces, an independent parallel Technical Assistance Facility will be established, financed by public sector, philanthropic sources and ongoing external funding from GCF. The TA Facility will provide technical assistance to countries in preparing proposals for GAIA's project identification, screening and origination processes. The associated institutional learning by local project proponents, promulgated to other institutions and projects and underpinned by standardized contracting documents, will contribute significantly to the development of the climate finance ecosystem.

Bringing together public, private and philanthropic partners, all of whom are investing their own funds, mobilized by the GCF, is a key feature of the GAIA proposal. This unique structure allows for the creation of a new financing architecture that addresses the adaptation financing gap. GCF's investment of USD150M legitimizes the financing structure at the country level and provides a foundation for scaling up climate finance in developing countries. It also serves as the foundation of the capital stock and makes possible the significant leverage provided by senior institutions and commercial banks, which are expected to contribute an additional USD375M (USD225M from each institution). This contribution, along with the USD100M that GCF has already committed, represents a total investment of USD625M. The GAIA proposal is designed to leverage this capital stock and make it available for climate adaptation projects in developing countries.

GAIA's vision originates from a close partnership between MUFG, Canada’s bilateral development bank, FinDev Canada, and Other International Financial Institutions (IFIs). This collaboration brings together the financial sector expertise of these institutions with geographical reach and technical expertise (e.g. UNDP, GGGI, UN IAF) in adaptation and resilience to support the meaningful deployment of GAIA's capital.

GAIA is a blended finance facility mandated to finance projects within energy generation/access (MR1A) and low-emission transport (MR2A) segments which may also have resilience co-benefits, while priviling three adaptation results areas: health, food security, and water security. GAIA will be structured as a common equity tranche focused on LDCs or SIDS by providing a “full lifecycle” project financing solution that responds to investors with different risk/remit profiles (ranging from philanthropists to public sector investors) as presented in Figure 2.

The concessional debt tranche provided by MUFG, FinDev Canada and Other Investors will be accompanied by a junior concessionary equity tranche which we propose to subscribed by GCF and supplemented by additional donors, including the World Bank.

A parallel TA facility funded by FinDev Canada and other donors will strengthen the transaction process, improve project climate impacts and success rate, and ensure that projects incorporate ESS and gender considerations. FinDev Canada will lead the design of this facility.

Borrowers will benefit from MFX Solutions’ hedging services. MFX is a socially-oriented company that supports impact investing with hedging products for thinly-traded currencies, for which currency risk is more difficult to cover. Where hedging costs lead to an increase in the cost of borrowing, GAIA will provide a subsidy to the borrower equal to the available borrowing rates within the country ensuring that borrowers can take advantage of local currency financing at competitive rates.

Finally, additional credit enhancement will be taken on a case-by-case basis at the project level or via an increase at the insurance/guarantee tranche. This could be through sourcing single name commercial credit or political insurance. The decision to provide this credit enhancement is subject to the recommendation to the Credit Committee which will also need to take into account the portfolio composition (e.g., to mitigate country or sector concentration, manage the credit-worthiness of the portfolio, etc.).

### D.2. Paradigm shift potential (max. 500 words, approximately 1 page)

#### Innovation

GAIA’s innovative financing model will mobilize private sector capital from large institutional investors by using blended finance and a new platform to address climate change and associated market failures, including difficult-to-manage FX risk and limited institutional capacity for investment. GAIA engineers a new paradigm that combines the strengths of both public and private sectors in order to provide sufficient scale and adequate financing to the countries. This does while also making investments that adhere to an impact measurement framework (IMF) and align with the Paris Agreement, as well as other international development goals and climate change development impacts/outcomes. This approach of marrying impact and climate finance is innovative for private sector investment in developing countries.

Our research indicates that no such structure has yet been created. While impact investing platforms with institutional investors involvement exist, they are either (a) not initiated by institutional investors; or (b) focused essentially on generating returns. GAIA’s platform will be the first of its kind to combine both elements: it is a new paradigm for financing at scale and will be the first large-scale USD1B+ impact platform that (a) stems from institutional investors-led initiative, (b) has a fully committed structure capable of making long-term illiquid loans in both local and hard currency. GAIA’s platform will also be the first to combine climate finance with an IMF-aligned impact measurement framework.

The philosophy and vision underlying GAIA is to become a lasting and replicable platform that will catalyze private capital into the developing world, while providing a new model for climate finance. ## Absence of alternative sources of financing

Climate finance accounts for approximately 7% of global climate finance. Annual adaptation costs in developing countries are estimated between USD150–USD330bn by 2030. By date, adaptation finance has been provided almost entirely through official development assistance (ODA) and multilateral development banks (MDBs), totaling around $4 billion. Developing countries have relied on scarce sources of domestic and public funding to support their climate actions.

Developing countries lack the ability to access long-term financial in local currency. Of all reported debt issuances in GAIA Platform countries, 85% were issued with a maturity of 10 years or less. Further, public sources of finance, such as aid, are often provided on terms that do not match the needs of developing countries and are denominated in foreign currency. Reported debt securities in GAIA Platform countries between January 2018 and March 2022 showed that 95% were issued in foreign currencies.

### Financial viability and other financial indicators
GAA will operate on fully commercial terms, ensuring a market clearing return for end borrowers and commercial investors. The expected annualised rate of return is approximately 8%. If the GCF’s concessional capital achieves its targeted return, the excess spread generated by the junior concessional capital will be used to build a reserve account to replenish the fund in case of any future drawdowns. This will ensure that the fund remains viable and sustainable for the perpetuity and sustainability of the platform throughout.
GAA’s guiding principle is to optimise use of its concessional capital as efficiently as possible over time.

## GAIA E&S and Human Rights (E&HR) Policies

- Apply transparency* on risks and impacts that are scaled, risk-based and fit for purpose approach.

### Continuous Improvement

- Ensure continuous improvement of E&S management systems through periodic assessments, minimize adverse impacts and enhance positive effects on the environment, workers, and stakeholders.

### Benchmarking

- Set in place appropriate E&S benchmarks and realize the follow-up of E&S measures throughout the investment process.
- Identify vulnerable people or groups who may be impacted by projects' activity and attach more attention to meet their obligations under international human rights law.

- GAIA does not tolerate any forms of SEAR through its management, operational and project implementation cycle. Any form of discrimination, harassment, abuse, exploitation that are an unacceptable behaviour, and violate human dignity generating a culture of domination and privation.

- Approved Projects are required to structure environmental and social guidance to enhance GAIA's capacity for positive impact.

### Environmental and Social Evaluation Cycle

- Environmental and social evaluation and elements of the Human Rights Policy of the proposed projects is embedded in each stage of the project cycle, from the initial screening phase through to the final approval during the Screening phase and ending with Environmental and Social benchmarks monitoring during project implementation.

# INTRODUCTION

Climate change is not gender neutral. The economic and physical consequences of a changing climate, such as increased temperatures, extreme weather events, and rising sea levels, are felt more acutely by women on the poor than on the rich and 70% of the world's poor are women (International Union for Conservation of Nature, 2015). The impacts of climate change on women are complex and inter-related. As they face multiple vulnerabilities, women are also often responsible for adapting to these changes and with-and exacerbate other factors contributing to marginalization, including age, race, socio-economic class, gender inequality, and lack of access to resources.

The importance of understanding the role that different factors play in explaining why women and girls are more vulnerable than men to the impacts of climate change. The impacts of climate change on women and girls differ from those experienced by men due to a range of factors which explain why women and girls are more vulnerable than men to the impacts of climate change.

1. First, the climate crisis exacerbates gender inequality and makes it harder to achieve gender justice. Women and girls are disproportionately responsible for collecting water and fuel for cooking and for caring for children and the elderly which makes it harder and more cumbersome to leave home. Women also play a significant role in gender-based violence including domestic violence, forced marriage and trafficking.

2. Second, social and cultural norms and barriers mean that women are less likely to be involved or decision about how to prevent, mitigate and cope with climate change, including leaving their homes.

3. Third, women and girls do most of the subsistence farming in poor countries and are the primary producers of food for their families and communities. Women and girls also play a significant role on the frontlines when it comes to combating climate change. They help protect the food and nutrition security of their families and communities and play a critical role in overcoming the challenges posed by climate change.

It is therefore imperative that climate mitigation and adaptation activities are not only gender-sensitive but additionally include targeted actions that advance gender equality and women's economic empowerment, including through increased access to finance. The GAA was developed by MUSIK, FinDev Canada and others (Sponsors) with a strong commitment to gender mainstreaming, equity, diversity and inclusion.

## GAIA OVERVIEW

Project GAIA is a blended finance platform that enables sophisticated institutional investors to deploy significant private sector capital in local and hard currency loans to finance climate adaptation, mitigation projects and activities. The project will be implemented through the Least Developed Countries (LDCs) and Small Island Developing States (SIDS) collectively ("Recipient Countries") to help them meet their Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) and National Adaptation Plans (NAPs). Co-benefits such as gender equality, diversity of access, and poverty reduction will be considered during the due diligence and evaluation of projects. These underlying benefits will be reflected in the recommendation to proceed to next steps as described in Annex 5 under "Outcomes of Project Screening".

1. Attuned to the needs of both men and women.
2. Adapting planned activities and operations where the needs of women are different than those of men.
3. Reaching beyond protection of the status quo to create improved, sustainable outcomes for women.

GAIA APPROACH TO GESI ASSESSMENT

Broadly speaking, the approach to both GAIA and GESI assessment draws on international best practices, developed by leading international organizations such as UN Women and multi-stakeholder initiatives. The GAIA approach is designed to provide guidance and resources for sector-specific key resources that will be used when screening projects in a corresponding sector. The goal of the assessment guidelines is to ensure that consistent international standards are applied and are then translated into national context, with the aim of ensuring consistency across sectors.

As noted above, the development of GESI assessments and action plans requires the full participation of Project Entities and their project teams. This process also requires a deep understanding of systemic gender inequality, as well as the risks and opportunities associated with specific GESI issues. The GAIA Platform provides guidance and resources to support these engagements via the parallel GAIA and GESI assessment processes. In this way, the development of sector-specific GESI action plans through the investment process can remediate these shortcomings where issuers and Project Entities are not fully aware.

The GAIA approach to GESI begins at initial project screening and moves into significant depth and detail at the Project Entity level during the Secondary Environmental and Social Due Diligence, (please refer to Annex 1 of the GAIG of the Funding Proposal[6] for more details on the GESI requirements during the investment cycle).

# Impact Statement:

GAIA is a blended finance platform that enables sophisticated institutional investors to deploy significant private sector capital in local and hard currency loans to finance climate adaptation, mitigation and cross-cutting projects in Emerging Markets (EMs), including in Least Developed Countries (LDCs) and Small Island Developing States (SIDS) (collectively “Recipient Countries”) to help them meet their Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs). The blended finance platform will be structured as a multi-donor trust fund, with contributions from multiple donors. These contributions will be considered in the evaluation of projects. These underlying benefits will be reflected in the recommendation to proceed to next steps as described in Annex 6 under “Outcomes of Project Screening”. Specific actions to be taken post-approval will be incorporated into a project’s Gender and Social Inclusion (GSI) Action Plan.

GAIA is designed to support the identified national climate priorities of all 19 countries as defined by their latest NDCs submitted to the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). It aims to address stated adaptation and resilience priorities and the countries’ respective GNI/CO2 emissions. The blended finance platform will be structured as a multi-donor trust fund, with contributions from multiple donors which will leverage both GCF and additional institutional, commercial, and philanthropic capital, to catalyze low-carbon growth in Recipient Countries.

At a more profound level, GAIA’s funding is meant to enhance each countries efforts to become more sustainable and inclusive and help local populations become more resilient to the adverse effects of climate change, ensuring that Recipient Countries will have stronger and broader access to resources for adaptation and resilience. The GSI Action Plan will be developed in close collaboration with the recipient country, addressing one of the critical needs identified in the Paris Agreement (Article 2.1 (c)).

In this context, the Gender and Social Inclusion Action Plans of GAIA and of all the approved projects it finances will support the core pillar of gender equality and social inclusion as defined by the UNFCCC. The GSI Action Plan will be developed in close collaboration with the recipient country, seeking to encourage increased participation of women in governance and leadership roles, job creation and career progression opportunities for women in the workforce, gender-sensitive capacity building to implement successful climate-adaptation and resilience projects and through project-level impact benefits or improvement in women’s health, well-being and education. The GSI Action Plans will be developed with the guidance of the Climate and GSI Committee and will be subject to Independent Gender Assessments. The GSI Action Plans will also include a gender equality statement and a social inclusion statement. These Statements with regard to gender are also applicable to social inclusion such that the needs and rights of Indigenous Peoples, youth, the disabled and other vulnerable groups are addressed.

The implementation of this GSA will be led by the IM which has been selected in part because of its ability to integrate a gender equality and social inclusion perspective into investment projects, included in similar countries than those ones targeted by GAIA. Since the gender equality and social inclusion perspective is an integral component of the project design, it will be reflected in the GSI Action Plan.

The GSI Action Plans will be done alongside the regular monitoring and reporting that the IM will conduct with Project Entities and with the same rigor. The IM will also be able to capitalize on the support, expertise and guidance of the members of the Climate and GSI Committee as well as of GAIA Sponsors which have strong policies and initiatives in place on equity, diversity and inclusion. Notably, gender equality and women’s economic empowerment (WEE) are strategic priorities for FinDev Canada who has a seat on the Climate and GSI Committee which applies a gender lens to all of their transactions.

## Environmental and Social Policy

This Policy outlines the E&S principles and commitments that serve as guidelines for GAIA. Proposed Projects will be analysed and evaluated according to the potential contribution of the project's activities.

GAIA is designed to enable investments in projects with specific development and climate priorities such as energy, water management, transport infrastructure, urbanization, sustainable agriculture, energy, reforestation, irrigation, and other agricultural processes (please refer Appendix 1 GAIA Proposed Projects and Activities).

## GAIA E&S Policy

GAIA's E&S Policy is approved by GAIA's management.

### Principles

1. **Legal Compliance**: All Project Entities must comply with all host country laws, regulations, and permits, including those laws that implement host country obligations under international law.
2. **Enhanced Governance (with Technical Assistance)**: Based on available resources, GAIA will support Approved Projects in developing their governance and management system through TA guidance, and collaboration with third parties. Where GAIA aims projects to adopt E&S Performance Benchmarks, it will assess the need to provide appropriate support to stimulate sector or geographic adaptation.
3. **Transboundary risks and impacts**: In case of potential transboundary impacts (e.g., GAIA-funded projects, all necessary consultations and due diligence processes, including prior notification and consultation with affected parties, must be carried out).
4. **Scaled risk-based approach**: E&S requirements will be implemented in a risk-based manner and not as a one-size-fits-all approach. This approach will require that E&S requirements and processes are commensurate with the level of risk and meet the relevant E&S standards.
5. **Fit for purpose**: In line with the Paris Agreement principle of equity and common but differentiated responsibilities, GAIA acknowledges that different countries and regions face different E&S challenges. This recognition does not change the applicable EMS or the Performance Benchmarks, nor the accountabilities of GAIA; although a reasonable timeframe for compliance may be required.
6. **Mitigation of hierarchy**: Seeking to avoid, and where this is not possible, minimize adverse impacts and enhance positive effects on the environment and affected stakeholders.
7. To contribute to the avoidance of any deterioration in the quality of human life, the environment and any loss of biodiversity and ecosystems.

#### Gender Equality and Social Inclusion Policy

GAIA’s management recognises that climate change disproportionately affects women. Indigenous and other vulnerable groups are also in the mainstream gender considerations in climate finance of the GCF.

Gender and equality policy is a key component of the GAIA Platform itself, and to disentrench Proponents who generate benefits regarding gender diversity and equality of access. GAIA’s management commit to mainstream gender and incorporate equality policies throughout the platform built and implemented by GAIA. The GAIA Platform will ensure that every project under GAIA’s watch adopt and implement gender and equality-responsive approaches.

Gender equality and women's economic empowerment are broad issues that span multiple sectors of the economy, and therefore require a multi-faceted approach to address. These issues are addressed broadly during Gender Assessment reviews and in more depth through the construction of Gender Action Plans (GAPs) and flexible goals related to the specific country, project owner and implementing entity’s context.

The high-level objectives of the Platform include:
- Encouraging women's economic participation as leaders, employees, and suppliers by promoting the adoption of gender-inclusive practices in the workplace and in supply chains.

* Promoting principles of inclusion of gender-responsive products and safeguards such that the needs and rights of Indigenous peoples, youth, persons with disabilities and other vulnerable populations are considered in all aspects of project design.

Sexual Exploitation, Sexual Abuse and Sexual Harassment (SEAH Policy)

GAIA has zero tolerance for any forms of SEAH, considering unacceptable behaviour which violates human dignity. Therefore, Project Entities will need to develop and adopt SEAH policies. Adherence to these policies must be a requirement of all project contract and procurement processes and specifically that:

1. Any risks or potential adverse impacts on women, men, girls, and boys are identified as early as possible as part of GAIA financed activities screening and reflecting in relevant safeguards instruments, including the ESAS, taking into account gender equality, diversity, and age where relevant, including Sexual Exploitation, Sexual Abuse and Sexual Harassment (SEAH).
2. The relevant safeguard instruments (including the ESAS and EMP) as appropriate) include measures to enhance gender equality, and to prevent, address and eliminate SEAH.
3. All measures to mitigate and manage the identified risks and impacts are implemented, monitored, and reported in accordance with GAIA’s policies.

In case incidences of SEAH occur, there are:

a. Established accessible and inclusive survivor-centred and gender-responsive grievance mechanisms for reporting incidents, with specific procedures for SEAH including confidential reporting with safe and ethical documenting of such cases, that indicate when and where to report incidents, and what follow-up actions will be undertaken; and
b. Modalities to provide timely referral services and redress to survivors, including as appropriate, medical care, psychological support, legal support, community driven protection measures and reintegration.

### Indigenous Peoples policy

GAIA’s Indigenous Peoples policy complies with the United Nations (UN) Indigenous Peoples Policy and its Operational guidelines for the UN Development Programme (UNDP) Corporate Standard 7 Indigenous Peoples. Projects will ensure that Indigenous Peoples do not suffer harm or adverse effects from the design and implementation of Project activities.

The definitions in Appendix 3 of the ESAS - Indigenous Peoples Framework are intended to provide guidance on how to identify, consult with, and/or engage with Indigenous communities or identification of Indigenous Peoples by a State. It will also not be limited by the legal status of titling of indigenous lands, resources, and territories.

Projects will be required to:

- benefit Indigenous Peoples in a culturally appropriate manner.
- promote empowerment of Indigenous Peoples.
- economically empower Indigenous populations and other vulnerable minorities.
- encourage the development of skills of Indigenous Peoples.


For more details on Indigenous Peoples’ Framework and other relevant deliverables required at Project level, please refer to Appendix 3 of the ESAS - Indigenous Peoples Framework.
