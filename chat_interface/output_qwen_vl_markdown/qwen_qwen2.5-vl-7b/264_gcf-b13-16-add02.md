**A2 Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)**

1. Armenia is a small, poor, land-locked country in the heart of Eurasia and is highly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. Unsustainable energy use in buildings underpins Armenia’s closely intertwined development, security and Climate Change challenges.
    - Approximately 30% of Armenian households are energy-poor, where energy poverty (often called “fuel poverty”) is defined as households spending more than 10% of their budgets on energy[2].
    - 45% of Armenian households experience temperatures in winter below 19°C (i.e. below established international standards for human occupancy)[3].
    - 50% of energy use in buildings depends on imported fossil fuels.
    - 24% of Armenia’s total greenhouse gas emissions come from buildings[4].
    - Over 50% of energy can be saved via energy efficient retrofits (Figure 1).
    
2. Improving energy efficiency (EE) in building has been assigned the highest priority in Armenia's housing, energy and climate change strategy. The European Bank for Reconstruction and Development (EBRD), its Third National Communication to the UNFCCC and the UNFCC Technology Needs Assessment.

The project aims at creating a favourable market environment and scalable business model for investment in EE buildings in Armenia. It is estimated that this project will generate approximately US$ 5.4 million of direct, and indirect emission savings over the 20-year equipment lifetime[5], green job creation and energy poverty reduction, benefitting over 200,000 people and catalyse private and public sector investment of approximately US$100 million.

[1] This definition of energy poverty, which focuses on energy expenditures relative to income, is sometimes also referred to as fuel poverty. It distinguishes it from the broader concept of energy poverty that is also concerned with a lack of access to modern energy services (e.g., electricity and clean cooking fuels) and the associated health impacts.

### Key Points:

- **Energy demand** as a result of climate change*: Average electricity tariffs for residential consumers increased by a factor of 1.8 between 2006–2014, and natural gas tariffs by a factor of 2.6 between 2007–2014.
- **Electricity prices**: A decision on July 15th 2015 by the Public Services Regulatory Commission to raise electricity prices further by 17–22% led to protests in February 2016, highlighting the vulnerability of low-income households and the significance of fuel poverty and has raised the issue to the top of the Government's agenda.
- **Buildings sector**: One of major energy consumers in Armenia. According to the 2010 World Energy Outlook, almost 39% of total final energy consumption is attributed to buildings (OECD 2010). Global GHG emissions from the building sector have more than doubled since 1970 and now represent 15% of all global GHG emissions. Armenia is a member of the Economic Community of Eastern Europe (ECA), which as Armenia, accounts for almost 40% of all non-OECD GHG emissions in the building sector*.
- **Due to Armenia's continental climate**: With a long heating season, average winter temperatures around -5°C and winter precipitation. The high energy consumption and GHG emissions in the Armenian building sector are primarily associated with space heating.
- **One sub-set of buildings with significant energy-saving potential** in Armenia are concrete panel multi-story buildings (Figure 1). These buildings were constructed between 1970–2005, and their average age is indicative of their modernisation at over 1.250 years/with a GHG reduction potential of 250 MCO2e/year.
- **Previous projects in Armenia on energy efficiency**: In buildings have targeted new buildings and energy efficiency retrofitting of public sector buildings. Private residential and industrial benefits have been largely overlooked due to the higher levels of financial risk they pose. Furthermore, whilst there are several commercial banks that provide loans for building retrofits, the financing needs of the private residential and industrial building sector is not addressed.
- **Interest rates**: Interest rates for commercial loans are very high (up to 28% per year) and with short repayment periods.

### Barriers/Risks:
Due to the presence of policy, financial, market and technical / capacity barriers, the overall investment risk profile of EE building retrofits is prohibitive in Armenia. Deriving private and public investment despite the significant energy savings potential of EE building retrofits remains a challenge. The main barriers are explained in Section 1.2 of the UNDP Project Document (Annex II to the Funding Proposal). By specifically targeting these barriers and investment risks, the project will contribute towards a commercially-driven EE building retrofit market.

### Main goal:
Scale-up investment in EE building retrofits in the cities of Armenia, and reduce the overall investment risk profile of EE building retrofits to encourage private sector investment and alleviate poverty.

### Anticipated outcomes:
- **Component 1**: Establishment of building sector MPP (Robust MPP) for the building sector established. Component 2: Policy-making National, sub-national and local authorities adopt and implement an enabling policy framework for EE retrofits.
- **Component 3**: Development of a financing mechanism to affordable capital for EE retrofits provided.
- **Component 4**: Financial incentives: Affordability of EE retrofits for the most vulnerable households ensured through targeted financial incentives to building / apartment owners (directly or via private-sector ESCOs).

### C.3 | Project / Programme Description

#### 40. Project Components, Outcomes and Outputs (for detailed information at the activity-level, please refer to Annex B, UNDP Project Document)

1. The project will use UNDP's de-risking methodology and framework for low-carbon energy investments. This methodology takes a systematic approach to identifying and quantifying investment risks, and then assembles packages of public instruments that can be used to mitigate these risks in order to unlock private sector investment.
2. The overall aim is to identify the most cost-effective package of public instruments to achieve significant scale for low-carbon energy sector investor action at scale. More information on the de-risking methodology can be found here: www.undp.org/DREI

#### 40.1 De-risking Methodology

The de-risking methodology identifies three types of public instruments. Each of these three types of public instruments addresses a different type of risk, either by removing or reducing it, either by reducing or compensating for risks. Components 2 to 4 are structured around these categories:

- **Policy de-risking instruments** refer to public interventions that reduce risk by removing the underlying barriers that prevent private sector investment in low-carbon energy projects. These can be policy changes, regulatory reforms, regulations and/or programs. An example of policy de-risking could be the introduction of standards or energy efficiency labeling.
- **Financial-de-risking instruments** refer to public interventions that transfer the financial impact of investment risks from the private sector to the public sector. These are typically financial products offered by development banks or other financial institutions, such as guarantees, loans, and equity investments. The aim is to reduce the risk to lending bodies for performing energy efficient measures.
- **Payment-for-performance instruments** refer to public interventions that de-risking or transferred through financial de-risking efforts to reduce risks can be supplemented by financial incentives to compensate for residual risks and thereby to incentivize private sector action, such as a targeted financing subsidy for certain household categories undergoing energy efficiency upgrades.

Four models have been developed, two in the residential sector (one for an individual single-family house and one for a multi-family apartment building), and two in the public sector (one for a government office building and one for a school). ## 181 The choice of discount rate for the NPV calculations is guided by which party is being affected and what the term of the investment is.
- For residential buildings, the discount rate varies considerably according to household members' perception of risk and the perception of likelihood of returns on the investment. There is a strong correlation between the discount rate and the type of building (single-family or multi-unit) and tenure (owner or renter).
- For investments in energy efficiency at the building level in multi-owner buildings, the discount rate is 17.5%.
- For households (houses and dwellings within apartment buildings), the discount rate represents the opportunity cost of other investment options. As a proxy for this opportunity cost, the interest rate on savings accounts is used as an indicator of the discount rate.

    - o This figure is consistent with that given in the EU analysis. The TE report will be made publicly available through the UNDP Evaluation Resource Centre (www.erc.undp.org) for a period of three months.
