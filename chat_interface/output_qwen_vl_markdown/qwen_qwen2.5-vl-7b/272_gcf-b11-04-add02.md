### A.2. Project / Programme Executive Summary (max 300 words)

1. The project will reduce the vulnerability of smallholder farmers to climate-related disasters by saving lives and enhancing livelihoods at risk from climate-related disasters. It will address technical, financial, capacity, and access barriers related to weather and climate information (CI) by investing in enhancing hydro-meteorological capacity for early warnings (EWs) and forecasting services, developing a demand-based model for CI, and strengthening capacities of communities to respond to climate-related disasters. The objective of the project is to reduce vulnerability of smallholder farmers to climate-related disasters through the provision of early warning systems and strengthening capacities of communities to respond to climate-related disasters.

2. The project is aligned with GCF's strategies such as the Malawi Growth and Development Strategy and the National Climate Change Policy. It will contribute to the achievement of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) by advancing a paradigm shift in the use of EWs and CI. By facilitating a demand-based model for CI information and use of EWs, it will increase access to CI services and reduce climate-related disasters. The project is expected to generate development benefits by saving lives (18 lives per year) and assets (average USD50), enhancing agricultural productivity (USD24 per farmer/year), increasing income (USD36 per farmer/year), and creating jobs (average 160,000). It yields a positive economic rate of return of 31%, leverages domestic financing (USD2.7M) and builds capacity for future investments. GCF grant resources address the project's non-revenue generating investment by supporting the non-revenue generating investments in provision of EWs and CI. The NDA has issued a no objection letter for the project.

# C1. Strategic Context

## 10. Malawi is a landlocked country with a population of just over 16 million and is expected to reach 22.8 million by 2035. Approximately 80% of its rural population lives in areas where the majority engage in subsistence farming, primarily for food security.

## The high level of subsistence farming is a major contributor to poverty with about 50% of rural population living in poverty compared to only 17% of urban population. Malawi has experienced several climate-related disasters over the past decade, including 174 out of 187 countries on the United Nations Human Development Index (2015). Agriculture remains a key source of economic activity and livelihoods for most rural households.

## Malawi faces a number of climate-induced disasters including floods, droughts, stormy rains, and strong winds. The intensity and frequency of climate-related hazards in Malawi have been increasing in recent decades, due to climate change. These events are expected to continue and intensify over the coming years.

## The impacts of such hazards have already severely disrupted food production, led to the displacement of communities, loss of livestock, and increased vulnerability among households. The Lake Malawi Basin (LMB), which constitutes a key agricultural region of the country, is prone to cycles of recurrent floods and droughts. Between 1967 and 2013, there were 45 flood events in the LMB, with an average recurrence interval of about five years for smallholder farmers.

## The 2011-12 drought had severe effects on food security in many districts, with >2 million people affected by food insecurity.

## Malawi has just recovered from an intense flood event in 2015, which left many lives and livelihoods destroyed. The country received rainfall amounts that were the highest on record for the country and which have been associated with climate change. This was followed by a severe drought in early 2016, which is now considered to be part of an already precarious situation for rural households in this region. This led to the declaration of a state of disaster in the LMB in March 2016. The country has experienced several other climate-related disasters including floods, displacement, and loss of lives. For example, the 2015 flood event displaced over 336,000 and killed 104 people! Evidence suggests that the likelihood of past drought and intense rainfall has increased due to climate change which, combined with projected increases in temperature, will result in more frequent and severe flooding events. The Lake Malawi Climate change impacts in the form of increased temperatures and reduced rainfall are also likely to result in considerable changes in the distribution and abundance of fish species in the lake. This is expected to have a negative impact on the staple maize crop. Amidst the backing of these climate vulnerabilities (further examined in the baseline section below), Malawi has been ranked as one of the most vulnerable countries to climate change.

## The CGIAR recognizes that no meaningful reduction in poverty can be achieved in the country without addressing the deleterious impacts of disasters and climate change. Thus, it is included in Theme 3 of the Malawi Growth and Development Strategy (MGDS) for 2015-2019. The MGDS sets out a number of strategies to address this challenge. The government will implement a number of strategies including: i) strengthening disaster risk management coordination mechanisms; ii) improving early warning systems, climate information services, and communication systems in disaster prone areas. This strategy will be supported by focusing on activities ensuring effective early warning systems (supporting the development of weather forecasting and warning weather and climate monitoring, prediction systems and information system).

## 14. Other relevant national strategies and policies provide strategic direction for early warning systems, climate information services, and communication systems in disaster prone areas. These include: i) the National Disaster Management Act (NDMA) of 1991; a draft National Disaster Risk Management (NDRM) Policy; and the National Climate Change Policy. The National Strategy on Disaster Risk Reduction (NSDRR) complies with United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). Objectives are: i) to provide early warning systems for timely, safe and early warning and awareness, uptake and response by the general public; and ii) to provide reliable weather and climate information services. The National Adaptation Programme of Action (NAPA) highlights the need for strengthened EWS to support improved disaster risk management.

### C.2 Project / Programme Objective against Baseline
#### Baseline Scenario
16. Malawi is particularly vulnerable to climate change and variability. Observed changes in climate include a shift in the timing of rainfall, with earlier onset of the rainy season and reduction in the length of the growing season.** Temperature increased by 0.9°C between 1960 and 2008, at an average rate of 0.21°C per decade**, with most of this warming occurring from September to November (early summer). The number of hot days per year has increased by 35-30 days between 1960-2003, particularly in summer; whilst the average number of hot nights has increased by an additional 41 days per year over the same period. There is a general trend towards more frequent and intense rainfall events with temperatures above 27°C to 35°C for 20 years, a decrease in total annual rainfall and water availability, and an increase in erratic rainfall events. The frequency of extreme weather has increased significantly since the early 1980s, with more severe storms and heavy weather over the coming decades. Over the last four decades, Malawi has experienced several climate variations that have had significant impacts on agriculture, including droughts (recorded), floods (recorded), and a combination of drought and flash floods (if recorded). An Economic Vulnerability and Disaster Risk Assessment** demonstrates that whilst droughts are more common in the north, floods are more common and cause severe economic hardship. Floods occur in the south, particularly in the Lower Shire River valley and the Buzi River basin, while droughts are more common in the northern regions of Malawi, the lower reaches of the Songozi river in the northern region, and the Bua and Limphe rivers in the central region.

17. Expected increases in the frequency and severity of extreme events such as floods and storms will increase the hazards faced by communities living along water courses and in low-lying areas. These communities are already forced to search for fish farther offshore and hence regularly encounter more severe weather, while taking longer to reach markets due to poor infrastructure and transport networks. This will have a negative impact on their livelihood productivity. These events, paired with climate variability and increasing in water stress, will negatively affect crop growth** and many aspects of the agricultural value chain including drying, storage and transport to market.

# Key Barriers addressed by the project

## Limited financial, technical and human capacity available for hydro-meteorological services, which has resulted in reduced capacity of DCCMs, DoDMA and DWR to fulfill their core mandates: One of the main reasons for the limited demand and use of climate information at scale across public and private sector actors is the lack of investment in hydro-meteorological services. With limited funding provided to DCCMs, DoDMA and DWR as well as other engaged ministries, departments and districts, there has been a decline in the capacity of these institutions to provide timely and reliable weather and climate information for Malawi over the last 20-30 years and ii) a limited ability of these entities to issue early warning systems and climate information products. This is compounded by the low density of meteorological stations and resultant limitations on the expansion of observational network coverage and forecasting as well as appropriate communication and packaging of warnings for local communities. Problems include the spatial and scale of climate information, often offered at a national level, which does not always reflect the needs of specific regions or districts. This is further compounded by lack of explanation of links between locally held and scientific information. These problems are compounded by the low density of meteorological stations and resultant limitations on the expansion of observational network coverage and transfer solutions such as weather index insurance.

## Limited availability of tailored, sector-specific climate information and early warning products relevant for public and private sector actors: The production of tailored sector-specific climate information and packaging and dissemination of weather/climate information is still nascent in Malawi, particularly for use in the agriculture and fisheries sectors. While there are a number of initiatives to provide tailored climate information to farmers and other stakeholders, the uptake of this information by farmers and those undertaking crop and livestock management activities is currently minimal for a number of reasons. These include: i) limited availability of tailored sector-specific climate information; ii) the information/advisories (average rainfall change for a season forecast, as opposed to information on onset, cessation and intensity of rainfall); iii) lack of translation of climate information into local languages; iv) lack of appropriate communication (translation to local languages, scientific concepts and living in low knowledge?); v) Forecast received by farmers is often too general and does not provide specific guidance for the next 2-3 days, such as daily weather forecasts, nor warnings of severe weather. Whilst there has been progress on flood forecasting using the Flash Flood Guidance System, forecasts are still for general areas, with no indications of which areas are predominantly at risk and neither flood water levels or depth.

## Lack of access to and awareness of use of early warning and climate information by vulnerable populations for urgent response action and adaptation planning: Access to weather/climate information is limited in Malawi. The most common method of dissemination used is, limited to radio (national and local), email, television, print media, internet websites, telephone calls, text messages, SMS, mobile phones, social media, etc. However, there are a number of challenges with these methods and uptake of this information by vulnerable communities, in particular women. This is partly because information is often not tailored to the needs of specific groups and does not take into account gender differences. It is clear how to integrate it with local knowledge and socio-cultural and gender-differentiated contexts. Technical and research institutions have a role to play in providing climate information products that are relevant for farmers and products by smallholder farmers and fishers. While the reach of mobile networks is extensive, use of ICT/mobile platforms is limited due to cost, lack of infrastructure, low levels of literacy and digital divide, as well as scale and effectiveness without scale. There is also limited knowledge generation, sharing, and collective learning to promote awareness and understanding by communities of the importance of weather and climate information and effective use of early warnings.

### Output 1: Expansion of systems that generate climate-related data to save lives and safeguard livelihoods from extreme climate events

#### 29. The Feasibility Assessment (attached as Annex II) details the status of infrastructure and current activities to support the use of climate-related information and early warning systems, as well as the gaps and needs which need to be addressed in order to provide a robust system for early warning and response.

The project will expand the coverage and scale-up of current meteorological and hydrological infrastructural investments. This includes:

- **Activity 1.1:** Expanding coverage of Meteorological and Hydrological infrastructure through installation of AWS, hydrological monitoring stations and sensors, lightning detection systems, and lake-based buoys.

#### Activity 1.1: Expanding coverage of Meteorological and Hydrological infrastructure through installation of AWS, hydrological monitoring stations and sensors, lightning detection systems, and lake-based buoys

This project will support the expansion and scale-up of current meteorological and hydrological infrastructural investments in Malawi. This includes:

- **AWS (Automatic Weather Stations):** 34 new AWSs will be installed to cover all major districts in Malawi for flood forecasting and water resource monitoring in the Shire River basin.
- **Hydrological water level stations:** 37 with telemetry will be procured and installed to improve flood risk monitoring, forecasting, and early warning systems. These will be connected to existing AWSs (34) via a network of LoRaWAN gateways for data transmission.
- **Lightning detection sensors:** 2 will be procured and installed as a proxy for more expensive and difficult-to-maintain lightning rod systems.

# E.1 Impact Potential

## E.1.1 Mitigation / adaptation impact potential

The project incorporates lessons learned and best practices from several successful international efforts to enable transformative impact through the scale-up of the use of early warning systems and climate information in Malawi. Key lessons include: (i) the effectiveness of community-based early warning systems in reducing disaster losses in the Philippines; (ii) the effectiveness of community-based food early warning systems in Mozambique; (iii) strengthening the capacity of communities to respond to disasters, such as floods and droughts, which can save lives in places like Lake Victoria; and (iv) the critical importance of community-based organizations and volunteers in contributing and organizing responses to early warnings in Bangladesh, Philippines, Cambodia and Vietnam[34, 36].

Furthermore, the project focuses on improving the generation, packaging, and communication of climate-related information. This includes: (i) the use of weather forecasts and climate outlooks for decision-making by farmers and agricultural advisors throughout Africa; (ii) additionally, the project emphasizes a participatory approach mechanism to ensure that the most vulnerable communities are involved in the development and implementation of the uptake[36]. The use of flood forecasting models, improved meteorological forecasts and mobile phones for dissemination, as recognized by the World Meteorological Organization (WMO), can reduce disaster losses from floods and other significant damages in Bangladesh[38]. ## H2 Arrangements for Monitoring, Reporting and Evaluation

### 168. Project-level monitoring and evaluation will be undertaken in compliance with the UNDP POPF and the UNDP Evaluation Policy. The Project Manager is responsible for developing and implementing an annual work plan for the Project Manager. ### 169. The UNDP Country Office will support the Project Manager as needed, including through annual supervision missions to monitor progress against the project’s objectives and outputs, and provide technical assistance in compliance with the UNDP POPF. Additional M&E implementation quality assurance, and troubleshooting support will be provided by the UNDP Evaluation Resource Centre (ERC) to key stakeholders groups and stakeholders including the NDA Focal Point will be involved as much as possible in project-level M&E.

### 170. A project inception workshop will be held after the UNDP project document has been signed by all relevant parties to: a) reorient project stakeholders to the project strategy and discuss any changes in the overall context that influence the project; b) review the project’s organizational structure, roles and responsibilities, communication lines and conflict resolution mechanisms; c) review the results framework, re-assess baselines as needed, and agree on the arrangements for data collection and reporting; d) review the project’s financial and/or other financial reporting procedures and mandatory requirements, and agree on the arrangements for the annual audit; e) plan and agree on the arrangements for the inception workshop and the project implementation. The Project Manager will coordinate the coordination of the NDA Focal Point and other stakeholders to the inception report no later than one month after the inception workshop. The final inception report will be cleared by the UNDP Country Office and submitted to the UNDP Evaluation Resource Centre (ERC) for review.

### 171. A project implementation report will be prepared for each year of project implementation. The Project Manager, the UNDP Country Office, and the UNDP Regional Technical Advisor will provide objective input to the annual PIR. The Project Manager will prepare a draft PIR no later than three months before the end of the reporting period in advance of the PIR submission deadline and will objectively report progress in the Development Objective tab of the PIR. This will include an assessment of project performance against the baseline, any changes made to the baseline, and the reasons for these changes. The Project Manager will also coordinate the input of the NDA Focal Point and other stakeholders to the PIR. The quality rating of the previous year’s PIR will be used as a basis for the quality rating of this year’s PIR. The final PIR will be reviewed by the UNDP Country Office, and submitted to the UNDP Evaluation Resource Centre (ERC) for review.

### 172. An independent mid-term review process will be undertaken and the findings and responses outlined in the management response document will be included in the mid-term evaluation report. The mid-term evaluation will take place at least six months before the end of the project’s duration. The terms of reference, the review process and the final MTR report will follow the standard templates and guidance available on the UNDP Evaluation Resource Centre. The final MTR report will be cleared by the UNDP Country Office and submitted to the UNDP Evaluation Resource Centre (ERC) for review.

### 173. The final MTR report will be available in English. An independent terminal evaluation (TE) will take place no later than three months after the project’s completion, and the terms of reference, the review process and the final TE report will follow the standard templates and guidance available on the UNDP Evaluation Resource Centre. The final TE report will be cleared by the UNDP Country Office and submitted to the UNDP Evaluation Resource Centre (ERC) for review.

### 174. The Project Board will receive a copy of the final MTR and TE reports, which will be made available in English and the management response to the public UNDP Evaluation Resource Centre (ERC) [www.erc.undp.org]. The MTR and TE reports will also be submitted to the UNDP Independent Evaluation Office for review.

### 175. The UNDP Country Office will retain all M&E records for this project for up to seven years after project financial closure in accordance with UNDP regulations. A detailed M&E budget, monitoring plan and evaluation plan will be included in the UNDP project document.
