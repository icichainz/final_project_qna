## Use of diesel and kerosene fuel is the current model for rural energy use with resulting high GHG emissions

On a binary basis, the national grid connected electrification rate is 30% which means that nearly 24.5 million people in rural areas are still without access to electricity. Rural households in these areas of the population may have some sort of electric access, often through the use of diesel generators.

### Renewable energy mini-grids (mini-grids) are expected to play an important role in bridging the electricity access gap and providing a sustainable solution for rural electrification, as well as other related services. The opportunity around mini-grids is centered around a number of key trends - lower hardware costs, increased availability of financing options, improved grid connection technologies, and potential interest from the private sector; bringing new innovative business models.

### However, there are several challenges to be addressed in order for mini-grids to become an alternative to national grid and centralized generation, not only because they are not suitable for mini-grid given differences in the cost of electricity production, but also due to the lack of a conducive policy environment. The lack of solar grid development in Afghanistan - and the potential for eventual, scaled-up commercial investment - will need an enabling environment that includes: (i) clear national policies and strategies; (ii) updated procurement guidelines, institutional frameworks, delivery models and financing, technical solutions, simply put, a conducive policy environment.

### The objective of the project is to kick-start rural renewable energy market transformation in Afghanistan and create favorable conditions for upgrading and long-term scaling up, through a combination of (i) policy and regulatory design; (ii) development of enabling institutional frameworks and financial mechanisms; and (iii) raising the profile of mini-grids. This will be achieved by: (a) developing a national strategy on the use of mini-grids, giving access to 48,000 beneficiaries of which 23,500 are women; (b) establishing an “updated” procurement platform including a solar mini-grid practical development manual, online evidence and data sharing platform, and a technical assistance facility; (c) developing a national strategy on the use of green procurement policies for solar mini-grids; and (d) development and mainstreaming of environmental and social governance policies (ESG) policies.

### Ultimately, the project will aim to: in a phased approach, a large volume of solar mini-grid development in Afghanistan - and with the long-term improvement in macroeconomic and security conditions that are expected over time - to ensure the sustainability of these projects. The project will also include maintenance only. The project covers the first, pilot phase out of a three-phased process of mini-grids implementation: (i) the first phase is a scaled-up version of the successful model of the 2014-2015 semi-subsidy ownership model with outsourced operation & maintenance to the private sector. This is expected to be implemented in 30 villages, covering approximately 6,000 beneficiaries; (ii) the second phase will consist of up-gradation of technical and managerial aspects related to mini-grid design, construction, and operation & maintenance, as well as the development of a national strategy on the use of mini-grids. This will include demand profiles for electricity, revenue streams, willingness to pay and average revenue per user (ARPU), exploration of public-private partnerships (PPPs) and other financing mechanisms; and (iii) the third phase is financial viability by means of productive use stimulation. Following this first pilot phase, a second scale-up phase will be implemented in 100 villages covering approximately 24,500 beneficiaries with a mix of private sector ownership BOOT/BBO models, with considerable public support via development banks. The ultimate goal is to have 300 mini-grids serving over 75,000 households and providing electricity to the spread private sector-based BBOOT/BBO models with minimal public input.

### This approach will create the basis for mainstreaming renewable energy mini-grid solutions and continued and sustainable access to electricity in rural areas. The project is expected to result in a reduction of 137,082 tCO2e reductions from the implementation of 3 solar mini-grids. The implementation of three proc-ess phases will be completed by 2025, with an estimated total investment of USD 46 million and approximately 39,500 beneficiaries, of which 17,500 are women as a result of the additional services that will become available with Term 3 mini-grids.

# B.1 Climate rationale and context (max. 1000 words, approximately 2 pages)

8. The Afghanistan Nationally Determined Contributions (NDC) to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) identifies extreme hunger and water as key problems for Afghanistan, and data from the World Bank show that these are strongly linked with climate change impacts such as reduced agricultural production, and the availability of energy, water and other natural resources. Rural energy poverty is considered a major barrier to food security in Afghanistan. The average rural household consumes 10 kWh connected electricity rate nationwide, and 141 kWh per capita annual energy usage**. Especially in rural areas this means that households are forced to use biomass for cooking and heating, and diesel fuel for cooling and heating. Large rural areas will remain disconnected to the grid during the next decades, due to the high cost of extending the grid to these areas. The concept of energy poverty is closely linked with the concept of water, energy and food (WEF) nexus means that this three sectors – water security, energy security and food security are interlinked and cannot be addressed in isolation. Energy poverty has a direct impact on one or both of them. It’s clear that access to energy is critical for addressing the various dimensions of rural energy poverty.

9. The baseline option for meeting increased energy demand in rural areas, as their income levels grow, is diesel generation. This would lead to an increase in GHG emissions in Afghanistan continuing to increase at current rates. In 2017, Afghanistan’s per capita electricity consumption was only about 35 kWh, which is below the global average of 486 kWh and even lower than the lowest income levels but appropriate support in the form of finance, capacity building, technology and legal aid is necessary to ensure that rural households can access energy services. The current level of diesel generation would lead to an increase in GHG emissions content or some 2.8 kgCO2e/kWh. Among the alternative options for energy access, renewable energy micro-grids are considered as a promising option because they have the potential to provide abundant natural resource potential without cost-prohibitive grid extension or GHG-intensive diesel systems. In Afghanistan, there is an abundance of solar and wind resources that can be harnessed by communities in rural areas. While expansion of the national grid will not be possible for many years due to the high costs involved, renewable energy micro-grids have. In particular in rural areas, where expansion of a national grid is not expected in the short and medium term, renewable energy micro-grids can provide an alternative solution that can help to reduce GHG emissions from diesel generation and increase access to electricity for rural energy use. This would likely halve GHG emissions in Afghanistan continuing to increase at current rates.

#### Project Description

The objective of this project is to prepare the ground for rural renewable energy market transformation in Afghanistan through a combination of (i) targetted de-risking activities to address key mini-grid investment risks and (ii) targeted capacity building and knowledge sharing. ## The project covers the first pilot phase out of a three-phased development process of mini-grid market development in Afghanistan, set out in this Figure. This first phase is based on a semi-utility mini-grid concept and will be implemented by DAB (the national power utility) in partnership with private sector entities to create a basis for private sector interest in mini-grid development and to contribute to the build-up of expertise in technical and financial aspects of mini-grid development.

This will include data gathering and key performance indicators including, but not limited to: demand profiles for electricity; capacity requirements; cost estimates for grid extension; costs associated with different technologies and smart meters and different payment models; and optimizing system sizing and mini-grid financial viability by means of a techno-economic analysis. The project is expected to be completed in 2016.

The scale-up phase will consist of moving towards further private sector engagement, including initial private sector ownership of the mini-grids. The final phase will see the transition from public sector to private sector ownership and development in Afghanistan is reflected in a third, mature phase - served by wide-spread private sector-based mini-grid development. This submission covers only the first pilot phase, while the scale-up phase and maturity phase fall outside of the scope of this submission.

## De-risking is that central mechanism by which this project will act. Public de-risking measures can improve the likelihood of private sector investment in mini-grids through a number of mechanisms including:

* **Financial Risk:** Grant to provide financial de-risking by reducing capital expenditure faced by private sector investors.
* **Energy Market Risk:** Grant to provide energy market de-risking by reducing uncertainty and market risk regarding future electricity demand, supply and prices.
* **Social Acceptance Risk:** Grant to provide social acceptance de-risking by reducing risks arising from lack of awareness and resistance to renewable energy and mini-grid technology in the target communities.
* **Labor Risk:** Grant to provide labor de-risking by reducing risks arising from lack of skilled and qualified potential employees at mini-grid operators (DAB).
* **Developer Risk:** Grant to provide developer de-risking by reducing risks arising from limitations on the mini-grid operator's management capability (system design and engineering, business model selection), and its creditworthiness and cash flow.

### Activity 1.4: Framework for Solar-Plus-Minigrids Development

#### 36. One of the challenges identified in Afghanistan, and in developing countries in general, for private sector investment in RE mini-grids is to improve the current financial viability of mini-grids. With current inputs for human resources, technical capacity, and capital, it will be difficult to make a profit from mini-grids and the implied tariffs for end-users can be prohibitive. To address this barrier, the project proposes to design a framework that will allow for private sector investment in RE mini-grids. The framework will include detailed techno-economic analyses, including a DREI analysis simulator[27]. The framework will essentially be a viable business case for investors and lenders to invest in RE mini-grids. It will also provide a set of example concessional financing and/or direct grants (capital supply, tax incentives), to achieve the requisite financial viability of RE mini-grids. This framework will be designed with the objective of attracting funding by international financial institutions and/or multilateral development banks, including innovative financing mechanisms such as blended finance, green bonds, etc., which can provide concessional funding to capitalize a revolving fund. Subsequent implementation of this framework will be a key step in the phased approach for mini-grid development.

#### 37. The development of the framework will be informed by a rigorous process, consisting of expert panel discussions, consultations with stakeholders (e.g., investors, lenders, developers, government officials, and market actors at AREU and beneficiaries). Strategies to be discussed can include mechanisms to compensate for high-risk projects (e.g., insurance, guarantees, risk mitigation funds, etc.), debt guarantees. The applicability of these strategies will in part depend on the macroeconomic and security development context of Afghanistan. It is important to note that the project will not directly use the very high risk-rating of Afghanistan at the moment. A viability gap funding mechanism will be elaborated upon as a possible strategy for addressing this issue, but it should be noted that Afghanistan has thus far had yet materialized. Mechanisms that involve using positive revenues from donor supported mini-grids to finance new projects are also being considered in order to address high-risk issues.

#### 38. The project will not directly implement revolving funds nor channel concessional funding (e.g., loans).

2.1 Project background

* Afghanistan faces severe energy shortages owing to an aging electricity infrastructure, high dependence on imported and biomass fuels, and weak power sector policy and regulatory frameworks. Renewable energy mini-grids are expected to play an important role in bridging the electricity access deficit in Afghanistan in a timely manner, while at the same time offering clean, low-carbon solutions.
* The objective of the project is to kick-start market transformation of the rural renewable energy mini-grid market in Afghanistan by developing and implementing a comprehensive policy for sustainability of the sector through a combination of (1) policy and regulatory design, facilitating public and private sector investment; (2) capacity-building and awareness-raising of the public and private sectors on the benefits of renewable energy mini-grids; and three greenfield solar mini-grids and creating an “upscaling platform” to facilitate additional solar mini-grid investments. The project will also develop a comprehensive policy framework for a solar mini-grid practical development manual, online evidence and data sharing tools, and full technical assistance to support the implementation of the policy. This includes the design of green procurement policies for solar mini-grids; and (3) development and mainstreaming of renewable energy mini-grids into national rural electrification plans.
* The project addresses the short-, medium- and long-term vision of the Afghanistan renewable energy mini-grid market. It clarifies how the project will contribute to kick-start the market transformation in the renewable energy sector, including the development of a policy framework for the country, including strengthened institutional and regulatory systems. The theory of change describes the expected impact on the short-, medium- and long-term development, including anticipated electricity use for increased resilience to climate change.

# c. Health

Over the past decade Afghanistan has made notable progress in increasing access to health services through construction of hospitals and health clinics, training of medical doctors and professionals and development of institutional framework governing the health sector. ### e. Political Participation

Women have the same rights to vote and stand for electoral seats as men. The Constitution of Afghanistan ensures that for each province, there is at least one seat for a woman in the national Parliament. According to this affirmative action, 25% of members of parliament are women. Women participation has also been guaranteed at the provincial, district and community level. Similar to the parliament, women have reserved seats in the provincial councils which has the main responsibility of implementing government programs and projects and service delivery. Members of the parliament and the provincial council are directly elected through votes where both men and women of age 18 and higher can vote.

In addition to the electoral bodies, international development organizations in a coordination with the government of Afghanistan have also established councils and committees at the district and community level to have direct decision making and oversight role in implementation of development projects. Community Development Councils (CDC) is elected rural institution actively involved in implementation and oversight of the Charter funded by the World Bank and implemented by the MRD. CDC's play much wider role in decision making and conflict resolution at the community level than only being involved with the implementation of the mentioned development programs. At the district and representative bodies at the district and community level, CDC are also involved in development and rural development plans and lobby for their communities at the provincial and national level.

The role of women in government leadership positions has also increased over the last decade. Women have been holding ministerial positions since 2001. Currently, four out of twenty-five ministers are women. Women are also appointed as deputy ministers, governors, mayors, and deputies.

Although there has been progress in advancing women's political participation, more needs to be done in this direction. Overall political participation and involvement in decision-making and government leadership positions is still much lower compared to men. However, over the last decade a strong civil society advocating for women’s right has emerged which constantly works towards greater representation of women in Afghanistan’s policymaking. The country is in the right path of advancing women's rights and representation in political arenas, but achieving complete equality will take much longer time.

A total of 412 other incidences of violence against women were related to sexual violence and 579 incidences recorded during this period are related to different types of sexual violence while 1842 incidences which make up 30.4 percent of all the registered incidences of violence against women are related to verbal and psychological violence. Similarly, 1624 incidences which make up 21 percent of all incidences of violence against women are related to economic violence (AIBHC). The remaining 487 incidences which included 10 percent of the registered incidences are related to other types of violence against women (AIBHC Report on Violence Against Women).

The Government has taken a number of initiatives to facilitate prosecution of perpetrators. These measures include establishment of special courts for the cases of violence against women in 26 provinces, and the Supreme Court has issued several decrees. In addition, it is intended that decisions have been made to establish a special branch within the Supreme Court to specifically rule on the case of violence against women, juvenile and family disputes.

Child marriage, a form of gender-based violence (GBV), is illegal but widespread in Afghanistan. Afghanistan’s minimum age of marriage for girls is 16 or 15 years old, which is well below the international standard of 18 years old. Child marriages mainly happen in rural areas, especially along the borders with Pakistan (UNFPA Afghanistan).

Gender-based violence also interacts with issues around access to and control over natural resources. For example, research has shown that gender-differentiated roles related to land and resources can put women in a more vulnerable position to suffer GBV while carrying out daily responsibilities such as collecting water or firewood.

When women are forced to leave their homes due to conflict or other reasons and enter into agricultural markets, women can experience intimate partner violence as their partners seek to control finances and maintain economic dependencies (UCN).

## The 2010 National Priority Programs with Women empowerment being one of the national priority programs.

Article 22 of the constitution guarantees equal rights to man and women; it reads: "Any kind of discrimination and distinction between citizens of Afghanistan shall be forbidden. The citizens of Afghanistan, men and women, have equal rights and duties before the law." This article guarantees the application of the principles of equality in implementation of all articles of the constitution. Recognizing the fact that the women of Afghanistan do not benefit from the same means and opportunities to impact development and benefit from development in the country, the need for affirmative action where necessary is justified in alignment with application of Article 22 of the constitution.

Article 54 of the Constitution also calls for abolishment of the harmful practices that inhibit the wellbeing of the Afghan women. Likewise, many other articles of the constitution make specific reference to women's needs in education, health, political participation etc.. Articles 83 and 86 of the Constitution provide for the establishment of a special committee on women within the Lower House of Parliament and selection of 50% women members by the President for the Upper House of the Parliament.

In addition to the constitution, international laws also provide legal framework for protecting women. The government of Afghanistan ratified the Convention on the Elimination of all forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW), without any reservations, in March 2003, which obligates governments to:

- Incorporate the principle of equality of men and women and non-discrimination in its legal system and repeal all discriminatory laws;
- Take all appropriate measures, including legislation and temporary special measures, so that women can enjoy all their human rights and fundamental freedoms without any discrimination, systematic or otherwise, by persons, organizations or enterprises.
- Establish national and other public institutions to ensure the effective protection of women against discrimination.

Countries that have ratified or acceded to the Convention are legally bound to put its provisions into practice. They are also committed to submit national reports, at least every four years, on measures they have taken to comply with their treaty obligations. Afghanistan submitted its combined periodic and interim report on CEDAW in 2011.

Another key national document is the National Action Plan for the Women of Afghanistan (NAPWA) with the timeframe of 2008-2018, a ten-year plan towards addressing gaps in gender equality and women's rights and empowerment in Afghanistan.
