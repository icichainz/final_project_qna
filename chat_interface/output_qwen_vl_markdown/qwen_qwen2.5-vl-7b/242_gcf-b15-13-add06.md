**A.2. Project | Programme Executive Summary (max 200 words)**

1. With 84% of its primary energy requirements met from imported fossil fuels, Mauritius, like many Small Island Developing States (SIDS), faces the challenge of high greenhouse gas emissions per capita and a carbon intensity level that is among the highest in the world at 1.01 tonnes CO2e/MWh due to the prevalence of imported coal (39%) and fuel oil (8%) in the electricity generation mix. Net greenhouse gas emissions are increasing at a rapid rate of 3% per year. The pressing need to strengthen energy security, reduce greenhouse gas emissions, and improve energy access is reflected in Mauritius’s Nationally Determined Contribution (2016), its Second National Communication to the UNFCCC (2010) and its UNFCCC Strategic Plan (2013-2025). The project will provide a comprehensive suite of Government strategies and policies contained in the Long-Term Energy Strategy 2011–2025.

2. Following a broad consultative process led by the NDA and backed by sound technical and financial analysis as well as documented evidence, the project proposes two complementary components for energy sector transformational development for: (i) grid-connected intermittent renewable energy; and (ii) mini-grid PV for the principal outer island, Agalega. The project will be implemented in a phased manner with the first phase being funded by the GCF and ensure that the second funding disbursement is contingent upon successful completion of the first phase.

3. The project seeks a total US$ 28.21 million of GCF grant resources, split across phase I (US$ 12 million) and phase II (US$ 16.21 million). The project will be implemented in two phases with phase I being the preparatory stage of the project and its requirement for grant resources are clearly laid out in the proposal. Overall, the project will result in a reduction of greenhouse gas emissions of 4.27 million tCO2e over the lifetimes of the investments enabled, at a cost to the GCF of just US$ 6.63/tCO2e.

### C1. Strategic Context

The Republic of Mauritius is an island nation off the south-east coast of Africa on the continent in the south-west Indian Ocean, approximately 900 km east of Madagascar. In addition to the island of Mauritius, the Republic includes the islands of Rodrigues and Agalega. The country has a population of about 1.3 million people.

A clear demonstration of the pertinence of this project is that the Mauritius Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) Action Plan 2015-2019, which was submitted to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), includes a proposal for the construction of a 110 MW coal-fired power plant.

Mauritius is heavily reliant on fossil fuels to power its economy. The grid emission factor of Mauritius is an extremely high 1.01 CO2/MWh due to the prevalence of imported coal (39%) and fuel oil (26%) in the electricity generation mix, which accounts for approximately 75% of total energy consumption.

The construction of a new coal-fired power plant will further increase Mauritius' energy independence and reduce greenhouse gas emissions. This fact is recognised by the Government, which has recently announced two key targets:

- A renewable energy (RE) target of at least 30% of electricity production by 2025.
- The establishment of a Mauritius Renewable Energy Agency (MARENA) to coordinate the rapid uptake of renewable energy.

To facilitate the achievement of these targets, the support of local, regional and international institutions is being actively sought.

- In 2013, the Government of Mauritius launched, with UNDP support, the Small Scale Distributed Generation (SSDG) scheme, which has assisted 257 households, schools and public institutions to install small-scale (≤50 kW photovoltaic (PV)) panels and wind turbines through the provision of a targeted feed-in tariff scheme. Such an approach was considered as a cost-effective way to increase access to renewable energy for those who are not yet connected to the national grid.
- With regard to the outer islands of the Republic of Mauritius, the Outer Islands Development Corporation (OICD) has been implementing a project since 2013 with the aim of increasing access to electricity and reducing impact than the numerous oil drums that currently litter the island. Agaléga, the largest outer island, is the furthest from the mainland and is also the most isolated.
- In addition, the berthing facilities at Agaléga are limited and the transport of oil drums to the island is difficult due to the lack of infrastructure on the island. The same applies for the carriage and barging of cargoes in unfavourable sea conditions. There is no central electricity grid on the two islands that make up the outermost part of Mauritius, Agaléga and Cargados Carajos Shoals.
- The only electricity supply to these islands comes from diesel-powered mini-grids. Sole PV – as a decentralised modular technology - represents a particular challenge for these remote islands. In 2013, the OICD launched a project with the aim of installing the Ministry of Energy and Public Utilities in 2000 to service the local population; for example, a PV mini-grid was installed on the island of Agaléga.
- The project is being implemented by the Mauritius Electricity Corporation (MEC) and involves the installation of a solar-powered water pump, a small generator, and a small dispenser, together with 30 solar-powered street lights (the first street lighting on the island), initially to provide electricity for the local population. This project was completed in December 2014.
- However, underlying barriers to sustainable use of the PV technology, notably lack of training for island-based technicians and maintenance personnel, have led to frequent system failures, resulting in major breakdowns and ultimately mothballing of the systems in 2009. Building local technical capacity, as well as reinstating and enhancing the current PV systems, remains a key priority for OICD.
- The GCF project will enable Mauritius to fully meet its stated target of 35% renewable electricity production by 2025* and will give credence to the Government’s stated objective in the NDC of sustaining such a level in the period 2025 to 2030.

Renewable energy sources of variable nature, such as wind and solar, have a direct impact on the stability of the power system. The integration of these sources requires careful planning and management of energy sources, the allocation of spinning reserves on fast-acting conventional generators such as diesel engines, hydropower plants or gas turbines, and the use of advanced control systems to ensure grid stability in the presence of variable RE sources. In this respect, BESS, AGC, ADMS and AMI are a key means of ensuring grid stability. As Mauritius is an island with limited interconnection to neighbouring power systems, the need to ensure grid stability prior to the scale-up of renewable energy is paramount.

178. Distributed energy generation also offers grid loss reduction as an additional benefit. The existing electrical power system consists primarily of centralised conventional (diesel and gas) and hydroelectric power stations that are located on Port Louis Island. These power stations supply electricity to the national transmission network, which in turn supplies medium-voltage distribution networks and low-voltage networks to the customer. The transfer of power implies losses and these losses are particularly high when the load is distributed around the island. In this respect, the studies conducted in Mauritius grid average 7%.

179. Distributed generation from PV sources, being located in the vicinity of, or directly within, the load centre, will reduce transmission line losses by bypassing distant power stations at the time while solar photovoltaic power is available, thereby reducing the energy losses along the transmission and distribution networks. The closer the distributed generation facility to the load centre, the greater the reduction in transmission and distribution network losses.

180. The actual improvement in the network losses due to distributed generation facilities will depend upon the installed PV capacity at each location. For example, a 5 MW PV plant located on Port Louis Island would reduce total network losses by approximately 2% of electricity produced annually and considering a grid emission factor of 1.0 tCO2/MWh.
