## A.21. Executive summary (max. 750 words; approximately 1.5 pages)
This proposal aims to foster the development of off-grid renewable energy mini-grids to reduce CO₂ emissions from the Senegal power sector whilst contributing to electricity access objectives and promoting gender-balanced rural economic growth in the country.

The project will be implemented by the Government of Senegal, represented by the Senegalese Rural Electrification Agency (ASEUR). The rural electrification sector was considered one of the greatest success of PSE phase 1, with national power generation increasing two-fold between 2013 and 2015. However, only about 4% of Senegal's population has access to electricity at a reasonable cost rate; this is still relatively low and reveals a great disparity between urban and rural populations, with regional disparities that are even more dramatic than national ones.

The project will focus on three regions: the Casamance region (Ziguinchor, Saint-Louis, Saloum), the Fatick region (Diourbel, Sédhiou, Matam, Thiaroye), and the Dakar region (Saly, Mermoz, Saléma, Médine Yom Foula, Rassene and Saraye). These regions have an electrical coverage rate of less than 10%. These low rates are mainly due to high costs of investments and operations, dispersion, low level of consumption, yet the benefits of mini-electrification projects are numerous: improved access to education and health services, increased productivity. Distributed renewable energy generation also helps increase the reliability and resilience of power supply by reducing dependence on diesel generators.

The project will contribute to the achievement of the following Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and co-benefits can further contribute to improving living standards of the communities:

- **SDG 7**: Ensure access to affordable, reliable, sustainable and modern energy for all.
- **SDG 13**: Take urgent action to combat climate change and its impacts.

The objective of the proposed project is to support the Gov by providing concessional O&Ms needed to mobilize private sector investment in the domestic renewable energy market and achieve its low-carbon rural electrification goals as proclaimed in the Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) of Senegal, under the form of sovereign loan and grant from the Green Climate Fund (GCF).

Climate change in Senegal is predicted to affect average yearly rainfall with an increase in rain intensity leading to flooding, along with increased air temperatures and sea level rise. Its effects on water resources thus include stressors affecting surface and groundwater availability, as well as soil erosion and desertification. These areas are also susceptible to variable rainfall and increased evaporation rates of retention ponds and dams. Its effects are also the increase in temperature leading to higher demand for cooling and air conditioning. The project will support the pumping of underground water. Rural electrification improves access to groundwater and thereby supports higher agricultural productivity, as well as improving health services through refrigeration and cooling equipment, and through access to electricity and thereby information and communications technologies, improving household income.

Proposed intervention
In line with Senegal NDCs1 and its national ambition of universal access to electricity by 2025 (SEAN), the project to be implemented by ASEUR is the Rural Electrification Agency (ASEUR) under delegation of the Government of Senegal. aims at deploying 10% solar mini-grids in a thousand (1,000) isolated villages across Senegal, i.e., half of the most vulnerable, least attractive locations to be electrified by rural electrification concessions. ### GENERAL OVERVIEW OF KEY CLIMATE CHANGE IMPACTS AND DRIVERS IN SENEGAL

Senegal's climate is generally characterized as tropical, with one rainy season from May–November and a dry season dominated by dry, hot harmattan winds between December and April. Temperatures and rainfall vary across regions, with rainfall generally decreasing from north to south.

**Climate Projections:**

- Increase in average temperatures by 0.9°C, with higher rates of warming in the north and more pronounced between 17–27°C.
- In the northern Sahelian zone, the climate is characterized by cool nights (minimum of 14°C) and hot days (maximum temperature above 35°C).
- Precipitation has increased since the mid-1990s but remains below pre-1990 levels and remain 15 percent below the long-term average.
- Rainfall decline is most significant in the southern region during the wet season (June–September).

## Business Model

The GCF sovereign loan to the Government of Senegal intends to reduce local operations' risks in operating and maintaining solar chargers in the smallest, most remote vulnerable villages to be electrified in Senegal. Based on the limited upfront connection fee of 5% of investments levied from the local operators and the applicable compensation from harmonized tariffs, private sector investors will have a minimum internal rate of return (IRR) unless governmental tariff compensations payable to operators are increased fivefold to reach a comparable return, given the current level of electricity consumption in these villages. The project is financed under the GCF, while construction is contracted to private suppliers and local operations & maintenance to historical contractors.

## D4. Key Recipients (max. 500 words, approximately 1 page)

### Vulnerable recipients

The electricity sector in Senegal (a Least Developed Country) is mostly exposed to climate risks as it relies heavily on fossil fuels for power generation and thermal power generation cooling. This increased damage to hydropower infrastructure from flooding and river sediment loads, but its increased damage to power transmission lines and distribution networks. The vulnerability of the grid also increases the vulnerabilities of off-grid rural communities span disasters caused by natural disasters like increasing mean temperature, sea level rise, and more frequent extreme weather events. These climate risks are further exacerbated by lower agricultural and economic productivity as well as displacement of women and young populations from building materials and other resources. Climate change impacts in Senegal are also further aggravated by the extensive fossil fuel use, low income and limited employment burdens on development efforts.

### Growing national debt level

Senegal's public debt currently stands at USD 7.5 billion, representing 53% of the country’s GDP. A combination of high interest rates and a depreciation in the exchange rate have led to an increase in treasury bills. The Ministry of Finance is committed to reduce debt ratio by the medium term by means of fiscal consolidation measures in the current account and improved debt management policies. With the help of GCF concessional loan, AER's project’s average cost per kilowatt-hour will be reduced from 12.50 CFA francs to 9.78 CFA francs. The long term obligations weigh on the government’s balance sheet. The long tenor of 40 years further optimizes its debt servicing capacity. (The interest rate is further sustained by private operators: 3% concession fee collection towards the harmonization fund).

### Local market capital constraints

Given the high cost and lack of track-record of similar projects in the country, local financial institutions are unwilling to provide affordable financing solutions to project developers. High cost of capital force project developers to change their business model from a public-private partnership (PPP) scheme to a concessional finance model. The initial 3 'pilot concessions' indicate that investor confidence in off-grid renewable energy IPPs is still weak. Furthermore, there is a significant mismatch between the amount of money that rural consumers can pay for electricity and project developers’ cost recovery requirements. This has led to the current situation where the government is shifting from the current BOT scheme to a concessional finance model is required to offer local financial lenders a reduced risk profile in order to attract private sector investment.

Without the concession co-finance extended through BOAD and the GCF, the investment hurdle for local financials will continue to exist and access to affordable finance will remain an issue for the concessions. The result will be lack of sufficient financing to support the development of the renewable energy sector and a significant challenge in achieving universal access by 2025, as demonstrating successful engagement of private sector concessionaires in the provision of off-grid electricity is critical to the achievement of this goal. Consequently, commercial lenders will have more confidence to step in by the time GCF’s involvement comes to an end.

## Summary of the Secretariat's assessment

### Project background

Although Senegal has been steadily increasing its national electrification rate, with almost fully electrified urban areas, the majority of people in rural areas still do not have access to electricity (approximately 38 per cent). For basic energy needs, households mainly rely on fuelwood for cooking and lighting. This situation is experienced as both health and environmental hazards. The rural electricity sector has been characterized by high upfront investment costs, low revenue generation capacity, and a lack of financial sustainability. This led to the need for recurring government subsidies to help private-sector operators maintain revenues at levels that are sustainable in the long term.

The main barriers that have been identified as challenging to low electrification rates in rural areas of Senegal include: high upfront investment costs, high levels of operational costs to run remote electricity assets, and the low level and widely dispersed nature of consumption, all of which make it difficult for private-sector operators to achieve financial sustainability. The project aims to tackle these barriers through a set of integrated interventions. GCF will provide the concessional financing needed to enable the development of mini-grids in 10,000 isolated villages, households with access to modern solar-powered mini grids in 1,000 isolated villages. The GCF proceeds will be used for the implementation of the project through a concessional scheme, which will not only make the connection charge affordable but also increase the commercial viability of each mini-grid by catalysing "anchor customers". This, together with the promotion of local content and the development of a local market (which is currently non-existent or productive), which will be achieved together with local micro-finance institutions, has the potential to create a virtuous cycle that can lead to the commercialization of solar energy in rural solar mini-grid. In addition, the capacity of main rural electrification stakeholders will be strengthened to enable the deployment of a network of mini-grids and facilitate project implementation.

The project is expected to reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions by approximately 13 million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) over its lifetime but by moving away from the use of traditional energy resources. ### Impact potential Scale: High

The pertinent question is whether Senegal has made progress towards improving access to electricity for rural households and communities since then. The lack of progress on the right improvement in access to electricity in rural Senegal in recent years reflects the aggressive effort of the Government of Senegal to improve electricity access in rural areas in its effort to rank among the top 30 countries in Africa.

2. In the last few years, the Government of Senegal has pursued an aggressive rural electrification programme called ASER (Association pour le Développement de l'Électrification Rurale). ASER is aimed at improving the low level of rural access to electricity, especially after the country joined the United Nations Sustainable Energy for All (SE4ALL) initiative. The Government of Senegal has set a target of achieving universal electrification of at least 90 per cent of rural households by 2025. The electricity access results obtained so far are not satisfactory and have been limited to selected rural communities; building isolated secondary and regional power plants that are operated by the national grid, or providing solar home systems (SHS) for individual households and microgrids in selected rural villages. Key achievements of ASER as of date 2015–2019 can be summarized as follows: electrification of 347 villages; installation of SHS in 68 villages; and the electrification of 1,000 rural villages. These achievements fall short of what is expected to put Senegal on a path towards its SE4ALL electricity access objectives. The unsustainable operation of these isolated power plants has been identified as one of the reasons why ASER has not been as fast as expected. Non-renewable energy sources such as diesel generators have been used in some cases, which has led to common problems. The national harmonization of tariffs between the rural consumers and those in urban areas has also been a problem that has been identified by the Government of Senegal as one of the reasons why ASER has not been as fast as expected. Efforts are being made to ameliorate these problems; however, these responses created a fiscal-financial burden for the Government of Senegal, necessitating alternative strategies.

# Introduction

Senegal’s National Rural Electrification Programme (PNER), piloted by ASER, aims to make electricity an instrument in the fight against poverty through the creation of wealth and jobs in rural areas. To achieve this, it wants to adopt a dynamic strategy, particularly with regard to alternative energies to hydropower, which have an important role to play.

Statistics show that the share of renewable energy in Senegal’s total energy production is still very low: 10% today, with a target of 40% by 2030. In a context of climate change, it is important to develop strategies including the installation of mini-solar networks for the benefit, in particular, of vulnerable groups that tend to take more wood resources than nature is capable of regenerating.

However, when formulating the programme, ASER did not take into account from the outset a differentiation between groups of men and women and their needs, taking as a reference the neutrality of energy policy. Projects have been designed specifically for women’s groups to respond to their specific needs in terms of access to electricity.

To remedy this, ASER, following a diagnosis of the level of gender mainstreaming in the PNER, recently decided to systematize gender mainstreaming in all its projects and programmes.

To this end, an action plan for the integration of gender in the national rural electrification programme using mini-solar grids has been drawn up accordingly.

For ASER, gender mainstreaming is a means of contributing to the achievement of sustainable development objectives by Senegal, through the PES, which considers rural electrification among its priority programmes.

### III.A Access to energy in the face of socio-cultural realities

There is a very strong inequality in access to energy because the needs of men and women are not the same and the roles are not the same either. The several domains of labour by society assign women to the domestic sphere, where their activities are confined to the reproductive role, i.e. the management of the household: cooking, fetching water, looking after and educating children, etc., while men are assigned to productive roles in agriculture or other economic activities. In community life, she rarely occupies decision-making positions. And it is influential factors such as socio-cultural, religious, legal and political beliefs that justify this state of affairs. There is also the fact that women have less access to education than men do; they are more often illiterate, given the roles assigned to them, have very little access to energy in a global way and to productive energy in a specific way.

In other words, men and women use energy differently because their roles, needs and responsibilities are different. Also, depending on whether one is in the rural or peri-urban or urban environment, the production factor "energy" does not intervene in the same way in the functions that men and women occupy; men have more of an economic role which allows them to access and control resources; in this case energy, they determine the allocation of energy

### III.4.3 Socio-economic and environmental impacts

- **Socially:**
  In terms of socio-economic consequences, the first constraint for women is the lack of time to carry out heavy and repetitive tasks using only their muscular strength. Thus, they devote themselves successively to milling cereals, collecting firewood, fetching water, working in the fields, etc., which leaves them little time to participate in the decision-making process unpaid and contribute to the lack of balance between the activities of men and women in society.

  In the absence of services to facilitate these activities, women completely lack autonomy. The only alternative for them remains the support of their daughters who accompany them in carrying out these tasks. This leads to a situation where they are not able to attend school regularly if they are enrolled, and thus find themselves in a situation of dropping out of school.

  The heavy workload of women’s activities leads to real health problems in a context where the health needs of the population are still very poorly met. Women are at risk of abortion and are exposed to fumes from the use of wood, cow dung, crop residues and other alternatives, at a time when they are not able to acquire income.

  The lack of time and services to facilitate women’s activities leads to their exclusion from local decision-making spheres and obscures the development of productive activities to enable them in the long run to acquire income. However, the trend is evolving positively due to the gender dynamic.

- **On the economic level:**
  In Senegal, women are very active in the agricultural value chain, as shown in Table 1, and increasingly, they are found in the processing and petty trade segments. In the processing chain, they are organized into women’s promotion groups (GPO) or economic interest groups (CIE) or micro enterprises to jointly address the challenges and issues of empowerment. They face many constraints, especially those related to the correct supply of inputs and other factors of production such as energy.

## Gender mainstreaming in the implementation at the household and community levels
Gender mainstreaming is not taken into consideration in rural electrification concessions, which are characterized by a diversification of electricity models and managed by private operators. Here, the beneficiaries have access to an intermittent electricity service guaranteed by the grid. The needs for electricity services are varied and huge.

In the places selected by our study, the process of rural electrification raises specific gender issues, notably on:
- the priority given to technical aspects in household access to electricity;
- the difficulties encountered in accessing electricity to stimulate regional development at the productive, economic, entrepreneurial and social levels;
- the lack of electricity for certain services and infrastructure in electrified localities;
- difficulty in integrating women within the economic capacity of women and men through electricity;
- the late integration of the beneficiary populations in the electrification process due to the lack of a predefined role, with women feeling less concerned because they assume that men should take care of electricity-related issues;
- the privileged position of men to be the interlocutors of technicians and their decision-making power within households because of their status as heads of the households;
- the very limited number of women heads of households and their economic situation; a parameter to be considered when it comes to connecting households and payment of bills;

### IV. Constraints and opportunities to incorporate gender considerations

Prior to the conduct of the analysis of gender mainstreaming in the national rural electrification programme, ASER was part of the audit of gender mainstreaming in energy policies and programmes conducted in 2005-2007. The audit exercise was initiated by the International Network for Women and Energy (INWE) and aimed at assessing the impact of the adoption of a more gender-oriented approach in the energy sector, which is known for its high degree of neutrality and lack of attention to gender issues. ASER participated as an expert on gender and energy at the national level. After several attempts to develop a framework for monitoring programme impacts, a gender-sensitive manual to strengthen the results-based management approach was produced by INWE in 2012. It is also a good opportunity for the institutionalization of gender in the agency.

The appropriate tool is the web that allows, from a diagnosis of the problems of a target group, to make an inventory of the actors involved and their relationships with each other, taking into account the different spheres of influence. From this analysis, ASER will have to use this tool (the gender institutionalization web). In our case, it will be necessary to question the gender roles of men and women who will be impacted by the transition to the mini-grid. To this end, ASER must ask itself a certain number of questions: What are the different spheres of influence in which the lives of men and women evolve and how will the various actors in the villages concerned intervene? Faced with the stakes, what will be the pressure groups at the community level in relation to access and control (and) to energy?
