## C. Green Climate Fund Funding Proposal V.2.0

### Dependence of Local Economies

The project aims to deliver indirect resilience benefits to Botswana's national population of 2.3 million by improving local economies dependent on livestock and tourism. An estimated 6,000 individuals will gain professional development as Ecorangers, Restoration Workers, and Graduate Monitors, enhancing their adaptive capacity. Improved management of 4.6 million hectares will build livelihood resilience and enhance economic resilience during extreme climate events.

### Project Impacts

The project will reduce bare ground, bush encroachment, and invasive alien plants, increase water infiltration, and decrease human-wildlife conflicts. Healthier ecosystems will form the basis of an EbA model for Botswana and the region.

## D.2. Paradigm Shift Potential

### Potential for Scaling Up and Replication

Africa's rangelands, covering 1.3 billion hectares, support mega-herbivores, predators, and over 200 million pastoralists. Southern Africa's rangelands, covering over 90% of the region, are under threat from land degradation and climate change. The Herding for Health model could benefit 355 million hectares across 17 countries, generating significant emissions reduction benefits.

### Implementation in Botswana

The project model is relevant for the Chobe area, Kwaneng, and Ghanzi Districts, where herders and farmers are vulnerable to climate change. The project aims to integrate restoration activities into an effective model that improves livestock management and livelihoods.

### Current Implementation

The H4H model is being implemented in Mozambique, South Africa, Zambia, and Kenya, with preparations in Zimbabwe, Lesotho, and Namibia. The project aims to expand to all SADC countries, involving nine million people living near transfrontier conservation areas.

### Knowledge Sharing and Learning

Conservation International is part of platforms like the Evergreening Alliance and AFR100, facilitating the distribution of project lessons globally. The project will be a flagship for the Herding for Health model, contributing to regional understanding and capacity building.

The project will engage various Botswana government ministries, sharing lessons learned to support replication in other countries. The Rangeland Stewardship Portal will be open source, sharing data widely.

### Contribution to the Enabling and Regulatory Environment

The project aims to institutionalize climate-resilient rangeland management practices in Botswana, influencing policies and regulations for replication and scaling. It will transform the Ipelegeng Programme and formalize professional qualifications for herding.

### Anticipated Benefits

The project will enhance local ecosystem health, livelihoods, and food security while contributing to global climate resilience. It aligns with Botswana's National Transformation Strategy priorities, promoting economic diversification and self-reliance.

# Risk Assessment and Management

## Selected Risk Factor 1: Land use conflicts/competition

- **Category**: Technical and operational
- **Probability**: High
- **Impact**: Medium

### Description

Land use that undermines the rest or rehabilitation of areas within a Rangeland Stewardship Agreement is the top risk to Project goals. This risk is greatest in areas targeted for privatisation under the recent revision of the Tribal Grazing Land Policy (2019) and in areas that are proximal to private or group ranches where communal lands are used by both communities and private landowners.

### Mitigation Measures

- Use negotiation process to reduce risk of land-use conflict and identify potential mitigation measures.
- Evaluate geography, availability of labour, mobile fencing, or opportunities for integrating collective herd management with community neighbours and private landholders.
- Share case studies from South Africa of successes and failures relating to community dynamics with the Farmer Facilitation Teams during the Training of Trainers.
- Involve Land Board representatives, traditional authorities, and VDCs in principles of climate smart rangeland management.

## Selected Risk Factor 2: Perverse / counterproductive market incentives

- **Category**: Other
- **Probability**: Medium
- **Impact**: Medium

### Description

Traditional relationships with the Botswana Meat Commission and livestock market middlemen could undermine incentives for smallholders to adopt climate resilient rangeland stewardship practices.

### Mitigation Measures

- Work closely with partner Meat Naturally Botswana (MNP) and other private sector actors to encourage price-based incentives.
- Support BMC by making better payment terms and/or help speculators meet their targets at a lower cost by hosting an auction for communities in Rangeland Stewardship Agreements.

## Selected Risk Factor 3: Institutional and regulatory barriers to integrated and adaptive management

- **Category**: Technical and operational
- **Probability**: Medium
- **Impact**: Medium

### Description

Current government policy and subsidy/incentive programmes for the livestock sector may reduce effectiveness or work against the objectives of the programme.

### Mitigation

- Focus and invest in coordination, policy alignment, and budgetary processes.
- Engage the highest-level decision makers and the broader public as witnesses to Project impacts.
- Ensure cooperation and communication between government ministries, departments, and stakeholders at all levels.

## Selected Risk Factor 4: Natural rangeland ecosystem dynamics prevent recovery

- **Category**: Technical and operational
- **Probability**: Medium
- **Impact**: Medium

### Description

As climate impacts increase, any area of recovery and/or new water sources are likely to attract wildlife resulting in increased grazing pressure.

### Mitigation

- Include management strategies for managing interactions and conflict between wildlife and livestock in Ecoranger training.
- Develop adaptive strategies to modify grazing plans and resource allocations.
- Mobilize fire awareness and basic fire-fighting partnerships with DFRR in all high-risk areas.

## Selected Risk Factor 5: Project staffing and scale-up

- **Category**: Technical and operational
- **Probability**: Low
- **Impact**: Medium

### Description

Implementation of this Project will require CI Botswana to scale up its operations from a small current office to a fully staffed team.

### Mitigation

- Structure the timeline for the first year to allow for sufficient time for staff recruitment, onboarding, training, and capacity-building.
- Use a transparent and well-publicised open recruitment process to fill all positions.
- Create mechanisms for sharing expertise and experience from across the organization with new project staff.

## Selected Risk Factor 6: Prohibited Practices, including ML/FT

- **Category**: Prohibited practices
- **Probability**: Low
- **Impact**: Medium

### Description

The Project involves a wide array of actors, including government, sub-grantees, vendors and service providers, and beneficiaries.

### Mitigation

- Apply rigorous CTF/AML screening to all CI funding recipients.
- Conduct an assessment of Prohibited Practices (PP) risks of grant recipients.
- Ensure all staff and subgrantees undergo training on PP.
- Provide mechanisms for reporting grievances or violations anonymously.

For more details, visit [CI’s website](https://www.conservation.org/about/our-policies/reporting-illegal-or-unethical-conduct-statement).

## 3.4.3.2 Communal Grazing Land

Local authorities in Tsetsebjwe (Bobirwa) noted insufficient grazing as villages converge, reducing communal areas. Self-allocation of land is common, with ploughing fields managed by the Land Board, while cattle posts are self-allocated. In Ngamiland, buffalo and buffer zone fences limit land for elephants, livestock, and humans. Wildlife deaths due to dried rivers impact tourism. Government compensates for elephant damage, but not repeatedly. Cattle roaming freely also damage crops, a challenge the project aims to address.

Communities feel powerless due to boreholes and fences. Ngamiland faces Foot and Mouth Disease, controlled by fences. Plans to drill boreholes may increase wildlife presence, causing injuries. In Hunhukwe, forming borehole syndicates is difficult due to land reduction.

## 3.4.4 Livestock Ownership

The Livestock Management and Infrastructure Development (LIMID) program supports women in purchasing small stock. In 2010, women were 78% of LIMID beneficiaries. Men often choose borehole drilling, which requires a down payment many women can't afford. Cattle ownership is male-dominated, with women often owning cattle through marriage. Women in Central Bobonong own 35% of cattle, 25% in Ngamiland, and 19% in Kgalagadi.

### Goats Population by Gender and District

Women own a significant proportion of goats: 40% in Central Bobonong, 48% in Ngamiland, and 65% in Kgalagadi. Drought and disease have caused livestock losses. The 2017 Agricultural Survey indicated reduced livestock numbers due to drought. In Ngamiland, cattle die in drying rivers, and borehole water has become saline. Bobirwa experienced high livestock mortality during droughts, with goats also dying.

Cultural practices affect livestock ownership, with men controlling cattle brought into marriage. Customary law favors men in inheritance, disadvantaging women. The project aims to ensure equitable participation of women in livestock management.

## 3.4.5 Access to Water

Land allocation impacts water resources. Borehole ownership is male-dominated, with women owning 27%. Water scarcity is high in Bobirwa, with boreholes and rivers drying up. Women rely on government standpipes and buy water. In water-stressed areas, goats are given potable water, which is illegal. The project will improve water access for livestock.

## 3.4.6 Access to Information and Technology

Mobile phone access is high, with 3.5 million subscribers. Women and men have similar access to mobile phones and internet. Radio and government newspapers are other information sources. Kgotla meetings are a key communication method, though announcements may not reach everyone. Women often attend these meetings and share information with their families.

## 3.5 Decision Making

In 2012, women held 30.6% of decision-making positions in political parties. In 2019, only a fifth of Cabinet members were women. Women are underrepresented in politics and local authorities, highlighting gender disparities in decision-making roles.

## The Local Government System

Botswana has a two-tier system of government: the national government, headed by the president, and councils forming the second tier. Urban councils are led by a mayor, while rural districts have a council chairperson. Tribal administration, an institution of traditional village leadership, supports rural development initiatives and comprises judicial and administrative units. The Local Government Act 2012 establishes village development committees (VDCs) to coordinate local development, promoting a bottom-up planning approach.

### Village Development Committees (VDCs)

VDCs are composed of community-elected members, with a maximum of ten members. Women often outnumber men in these committees, indicating their significant role in local decision-making. Chiefs are ex-officio members, and VDCs are highly regarded in villages, second only to the chief in decision-making.

### Community Based Natural Resource Management

Community-based organizations (CBOs) manage natural resources, with boards having a near-equal gender balance. However, youth representation is low. A notable example is a community water agreement in Tshane, chaired by a woman.

### Other CBOs

Few men participate in associations, while women are more involved in PTAs and organizations like the Red Cross. The project aims to ensure equal participation of women and men.

## Gender Based Violence

Gender-based violence (GBV) is prevalent in Botswana, with 67% of women experiencing it. Intimate partner violence (IPV) is common, and climate change exacerbates GBV by increasing social and financial stresses. Projects should prioritize gender-responsive actions and include GBV considerations in policies and awareness materials.

### Intergenerational Vulnerability

Single women, though lacking social safeguards, have more autonomy in decision-making than married women. Older women, often widows, have many dependents and face financial challenges. Empowering single women may offer strategic entry points for project success.
