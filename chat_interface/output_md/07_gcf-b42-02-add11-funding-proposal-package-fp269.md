# B. PROGRAMME INFORMATION

## B.1. Climate context

For more details, see Annex 2 (Feasibility Study)

12. Smallholder farmers dominate agriculture in East Africa. They rely heavily on livestock for their livelihoods, more particularly dairy, which are found in various production systems and diverse agroecologies, all driven by climate. The region is dominated by a tropical savannah climate, separated by a corridor of arid steppe and desert climates parallel to the Indian Ocean coastline and northwest of Kenya, as well as in the central parts of Tanzania. Tropical rainforest conditions are found in the Lake Victoria and temperate climates are present along the highest altitude areas of the Great Rift Valley bordering Kenya and Tanzania. Kenya is the driest among the four targeted countries, with a total annual rainfall ranging between 400 to 600mm in the northwest of Lake Turkana and in areas bordering Somalia. Conversely, Uganda and Rwanda have the highest total annual precipitation, ranging between 1000 and 1600mm. These conditions lead to a mosaic of climates and, consequently, to a wide range of agroecological zones.

13. The dairy sector in Kenya, Rwanda, Tanzania, and Uganda is highly vulnerable to climate change (CC) and is characterized by high GHG emissions, especially methane. The four countries face significant CC challenges, including extreme weather events and changing rainfall patterns. In the dairy sector, the impacts of CC include heat stress on dairy animals, prolonged and more recurrent droughts, intensifying heavy rainfall, flooding, and other extreme weather events that reduce dairy cow productivity. Indirect effects include changes in disease patterns and occurrence, decreased feed and water availability, and decreased pasture quality. As a result, the Programme is cross-cutting and addresses both mitigation and adaptation. Section B.1 describes the adaptation needs following IPCC’s AR5 Risk Conceptualization (compounded effect of observed and projected climate hazards, exposure, vulnerability, modulated by the existing adaptive capacity), as well as the mitigation needs based on the GHG emission profile of the dairy sector.

### CLIMATE HAZARDS

14. East Africa's dairy systems and their spatial distribution are diverse, exposing dairy farmers’ livelihoods to a wide range of hazards. Climate change poses risks both because of the potential impacts of weather hazards and human responses to modulate their effects. The analysis of livestock and dairy exposure to observed and projected weather hazards is supported by geospatial datasets on flood and drought prone areas.

#### Observed climate change

15. Precipitation declined over most of the four countries during the 1950s (-6.7mm/yr.) and the 1960s (-21.8mm/yr.) but increased during the 1970s (+16.0mm/yr.). There are also statistically significant differences on seasonal precipitation trends (p-value 0.05) across central Uganda between January and March, extending to the north of Kenya and northern Tanzania between April and June where seasonal precipitation rates have declined at a rate of -4 to -8 mm/yr. over 1981-2016. On the other hand, a precipitation increase has also been observed in the southeast of Tanzania during the austral summer (January to March) over 1981-2016.

16. Temperature. The annual average maximum temperature (mean Tmax) has increased significantly (p-value 0.05) across Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania. In Uganda (Eastern and Northern region) and Kenya (north of Lake Turkana County), for example, the annual mean Tmax has increased by +0.030 to +0.035°C/yr., equivalent to +1.1 to +1.3°C during 1981-2016. In addition to having the highest mean Tmax increase, the Kenyan north Rift Valley has the warmest mean Tmax year-round among the four targeted countries. There is also a significant change in average daily minimum temperature (mean Tmin) in Kenya and Tanzania, but not in Rwanda or the west of Uganda. For mean Tmin, the increase is observed in the east of Uganda and the northwest of Kenya where temperatures have increased by +0.035ºC/yr., equivalent to +1.3ºC during 1981-2016.

17. Mean relative humidity. The Rift Valley in Kenya has the lowest average annual relative humidity (mean RH <50 percent), while central and southern Uganda, especially the areas bordering Lake Victoria, have the highest mean RH (75-85 percent). The Kenyan and Tanzanian coastlines are characterized by high mean RH (70 to 80 percent) all year round due to the incoming moisture from the Indian Ocean. A statistically significant decrease (p-value 0.05) in mean RH between -0.10 and -0.25 percent/year was observed in most areas of Uganda and Rwanda between 1981 and 2016. In addition, eastern and northeastern Kenya have experienced a statistically significant decrease (p-value 0.05), while central and southern Tanzania have experienced the largest decline (-0.10 to -0.15 percent/year).

18. Rainfall variability. The region’s climatic seasons are influenced by a coupled transboundary ocean-atmosphere phenomenon called the Inter Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ). A long rainy season occurs from March to May (MAM), and a short rainy season from October to December (OND) when the ITCZ migrates southwards. There is high rainfall variability in OND due to large-scale oceanic-atmospheric patterns such as the El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD), which frequently coincides with ENSO events. The relationship between the 3-month Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI-3) and ENSO is significant for understanding precipitation variability in East Africa. SPI-3 categorizes precipitation conditions, indicating whether they are normal (-0.99 and +0.99), moderately dry (-1.49 to -1.0) and moderately humid (+1.0 to +1.49), severely dry (-1.99 to -1.5) and severely humid (+1.5 to +1.99), and extremely dry (≤ -2.0) and extremely humid (≥ +2.0). During the strong El Niño of 1997-1998, SPI-3 values indicated very humid conditions, while the subsequent strong La Niña of 1999-2000 resulted in severe dry conditions over East Africa. The impacts of ENSO are apparent, since peak ENSO events occur between October and March, coinciding with the short (October to November) and long (March to May) rainy seasons in East Africa, further amplifying precipitation anomalies.

#### Extreme weather events

19. Dry-spells and very wet days. The average annual number of dry days (R≤1mm/day) in East Africa exceeds 300 days in the northern Rift Valley and northeastern Kenya. Conversely, the annual number of dry days decreases toward Lake Victoria (including Mount Elgon in Uganda and Kenya) and the tropical rainforest near the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). On average, Tanzania records 180 dry days/yr. along its border with Uganda and Rwanda and over 280 days in its interior. The Indian Ocean coastline has seen an increase in dry days by +1.0 days/yr., equivalent to +36 dry days per year since 1981. In eastern Uganda, the number of dry days by -0.50 days/yr., equivalent to +18 wet days on average each year since 1981. Trends on very wet days (R≥20mm/day) show statistically significant differences (p-value <0.05) along the Great Rift Valley in Kenya and Tanzania, with an average annual increase of +0.15 days/yr. over the period 1981-2016, equivalent to an additional +5 very wet days on annual average since 1981. In contrast, neither the interior of Kenya, Tanzania nor Uganda have seen an increase or decrease in the number of very wet days since 1981.

20. Extreme temperatures. Most of the change in the number of very warm days (Tmax ≥35ºC) on annual average is reported in the northern Rift Valley, where the number of very warm days has increased by +1.2 to +2.8 days/yr., which is equivalent to an additional 40 to 100 very warm days as an annual average since 1981. In addition, northern Uganda experienced an increase in very warm days at a rate of +0.8 days/yr. although this increase is not statistically significant.

#### Projected climate change

21. Annual precipitation. Some parts of East Africa may experience an increase in rainfall in the distant future under various Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs). Arid and semi-arid regions are expected to receive less precipitation, while the highlands and lake regions may receive more rainfall. Dry seasons in Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda could see an increase in precipitation. Future projections indicate an eastward increase in Kenya under RCP 2.6 (+100mm), accentuated under RCP 8.5 (+100 to +200mm) in the distant future (2070-2099). In Rwanda, a decrease in rainfall is expected under RCP 2.6 (-0.20mm/yr.) and an increase under RCP 8.5 (+0.18mm/yr.). In Uganda, climate projections show a decrease in rainfall of -0.30mm/yr. under both RCPs.

22. Mean maximum temperatures. Under RCP 2.6, the mean Tmax are expected to peak in the mid-term (2040-2069); whereas under RCP 8.5, the highest mean Tmax increase is expected by the end of the century (2070-2099). Under RCP 8.5, mean Tmax may increase by up to +4.5oC in central parts of East Africa by the end of the century (2070-2099). The rate of increase in both RCPs is slower (+1 to +2oC depending on the RCP) towards the ocean since water can hold more heat than air, thereby taking longer to warm up than continental parts. Accordingly, the coastal areas of Kenya and Tanzania may experience a mean Tax increase of +0.5 to +1.0oC (RCP 2.6) and +2.0 to +3.0oC (RCP 8.5), respectively, by the end-of-century (2070-2099) compared to the baseline period (1976-2005). Similarly, because of its large size, Lake Victoria may warm more slowly than its surroundings and, thus, have a lower rate of mean Tmax increase over time. Overall, most models agree on a positive climate change signal for mean Tmax.

23. Mean minimum temperatures. The mean Tmin increase over East Africa is expected to follow the same spatiotemporal pattern as mean Tmax. However, the rate of change in mean Tmin is slightly higher to that of mean Tmax. Overall, mean Tmin could increase by +1.5 to +2.0ºC (RCP 2.6) and by +4.0 to +5.0ºC (RCP 8.5) inland and by less than +1.5ºC (RCP 2.6) and +3.0ºC (RCP 8.5) on the Tanzanian and Kenyan coast by 2070-2099 compared to 1976-2005.

24. The Mean relative humidity (mean RH) will change over the course of the century for RCPs 2.6 and 8.5. The annual mean RH (+2 %) is expected to increase along Kenya and Lake Victoria. Under RCP 8.5, however, the mean RH is expected to decrease on a yearly average (-2 to -8 %) in the central parts of Tanzania, with a high rate towards the south.

25. Rainfall variability. In all countries, positive SPI-3 values will remain lower under RCP 8.5 than RCP 2.6, implying fewer wet-years under RCP 8.5. For negative SPI-3 values (dry periods), negative trends are accentuated in Rwanda, Uganda, and Tanzania, implying that 3-month precipitation values are increasingly more dispersed from the mean.

26. Dry spells. The models show a positive agreement in the time of emergence on the number of dry days (R≤1mm/day), slightly earlier and widespread for RCP 8.5 (2030-2050) compared to RCP 2.6 (2040-2060). The positive change (increase in the number of dry days) is widespread throughout East Africa, particularly towards western Kenya, all of Uganda and Rwanda, and most parts of Tanzania. Conversely, the timing of the emergence of negative values (reduction in number of dry days) is projected from 2060 onwards for RCP 8.5 and a little earlier for RCP 2.6, mainly in eastern and southern Kenya. In addition, most of the increase in very wet days (R≥20mm/day) is projected in southwestern Kenya under RCP 2.6, where there could be an extra +1 to +2 very wet days on average per year by the end of the century (2070-2099). For RCP 8.5, the increase in very wet days is widespread over Uganda, Rwanda, Tanzania, and southwestern Kenya.

27. Extreme temperatures. Very warm days (Tmax ≥35ºC) are expected to increase in frequency and intensity. In the low emission scenario (RCP 2.6), +25 to +75 additional very warm days on average per year are expected in Uganda, the Rift Valley of Kenya, and central Tanzania in the mid-term (2040-2069). Most of the increase in very warm days is projected by the end of the century (2070-2099) under RCP 8.5, showing an increase in the range of 100 to 200 additional very warm days on average per year compared to 1981-2010. On the contrary, the cooling effect of large water masses such as Lake Victoria and the Indian Ocean can significantly modulate the upward trend of very warm days over time.

### EXPOSURE

28. Exposure is the presence of people, livelihoods, species, and ecosystems that could be affected by hazards, more particularly droughts and floods that cause disasters.

29. In East Africa, droughts tend to be more frequent, longer, and more severe in boreal spring and summer, as rainfall and water storage decrease overall. Table 1 shows how drought will change under different climate scenarios. Haile et al. (2020) show that the drought area in East Africa will increase, with extreme droughts increasing faster than severe and moderate droughts. For example, by the end of the century (2080s), severe droughts are expected to increase by 13.5 percent under RCP 8.5. In general, farmers can cope with mild droughts, but severe droughts lead to large-scale livestock losses, decreased pasture regeneration, and an increased prevalence of drought-related livestock diseases. Overall, droughts affect the availability and quality of water for pasture regeneration, feed production, water and hygienic practices in the dairy production system.

30. Floods. East Africa experiences frequent flooding due to climate variability and anthropogenic factors that exacerbate livestock exposure to flooding. Among its flood-prone areas, Uganda has the highest number of flood-related casualties, particularly in the Northern, Eastern, and Central regions along the White Nile River. Each year, floods affect between 10,000 and 25,000 people per administrative boundary in these regions of Uganda, with up to 36,000 people per administrative boundary in the Northern region. In Tanzania, floods affect 10,000 to 25,000 people per administrative boundary in Tanga, Morogoro, and Pwani. In contrast, in the central parts of Kenya, fewer than 100 people per administrative boundary were affected by floods on an annual basis. Due to the increase in population and increased rainfall in Lake Victoria under RCP 8.5, flooding along the White River Nile is likely to increase.

31. Disasters. In East Africa, between 1971 and 2023, more than 95 million people were affected by disasters caused by meteorological (storms), hydrological (floods, landslides) and climatological (droughts) events. Since 1971, 248 disasters have occurred, of which 78 disasters in Kenya, 70 in Tanzania, 60 in Uganda, and 40 in Rwanda. About 69 percent related to floods and 17 percent to drought. Over time, the number of disasters has increased exponentially, from 28 during 1971-1990 to 174 during 2001-2020. The most common disaster is flooding, followed by drought and landslides. Between 1900 and 2023, 176 floods were recorded in the targeted countries, most of them river floods caused by heavy rainfall. Evidence shows that women and men are affected by drought differently, and that gender inequalities diminish women’s capacity to cope with these calamities.

### VULNERABILITY

32. CC also poses many challenges and threats to the East African dairy sector, which is subject to pre-existing physical and socio-economic vulnerabilities. By reducing these vulnerabilities, which will be exacerbated by climate change, the sector becomes more resilient. These vulnerabilities are presented below:

a) Poverty and income inequality. Kenyan, Tanzanian, Rwandan and Ugandan societies are marked by important poverty and income inequality. The Human Development index (HDI) 2022 ranking in Kenya (146th), Rwanda (159th), Uganda (159th) and Tanzania (160th) reflect low achievements in some of the key dimensions of human development, while high GINI indices (38.7 in Kenya, 43.7 in Rwanda, 40.5 in Tanzania and 42.7 in Uganda) evidence strong income inequalities. The vulnerable – more particularly poor dairy farmers and women - will be the most affected by climate change.

b) Degraded of rangelands and ecosystems. The availability of feed depends on the quality of rangelands, which are degraded in the region. Figure 8 shows that degraded land accounts for 27 percent of total rangelands areas in Tanzania, 3.6 percent in Kenya, 2.1 percent in Uganda and 2.1 percent in Rwanda. The main factors leading to the degradation of rangelands and pasture are soil structure and erodibility, climate (rainfall intensity and temperature), reduction of vegetation cover and topography (influencing water runoff capacity). Degradation prevents rangelands from providing ecosystem services such as provisioning (production of above-ground biomass, such as grass, as well as water) and regulating (such as the capacity of rangelands to sequester carbon in the soils). It also has a negative impact on the forage balance, as well as on the livestock carrying capacity, which highlights the pressure that the land is under in terms of maximum number of animals that the ecosystem can sustainably support over time, without causing damages to the environment.

c) Limited access to quality inputs. Feed that reduces enteric emissions, veterinary services and climate-resilient breeding stocks are difficult to access for dairy smallholder farmers. Limited veterinary services lead farmers to prefer local or crossbred animals that are not as high yielding, whereas exotic breeds are highly susceptible to local disease. In addition, limited access to improved breeds hinders genetic progress.

d) Limited water availability. Climate change affects water resources with changes in precipitation patterns leading to water scarcity or excessive water supply in different regions. In food insecure areas, limited access to safe and reliable water resources, and limited water harvesting systems can hinder livestock and dairy production, contributing to food insecurity and increased vulnerability, especially for women who often provide labour for livestock.

e) Frequent disease outbreaks. Foot-and-mouth disease, mastitis, and East Coast Fever (ECF) are common livestock diseases. Disease outbreaks result in a high mortality rate, reduced milk production, and increased veterinary costs. Poor disease control measures, inadequate quarantine facilities, and limited veterinary services contribute to the prevalence of diseases in the dairy farming sector.

## Dairy Processing Facilities, Energy Supply and Access to Markets

Dairy farmers in East Africa face inadequate infrastructure along the dairy value chain. The availability of reliable (and sustainable) energy supply is also irregular, affecting the ability to cool and process milk, and reduce losses. Price fluctuations and market volatility are common challenges. Most milk prices are determined by bulkers and processors. Access to stable higher-value-added markets is further hindered by limited outreach of milk collection centers (MCCs), dairy cooperatives, and business alliances. More details are provided in the Market Assessment (Annex 2).

## Limited Access to Credit and Financial Services

Critical aspects of dairy farming, such as infrastructure, animal health, and feed, require access to credit and finance. Small-scale dairy farmers and pastoralists, especially women and young people, have difficulties accessing these services due to lack of collateral, which limits investments in modern equipment, infrastructure, and improved livestock.

## Land Tenure

In Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda, a main risk for intensive grazing is tenure insecurity, i.e., tenure rights or land ownership. Access to natural capital, particularly women’s and youth ownership, management, and control of land and land resources are major barriers to sustainability, and the adoption of climate-resilient and low-carbon practices (Annex 2).

## Climate Adaptive Capacity

About 70-90 percent of East African agriculture is rain-fed, with small-scale farmers relying on seasonal rainfall for feed, fodder crops, and pasture. As dairy systems are subject to significant seasonal, temporal, and spatial variations, dairy farmers face extreme difficulties in planning investments, which also become riskier under climate change (CC). The resilience of the dairy system depends on its ability to adapt to different climate conditions and to cope with the impacts of CC. Identifying adaptation needs facilitates prioritizing actions and allocating resources for addressing critical challenges.

Rising temperatures are disrupting agriculture, livestock, and dairy, the economic backbone of East Africa. Water scarcity, pest outbreaks, and declining productivity threaten food and nutrition security. Droughts and deforestation exacerbate soil erosion. The intensification and frequency of droughts will hinder access to water under different climate scenarios. Areas affected by extreme drought will expand faster than those affected by severe and moderate droughts, especially under RCP 8.5. Storms and floods have become more frequent and intense, causing deaths, damage to infrastructure, and loss of livelihoods. The dairy sector’s adaptive capacity stems from the following:

### Access to Climate-Smart Practices and Technologies

Improved dairy breeds, drought-tolerant forage varieties, and efficient milk processing equipment are some of the climate-smart technologies farmers need. These practices and technologies should be labor-saving to reduce workloads for women.

### Capacity Building and Knowledge Transfer

Farmers' technical knowledge and skills in dairy management can be enhanced by providing training programmes, Livestock Farmer Field Schools (L-FFS), and extension services on sustainable feeding practices, breeding strategies, disease control measures, and water resource efficiency.

### Financial and Market Support

Affordable financing supports farmers' investments in productive assets, infrastructure, and value-addition. Dairy cooperatives and value chain linkages can improve farmers' incomes. Agribusiness can provide input and output markets that improve the productivity of smallholder dairy farmers.

### Strengthening Infrastructure

Improving rural infrastructure, such as water harvesting systems, renewable energy, milk collection, and processing facilities, will reduce transport costs and improve market access for dairy farmers, and reduce the drudgery associated with fetching water by women.

### Climate Information

Informed decisions on feed management, breeding, and disease control can be made through climate information, early warning systems, and advisory services. This will help dairy farmers anticipate and adapt to climate-related risks. These skills equip youth as agents and service providers in the dissemination of new information.

### Policy Support and Institutional Strengthening

To enable dairy farmers to thrive, it is imperative to develop policies, regulations, and institutions that are tailored to their needs, especially addressing barriers related to gender inequalities and youth, as well as strengthening research and extension actors. Key adaptation requirements require collaboration between governments, research institutes, dairy cooperatives, and financial institutions.

## Impact of Climate Change

East Africa’s dairy sector is one of the most vulnerable sectors to climate change, the impact of which is felt at all levels of the dairy value chain. The impacts at the dairy farm level are as follows: (i) a decline in milk yields, especially of high milk-yielding breeds; (ii) heat stress in dairy cattle; (iii) increased development of pathogens, diseases, parasites, and vectors in dairy cattle; (iv) decreased feed production and productivity, due to reduced or excess soil moisture and fertility; (v) a decrease in the quantity and quality of pasture; (vi) deterioration of fodder in an unprotected warehouse. At the rangeland level, livestock owners face: (i) reduced access to rangelands resources; (ii) ecosystem degradation; (iii) a decrease in rangeland and pasture productivity and forage quality. At the dairy value chain level, high temperatures compromise the safety of milk and dairy products, higher risks of milk losses, and higher costs per unit of produce.

## Climate Change and Milk Yields

Temperature and humidity have a significant impact on milk production. Significant milk production losses have been observed in Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania’s coastal regions between 1981 and 2016, from 4 to 8 kg/day for the Holstein-Friesian breed and between 0.5 and 1 kg/day for the Boran breed. Over the period 1981-2016, milk production declined less than 2 kg/day for the Holstein-Friesian breed in the high-altitude areas of Kenya, Tanzania, and Rwanda. Future milk production is expected to decrease up to 1 kg/day for Holstein-Friesian cows and up to 0.15 kg/day for Boran animals by the end of the century under RCP 2.6, and even up to 2.5 kg/day for Holstein Friesian cows and 0.5 kg/day for Boran cows under RCP 8.5. Heat stress also causes reproductive problems (missed mating or insemination).

## Ruminant Feed and Forage Supplies

Ruminant feed and forage supplies are most affected by climate change due to changes in cereal, forage, and rangeland productivity. Changes in temperature and CO2 levels will affect the composition of pastures by altering the species competition dynamics due to changes in optimal growth rates. In addition, availability of rainfall determines rainfed crop production. Feed and fodder are available in the rainy and early dry seasons and sparse in the dry season, leading to strong seasonal fluctuations in milk production and milk prices. Even during the rainy seasons, there is often not adequate forage (fodder and pasture) to be stored for utilization during the dry season. Feed availability fluctuates throughout the year, challenging livestock production. It can also lead to conflicts between communities and competition with wildlife in pastoral areas. Pastoral communities are more vulnerable to feed shortages because of droughts, degradation of rangelands, and limited access to grazing lands. Both RCPs predict the greatest yield change in the north of Kenya and Uganda in the mid- and long-terms (2031-2060 and 2061-2090). Under RCP 2.6, maize yields, commonly used as feed, are likely to decline by 5 to 15 percent in Uganda and by 20 to 25 percent in Kenya under RCP 8.5. Napier grass yields are likely to decline mostly in Uganda (e.g., North region by more than 30 percent), the north of Kenya by 30 percent, and the southeast of Tanzania by 20 to 30 percent. Under RCP 8.5, Napier grass yield losses are up to 10 percent lower in the southwest of Tanzania both in the mid-term (2031-2060) and into the future (2061-2090). For maize, models show that Africa is likely to suffer up to 10 percent yield loss by 2055 due to CC.

## Climate Change and Livestock Diseases

Climate change will affect pathogens, vectors, hosts, or reservoirs, as well as human behavior. Estimating climate change effects on livestock diseases is difficult due to the complex interactions between climatic and non-climatic factors. Temperature and humidity play a significant role in pathogen and parasite development. Wind patterns affect the movement of locusts and pathogens. Pathogen propagation may be limited by hot and dry conditions, changes in rainfall and temperature regimes which influence disease vector distribution, such as ticks, mosquitoes, and flies. Vector dynamics are also influenced by soil and vegetation type, and topography. In most cases, vectors and pathogens negatively affect livestock. Zoonotic diseases spread during droughts and feed shortages. Further details are available in Annex 2, Chapter 2.

## Impact on Food and Nutrition Security

The above impacts of climate change on milk production at the farm level will have a direct impact on national and household food and nutrition security. At the household level, less milk will be available for dairy consumption, with an immediate impact on the nutrition and health of young children and women. It will also reduce revenues from sales of milk. In the four countries, small daily revenues from the sales of surplus milk, often 1 to 5 liters per household, are essential for rural poverty reduction. At the national level, a reduction of domestic milk production will lead to higher imports from the international market and require monetary reserves. Also, a higher dependence on the international market to purchase feed, in particular maize and soybean, will impact the national balance of trade.

## Greenhouse Gas Emission Profile of the Dairy Sector

### Dairy Production Systems

In Kenya, 39 percent of milk is produced by intensive systems or zero-grazing systems, 43 percent by semi-intensive systems, and 18 percent by extensive systems. In Rwanda, 93 percent of the milk is produced in intensive/zero grazing systems, 4 percent in semi-intensive, and 3 percent in extensive. In Tanzania, dairy systems are traditional and intensive. The traditional system is characterized by meat-milk production and large herds of cattle grazing natural pastures, producing 91 percent of the country’s milk. In Uganda, systems are also traditional and intensive. Traditional systems produce 98 percent of the country’s milk.

### Absolute GHG Emissions

Methane (CH4), nitrous oxide (N2O), and carbon dioxide (CO2) are released by ruminants (cattle, sheep, goats) during the enteric fermentation and through manure production. East Africa represents about 5% of GHG emissions of the global cattle sector, but they are increasing. Enteric methane emissions from the dairy sector dominate the GHG emission inventories of Kenya, Rwanda, Tanzania, and Uganda, followed by cattle manure. Enteric methane is not only a short-lived climate pollutant, but it represents a waste in the production process that doesn’t result in milk or meat. In addition, cattle manure releases N2O and CH4. Over 90 percent of dairy sector emissions occur on farm. Land-use changes and degradation further contribute to GHG emissions due to the disruption in carbon stocks.

### Emission Intensities (EI)

The methane emission intensity, defined as the volume of methane emissions generated per kg of milk, varies widely across intensive, semi-intensive, and extensive dairy production systems, with more productive systems having much lower EIs per kg of milk. In East Africa, EIs vary from 1.5 kg CO2eq./kg milk for exotic dairy breeds in Kenya to 24.2 kg CO2eq./kg milk for traditional systems in Uganda. This shows the range of performances between (but also within) systems and the potential for improvements, including by importance of increasing the productivity in dairy production and promoting intensive and semi-intensive practices from dairy systems with a low emission intensity per kg of milk.

## Proposed Climate Solutions

The Programme’s interventions will address the climate change adaptation and mitigation issues across the various dairy production systems and along the dairy value-chain, while addressing the barriers identified in the theory of change. It will scale-up: (i) farm and herd level practices to increase productivity, reduce emission intensity, and decrease absolute emissions compared to a BAU scenario; (ii) farm level solutions for manure management and nutrient recycling, to improve circularity, resilience, and reduce emissions; (iii) landscape-level interventions to improve the resilience and services of rangelands ecosystems, particularly soil carbon sequestration, and water and biomass supply for animal feed; and (iv) value-chain level interventions, to reduce milk losses (and corresponding GHG emissions) and increase the use of renewable energy. This holistic and sustainable approach, that will also address enablers such as access to markets, finance, knowledge, and services, will lead to lower emissions from the dairy sector when compared to the business-as-usual scenario.

### Interventions at Farm and Herd Levels

Interventions will focus on the various climate risks and impacts, namely the decrease in milk yields, heat stress of animals, the increase in cattle mortality, the change in dairy cattle diseases, the decrease in feed productivity, the deterioration of fodder in unprotected warehouses due to excess humidity. For all dairy systems, the Programme will encourage the combined adoption of climate adaptation and mitigation practices, including the enhancement of animal genetic resources (artificial insemination with locally-adapted improved and indigenous livestock breeds, sexed semen), improved herd health (e.g., vaccination against East Coast Fever), animal welfare, water access, recycling, harvesting, and reuse for cattle watering. In intensive and semi-intensive systems, in addition to the above, interventions will improve the availability of forage grasses and legumes, feeding practices, and use of conserved silage and complementary feed. In intensive systems, interventions will include the establishment of forage grasses and legumes (Calliandra, Leucaena, etc.), the use of conserved silage and urea-molasses multi-nutrient blocks (UMMB), the use of methane inhibitors (e.g., tannins rich Calliandra, lipids), supplementation (use of non-conventional feed resources, e.g., biochar or sweet potato vines), precision feeding, and improving animal welfare and housing. In extensive systems, activities will include improving pasture management (e.g., rotational grazing), the use of improved pasture species and management practices to improve forage quality and quantity, and use of multipurpose leguminous trees.

### On-Farm Solutions for Manure Management and Nutrient Recycling

These interventions will address the CH4 and N2O emissions on dairy farms and limits related to the cost and availability of mineral fertilizers. Interventions include promoting simple circular manure management practices to reduce nutrient losses and GHG emissions (covering, compaction, composting, liquid-solid separation, production, and addition of biochar). The Programme will also invest in biogas solutions to produce renewable energy for cooking and electricity, as well as nutrient-rich biofertilizer.

### Interventions at the Landscape Level

Interventions at the landscape level will address the negative impact of CC on ecosystem degradation (rangeland and pastures) that result in reduced soil carbon sequestration, decreased quantity and quality of pasture, reduced feed and water availability, and livestock carrying capacity, all leading to lower milk yields. The interventions will focus on improved rangeland management, through participatory learning and promoting sustainable grazing practices. Land management activities will rehabilitate degraded rangelands to increase soil carbon sequestration, promote access to fodder, and address the natural barrier to rangeland degradation. In the four countries, soil carbon impacts were identified as a strategy for carbon sequestration activities such as grazing intensity management, mowing frequency, organic inputs, and fertilizers used in fodder production, and improved agroforestry systems associated with mixed crop-livestock production systems.

### Interventions at the Dairy Value Chain Level

Interventions at the dairy value chain level will include the upgrading or equipping MCCs, milk collection points (MCPs), and processing centers. This will increase the supply of milk through formal aggregation and processing channels and create a stable market outlet for dairy farmers. Interventions will also aim to reduce milk losses and its carbon footprint, and reduce GHG emissions through the use of photovoltaic (PV) systems as a renewable energy (RE) source for milk collection, cooling, and processing.

### Interventions to Improve the Enabling Environment

The focus will be on (i) a climate-sensitive policy and regulatory environment (institutional framework, dairy value chain governance, farmer awareness campaigns) for the dairy sector; (ii) strengthening proximity public and private services to the dairy sector, in particular expanding access to veterinary services and innovative gender-inclusive advisory services combined with input supply services; (iii) strengthening dairy cooperatives, MCCs, and MCPs to encourage farmers to adopt the above-mentioned pathways. Improved MRV capabilities will provide more precise Tier2-emissions data and improve the quality of national inventories, as well as policies, planning, and investment decisions. Through regional cooperation, knowledge sharing, and joint learning, coordinated strategies will be adopted and efforts of the individual countries will be amplified.

## II. Summary of the Secretariat’s assessment

### 2.1 Programme background

3. The dairy sector in East Africa, including Kenya, Rwanda, Uganda and the United Republic of Tanzania, faces significant challenges due to climate change. Dairy farming is a major contributor to greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in each of these countries and the effects of climate change threaten its long-term sustainability. Inefficiencies in dairy farming lead to high GHG emission intensities per kilogram of milk. Additionally, many dairy farmers struggle with limited market access, challenges in sourcing local feed and fodder, inadequate extension and financial services, and difficulties in risk management. The sector is further impacted by low livestock genetic potential, herd management issues, animal diseases, insufficient extension support, limited awareness of climate-resilient and low-carbon practices and technologies, rangeland degradation and gender inequalities.

4. To address the above-mentioned challenges, this programme, “DaIMA – Dairy Interventions for Mitigation and Adaptation”, will have a comprehensive and holistic approach to improve all aspects of the dairy sector, from improving policy, institutional and service delivery environments to building the capacity of smallholder farmers, including women, youth and marginalized groups, by strengthening climate resilience, reducing emissions across the dairy value chain and improving the livelihoods of dairy farmers in East Africa. It also involves strategies to improve access to finance and innovative technologies that offer both mitigation and adaptation benefits. As a result, dairy farmers and processors will be encouraged to adopt more efficient, climate-resilient practices that enhance incomes and lower production costs.

5. The total budget for the programme is USD 358.26 million. USD 58.20 million is allocated for component 1, USD 234.98 million for component 2 and USD 45.0 million for the Green Dairy Financing Facility (GDFF) (component 3), with the remaining USD 13.36 million allocated for programme management and USD 6.72 million for monitoring and evaluation (M&E).

6. The programme will impact 2.5 million direct and 15.4 million indirect beneficiaries across all four countries. The programme aims to reduce the milk emission intensities by 29 per cent (of which 28 per cent is from Kenya, 50 per cent from Rwanda, 33 per cent from Uganda and 29 per cent from the United Republic of Tanzania) over a 20-year lifespan. The programme is also expected to reduce total GHG emissions on the targeted dairy farms, while significantly increasing milk production by 34 per cent (of which 31 per cent is from Kenya, 73 per cent from Rwanda, 30 per cent from Uganda and 36 per cent from the United Republic of Tanzania).

7. Furthermore, it is projected to reduce total GHG emissions from milk production by 4.01 per cent. Specifically, emissions are expected to decline by 2.02 per cent (61,967 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent (t CO₂ eq)) in Kenya, 9.56 per cent (56,507 t CO₂ eq) in Rwanda, 5.22 per cent (82,372 t CO₂ eq) in Uganda and 4.69 per cent (65,718 t CO₂ eq) in the United Republic of Tanzania. Additionally, methane emissions from enteric fermentation are anticipated to drop by 4.6 per cent, while methane from manure management is expected to decrease by 5.93 per cent. The programme will also contribute to estimated emission savings of 54,435 t CO₂ eq per year through improved energy generation and access.

8. Also, the programme contributes to the Food systems (Target 4), Ecosystems (Target 5), Clean Energy (Target 7), Local private sector ventures (Target 10) and National and regional financial institutions (Target 11) objectives of the GCF Updated Strategic Plan (USP-2).

9. The DaIMA Programme strengthens climate resilience in East Africa’s dairy sector by enabling 2.5 million smallholder farmers to adopt adaptive practices. It enhances water access, pasture management, animal health, and disease control, leading to increased milk productivity and improved food and nutrition security, while reducing vulnerability to droughts and climate hazards (Target 4). The programme will also restore and sustainably manage over 178,000 hectares of degraded rangelands (Target 5) and strengthen dairy value chains by upgrading milk collection and processing facilities powered by renewable energy (Target 7). Under Component 3.1, the Green Dairy Financing Facility (GDFF) will provide two loan products to support the adoption of climate-smart technologies and sustainable dairy practices. Through this facility, DaIMA mobilizes concessional loans and technical assistance to promote low-emission investments and institutional capacity building in the dairy sector (Targets 10 and 11).

10. The programme will have three components as set out below.

### 2.2 Component-by-component analysis

#### Component 1: Strengthened national capacities and enhanced enabling environment for low emissions climate resilient dairy sectors (total cost: USD 58.2 million; GCF cost: USD 17.28 million)

11. Component 1 will create a supportive environment for transforming East Africa’s dairy sector, driving higher productivity, enhancing climate resilience and reducing emission intensity. It will focus on four key outputs: establishing a supportive policy and regulatory framework, including enhanced consumer awareness and nutritional balance (output 1.1); enhancing public service delivery in the dairy sector, particularly through specialized livestock extension and veterinary services (output 1.2); strengthening the capacity to monitor GHG emissions and climate vulnerabilities (output 1.3); and promoting regional cooperation and improving knowledge management (output 1.4).

12. **Output 1.1** (Climate-responsive policy and regulatory environment established) aims to establish a climate-responsive policy and regulatory environment to support the sustainable transformation of the dairy sector in East Africa. Activity 1.1.1 focuses on reviewing and formulating dairy sector policies, strategies and regulations to enhance climate change adaptation and mitigation. This includes aligning national policies with nationally determined contributions (NDCs) and national adaptation plans, improving trade and investment regulations and fostering private sector engagement. Sub-activities 1.1.1.1 and 1.1.1.2 will support national-level policy revisions and regional policy dialogue to harmonize dairy sector regulations and enhance technology transfer. Sub-activities 1.1.1.3 and 1.1.1.4 will strengthen dairy value chain governance and local stakeholder participation. Activity 1.1.2 will raise public awareness on the nutritional benefits of milk and food safety, promoting increased consumption of high-quality dairy products through educational campaigns and school milk programmes. Sub-activity 1.1.2.1 will focus on awareness initiatives for climate-vulnerable populations, while sub-activity 1.1.2.2 will enhance dairy inspection systems to ensure quality standards and reduce milk waste. Activity 1.1.3 will work towards improving land tenure security for sustainable dairy farming, identifying tenure risks and supporting land-use planning to enhance dairy productivity. Sub-activities 1.1.3.1 and 1.1.3.2 will provide training, public awareness campaigns and capacity-building efforts to secure land rights, particularly for women and youth. These activities collectively aim to create a more climate-resilient, inclusive and sustainable dairy sector in East Africa.

13. **Output 1.2** (Enhancing public service delivery in the dairy sector, particularly through specialized livestock extension and veterinary services) aims to enhance service delivery in the dairy sector through improved veterinary and extension services. Activity 1.2.1 focuses on strengthening veterinary services to improve animal health, boost milk yields and reduce losses caused by climate-induced diseases. This will involve expanding veterinary outreach, investing in diagnostic laboratory capacities and equipping veterinarians, paraveterinarians and community animal health workers with better tools and training. Sub-activity 1.2.1.1 will support the deployment of veterinarians and paraveterinary professionals, particularly young and female practitioners, while sub-activity 1.2.1.2 will enhance the diagnostic capacity of veterinary laboratories and improve feed quality testing. Activity 1.2.2 focuses on strengthening extension services to support a low-carbon and climate-resilient dairy sector. This will involve innovative advisory approaches such as digital tools, Farmer Field Schools and participatory extension models to enhance farmers' knowledge of climate-smart dairy practices. ### PART 1: GESI ANALYSIS

Agriculture plays a significant role in the economy, providing employment and income to millions of people. As a result, the impact of climate change on agriculture may have long-reaching consequences that extend well beyond individual farmers and their families. According to the United Nations, this output is mostly generated by family farms, where women supply around 60 to 80 percent of the labor input. East Africa has demonstrated the positive effects of dairy production on income, food security and dietary diversity. Most of the EAC countries have ratified international frameworks and progressive national legal and policy frameworks promoting gender equality and women empowerment. However, women in agriculture face gender-specific barriers, including limited access to land, financing, and other resources. Policy implementation remains weak in country and regional disparities persist. Each country, therefore, presents a unique context with varying statistics and progress towards closing the gender gaps. Nevertheless, present data reveals some progress towards achieving the stated policy commitments for the four DaIMA project focus countries and the need to address barriers in the dairy sector as outlined below.

#### Rwanda

In Rwanda, maternal and child health has improved significantly over the last two decades and exceeded Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) ambitions. Maternal mortality reduced by 80% between 2000 and 2014 while infant and child-mortality decreased by over 70% in the same period. Free universal basic education was initiated and scaled up to 12 years. This resulted in a net enrolment of nearly 100% in primary school for both boys and girls. Gender parity was achieved with more girls than boys in primary school.

Women outnumber men (92% compared to 77%, as per 2012 population census) in the rural population working in agriculture. About 25% of the (rural) households in Rwanda are headed by women and working age youth (ages 15-34) constitute 77% of the rural population. Women account for only 24% of the agro-dealers in Rwanda. Women and men farmers in dual households are characterized by unequal power relations, and women have limited decision-making powers. Within the dairy value chain, women have distinct and significant roles, although those roles vary by production system. In extensive systems, men and boys predominately bring the animals to graze and find water, while women care for the calves and process the milk. In zero grazing systems, which are required for participation in the GIRINKA program, women are responsible for feeding the animals, cleaning the stalls and milking utensils, while men are more involved in the milking of cows as well as the transport and sale of milk. These social and gender dynamics related to both women’s roles in the production as well as consumption of livestock, if not fully considered, limit the effectiveness of such programs, or worse, increase gender inequalities within the household. Such cases may occur, for instance, when men take over decision-making and control of income from a livestock product, such as dairy, that has become more lucrative following the intervention. Additionally, women in agriculture are more vulnerable to climate change and land degradation because they generally do not have other livelihood options to support their families.

#### Uganda

The Government of Uganda has identified gender equality and women's empowerment as critical to achieving accelerated socio-economic transformation. The country has ratified key gender equality instruments and commitments and has put in place a legal framework to promote Gender Equality and Women's Empowerment (GEWE). Although the Government of Uganda has established the Gender Equality Commission, which produces an annual report on the state of gender equality in the country, and signs Gender Equality Compacts with Ministries, Departments and Agencies to track the country's progress in improving the livelihoods and well-being of the most vulnerable, gaps remain in the economic, political and social inclusion of women. Men generally own cattle while women have ownership over the milk with women tending to engage more in dairy processing. Women are hindered in dairy development by a lack of ownership, access to resources and assets, which also results in lack of collateral for financial instruments. Generally, at household or farm level women’s decision-making power can be assumed low as also seen in farmers groups, in which they seldom hold leadership positions. The proportion of women in the cabinet rose from 9.4 percent in 1996 to 32.8% in 2003 but decreased to 19.4 percent in 2006. However, most women in Uganda are engaged in livestock yet have limited access to agricultural assets, including large-livestock, land, labor and credit. In most communities, land and other productive assets belong to men, and women's access is mediated through men.

#### Tanzania

Women constitute 52 percent of the population in Tanzania. 75 percent of these women live in rural areas. Of these, 90.4 percent work in the agriculture sector, 0.1 percent in the mining sector and 1.2 percent in small and medium enterprises (SMES). 44.1 percent of women in the urban centres work in SMES and mainly sell clothes, food and non-alcoholic and alcoholic beverages. Among the challenges experienced by women doing business include lack of capital, materials, markets, expertise / skills, knowledge and physical spaces to conduct business. The constitution recommends 30 percent among all appointed and elected officers. Women and youth tend to have limited access to and control over the resources and information needed for dairy production, value addition, and marketing. Men generally own cattle while women have ownership over the milk. Women are hindered in dairy development by a lack of ownership and access to resources and assets, which also results in lack of collateral for credit. Generally, at household or farm level women’s decision-making power is low as also seen in farmers groups, in which they seldom hold leadership and decision-making positions.

Mostly, gender relations vary with diverse cultural contexts, and this diversity should inform analysis and interventions. In traditional dairy production systems, gender roles vary with geographies. For example, in the Southern Highlands of Tanzania, milking is a man’s activity, but not a taboo for women, whereas in Tanga both women and men can milk. In both these areas, men own the cattle and women the milk, but the man can sell the milking cow and therefore the woman loses the milk. Youth too often have limited rights over household assets, control and ownership of commodities. As value chains commercialize and markets become sophisticated, moving further away from the farm gate, women tend to lose control of the commodities in question. For as long as the production of milk is low and the markets rudimentary, milk shall remain a woman’s commodity. Once the productivity increases to profitable magnitudes and the value chain becomes commercialized the ownership of milk is likely to change to men. To address these, lessons learnt from the Heifer Project International (HPI) Tanzania, which DaIMA foresees as implementing partner, uses participatory sensitization meetings with women and men at household and farmer group level to develop agreed gender strategies and action plans for mainstreaming and transformation. Couple training in the Gender Action Learning System (GALS) where women have multiple livelihood options, such as dairy production, crop residue preservation for fodder production and fodder harvesting “cut and carry” from communal lands, raise poultry, collect and trade with non-timber forest products such as medicinal herbs and gums (for food, medicine and other uses). With support for the various livelihood options, the women can still survive the worst-case scenario of losing milk business to men. The DaIMA and the IFAD Climate Smart Smallholder Dairy Transformation Project (C-SDTP) interventions have much complementarity including areas of geographical coverage. DaIMA will, support additional geographical areas such as the peri-urban dairy communities in the coastal region as well as some pastoral communities not included in the C-SDTP.

#### Kenya

Women have a right to own land according to law, however socio-cultural practices favour male inheritance. Only about 7 percent women own land alone. Without secure rights to land, women’s participation in and benefit from land uses such as dairy is inhibited. Yet women provide up to 75% of agricultural labour in addition to heavy workloads related to unpaid childcare and other domestic chores.
