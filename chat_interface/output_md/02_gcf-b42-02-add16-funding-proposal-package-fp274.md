# GREEN CLIMATE FUND FUNDING PROPOSAL V.3.0

## 1. Introduction

1. Globally, one billion children, nearly half the world’s children, live in countries at extreme vulnerability to the impacts of climate change. All three countries targeted by BRACE through Component 1 - Cambodia, South Sudan, and Tonga - are exposed to increasing temperatures, changing rainfall patterns, and extreme events (e.g., droughts, floods, and high/extreme temperature events) which result in a range of education-related impacts. The main impacts the project will address include climate-related increases in: children dropping out of school, children and teachers absent from school, learning loss and impacts on learning outcomes, and gender-related impacts (e.g., increases in gender-based violence), and physical impacts that climate hazards have on school facilities (e.g. water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) impacts, etc).

2. Component 1 will directly address and respond to the impacts of climate change on the education sector and address future climate risks in Cambodia, South Sudan, and Tonga. The project combines national-, sub-national-, and school-level interventions to create sufficient conditions for scalability, and sufficient integration to catalyse the transition to a climate-resilient education system. In Output 1.1 – Enabling systems and policies are in place and supported, climate and education policies, plans and guidelines will be prepared with Ministries of Education and Environment (Activity 1.1.1), and coordination on climate change within Ministries of Education (MoEs), and other relevant line ministries will be strengthened (Activity 1.1.2). Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) plans will be facilitated, tested and updated (Activity 1.1.3), and children will be supported to participate in climate change and education policy initiatives (Activity 1.1.4). In Output 1.2, school facilities are safer and greener, national standards for climate-resilient school facilities will be prepared (Activity 1.2.1), and school facilities, including WASH, retrofitted to strengthen climate resilience (Activity 1.2.2). To ensure sustainability, school communities and education officials will be supported to operate and maintain the retrofitted school facilities (Activity 1.2.3). In Output 1.3, school safety and educational continuity management systems are operating effectively, master trainers from the Ministry of Education will be supported (Activity 1.3.1) to train school management, teachers and children in how to prepare climate-related school safety plans (Activity 1.3.2), with schools supported to implement their plans and update their plans annually throughout the duration of the project (Activity 1.3.3). Climate resilience tools, equipment and kits will also be distributed to schools (Activity 1.3.4), and Early Warning Systems (EWS) and Climate Information Systems (CIS) access and use in schools will be strengthened (Activity 1.3.5). In Output 1.4, climate change resilience teaching and learning embedded in national systems will be supported through the development of teaching and learning materials on climate change (Activity 1.4.1), building the capacity of teachers and education managers to implement climate change lessons inside the classroom (Activity 1.4.2), support to child clubs to lead climate change initiatives in schools and communities (Activity 1.4.3), and uploading climate change teaching and learning materials to e-learning and other platforms (Activity 1.4.4). Learning documents and events with Ministries of Education to share experience of integrating climate change into the curriculum will also be held (Activity 1.4.5). The project will directly benefit 607,098 people in Component 1, including 392,185 people (188,373 female) in Cambodia, 201,242 (104,929 female) in South Sudan, and 13,671 (6,631 female in Tonga) and a total of 850 schools across all three countries.

3. In Component 2, the project will support Ministries of Education in the three countries – Cambodia, South Sudan and Tonga - supporting them to further access climate finance and to lead climate-resilient sustainable development in the education sector. This will include the development of education sector risk analyses on the impact of climate change on the education sector and children’s learning (Activity 2.1.1), MoEs to engage in National Adaptation Plan (NAP) processes, including those that may feed into global decisions such as those of the UNFCCC. MoEs will also be supported to develop and implement climate finance mobilization road maps, with Direct Access Entities (DAEs) supported to prepare concept notes for the GCF focused on the education sector. Direct beneficiaries will be 210 Ministry of Education and other education and climate change officials from the three countries, with the impact potential to reach children and education personnel in each country.

4. Component 3, will support the education community to better coordinate, collaborate, and share lessons learned and experiences with a focus on climate finance. The project will ensure that education and climate stakeholders are connecting, coordinating and cross-learning on climate finance for the benefit of the BRACE countries and future scaling up through the GPE parallel project finance and broader education systems strengthening work. This will be done by establishing a BRACE Climate and Education Financing Coordination Platform, including a steering committee and core team, to drive forward the interventions (Activity 3.1.1). In addition, Ministries of Education and Environment from Cambodia, South Sudan and Tonga will participate in face-to-face meetings to support cross-learning and build political momentum for climate and education (Activity 3.1.2). Global climate finance opportunities in the education sector will be mapped and tracked (Activity 3.1.3), and adjustments to international climate finance reporting to reflect the dual-benefit of investments in climate and education will be undertaken (Activity 3.1.4). The parallel project finance-supported knowledge management platform in a climate change space will also be leveraged to support the three countries access and share learning (Activity 3.2.1) and guidance developed on climate change and education for the GCF, as well as the global education community on climate finance (Activity 3.2.2) will be developed. Webinars, thematic sessions and learning events will also be held to support the BRACE countries (Activity 3.2.3), and strategic communications materials on climate and education prepared and disseminated (Activity 3.2.4). Component 3 of BRACE will directly reach 1,000 beneficiaries including education and climate change actors in the three target countries. The project’s goal statement is IF the education stakeholders in climate-vulnerable countries are better informed of climate risks and adaptation options for the education sector, as well as have access to global knowledge, policy exchange and financing, and IF the target schools are teaching children about climate change and become safer and greener, THEN the school systems of the target countries and beyond, including children and communities, become more resilient, BECAUSE, policies of the education sector becomes more responsive to climate change, there will be increased climate finance for the sector, Ministry of Education staff, students and communities will become more knowledgeable and engaged in climate adaptation actions and advocacy.

## B. PROJECT/PROGRAMME INFORMATION

### B.1. Climate context

5. South Sudan is expected to face significant climate impacts under the SSP2-4.5 scenario from 2020 to 2050, with critical implications for school-age children. These impacts include increased rainfall variability, heightened risks of flooding, intensified heat extremes, and changes in drought severity, all of which will profoundly affect water availability, agriculture, and health.

6. Rainfall is projected to increase slightly, with annual totals averaging around 1,236 mm and modest upward trends. Extreme rainfall events, such as one-day and five-day maximum rainfall, are expected to intensify significantly. Regions like Rumbek North and Akobo in the central areas face heightened flood risks, with severity anomalies reaching up to 100 mm during peak events. Southeastern areas, such as Kapoeta East, are likely to experience moderate rainfall increases. The variability in rainfall patterns and extremes will exacerbate flood risks, disrupting infrastructure and access to schools, especially during the wet season.

7. South Sudan will experience a significant warming trend, with mean temperatures rising by 0.33°C per decade. By mid-century, maximum temperatures will exceed 42°C in some areas, with extreme heat days above 35°C projected to increase by over 9 days per decade. These changes will disproportionately affect northern regions like Rubkona and Twic, where temperature anomalies are expected to exceed +1.25°C. For school-age children, extreme heat will heighten risks of heat-related illnesses, dehydration, and reduced ability to concentrate, particularly in poorly ventilated or outdoor learning environments.

8. Despite projected increases in rainfall, drought intensity is expected to decrease in many areas, reflected by improvements in the SPEI drought index. However, rainfall variability and changes in wet and dry spell duration present challenges. Central and northern regions will see slight reductions in consecutive dry days, but the southeastern regions will face more significant decreases in dry spell durations. These changes may improve water availability but could disrupt traditional planting cycles, impacting food security and nutrition—a critical concern for children’s development and learning.

9. Cambodia's climate projections under the SSP2-4.5 scenario from 2020 to 2050 indicate significant changes in rainfall patterns, temperature increases, and heightened risks of extreme weather events. These changes will profoundly affect water management, agriculture, and public health, with notable impacts on school-age children.

10. Annual rainfall is projected to increase slightly, averaging 1,791.78 mm with a 1% per decade upward trend, but regional and seasonal differences will result in uneven impacts. Central regions are expected to receive more rainfall, while parts of the northeast, southeast, and northwest may face declines. Seasonal rainfall patterns will shift, with wetter monsoons from July to October and reduced rainfall in critical planting months like April and May. Extreme rainfall events, such as five-day and one-month maximums, are projected to intensify, increasing flood risks, particularly in southern and northeastern areas.

11. Cambodia will experience a significant warming trend, with minimum, mean, and maximum temperatures increasing by 0.27–0.28°C per decade. By mid-century, average maximum temperatures will reach 32.62°C, with inland areas seeing the highest anomalies. The number of hot days exceeding 35°C will rise dramatically, from 67 days annually to over 90 days, particularly in central and northwestern regions. Days above 40°C, though rare, will also increase.

12. This sustained heat poses significant risks to school-age children, including heat-related illnesses, dehydration, and reduced learning performance due to discomfort in poorly ventilated classrooms. Prolonged exposure to extreme temperatures can also disrupt sleep, affecting children’s cognitive function and academic achievement.

13. While the drought index (SPEI) shows an overall improvement, indicating fewer and less severe droughts, regional disparities persist. The northwest may face increased drought severity, while other areas experience moderate improvements. Rising aridity and evaporation rates in the northwest could exacerbate water scarcity, impacting agriculture and household water supply.

14. Tonga's climate projections under the SSP2-4.5 scenario reveal nuanced changes in rainfall, extreme weather events, and temperature patterns that will significantly affect its people and ecosystems. These changes pose distinct challenges for school-age children, who are particularly vulnerable to disruptions in education, health, and well-being. Tonga is expected to experience a slight decrease in annual rainfall, averaging 1,732.93 mm, with more pronounced variability and intensified extreme rainfall events. The northern islands are likely to face higher extremes, with one-day and five-day maximum rainfall totals increasing by nearly 1 mm and 1.36 mm per decade, respectively. Rainfall seasonality will intensify, causing greater year-to-year variability and potentially disrupting water availability and agricultural cycles. For children, this could mean disruptions to schooling due to infrastructure damage or unsafe travel conditions during extreme rainfall events, especially in remote areas.

15. While drought conditions are projected to improve slightly, increasing aridity and evaporation in some regions could strain water resources, essential for both schools and households. Such conditions may lead to water shortages, which can directly impact hygiene and nutrition—key determinants of children’s health and cognitive development.

16. Tonga will face significant warming trends, with minimum, mean, and maximum temperatures rising by approximately 0.17°C per decade. By 2050, the number of days with temperatures above 25°C is expected to rise substantially, with such conditions becoming the norm for over 270 days annually. Northern regions will experience longer warm spells, with durations extending by 50 days per year, while maximum daytime temperatures are projected to exceed 29°C in western areas.

17. The frequency of category 3 cyclones is projected to decrease slightly, but tropical cyclones remain a significant threat to Tonga, given their potential for widespread destruction. Schools are particularly vulnerable to damage from cyclones, which can displace children, interrupt education, and exacerbate mental health challenges due to the trauma of extreme weather events.

## Impacts on School-Age Children, School Infrastructure, and Learning

18. The projected climate changes for South Sudan, Cambodia, and Tonga under the SSP2-4.5 scenario highlight significant challenges for school-age children, school infrastructure, and learning environments, with shared themes of extreme weather events, rising temperatures, and rainfall variability posing threats to education.

19. In South Sudan, increasing rainfall extremes and heightened flood risks will likely disrupt school attendance and damage educational infrastructure. Regions prone to flooding, such as central and northern areas, may experience repeated closures or unsafe conditions, limiting consistent access to education. Rising temperatures, with days exceeding 35°C and extended heat waves, will exacerbate health issues like dehydration and heat stress for children, impacting their ability to focus in classrooms that often lack adequate cooling. Prolonged heat and food insecurity from disrupted agriculture will further affect children’s cognitive performance and overall well-being.

20. Cambodia faces similar challenges, with intensified monsoons and increased frequency of extreme rainfall events leading to higher flood risks, especially in southern and northeastern regions. Flooding could damage schools, disrupt access, and pose sanitation challenges, increasing the risk of waterborne diseases. Rising temperatures, with an average increase of 0.28°C per decade and a dramatic rise in days exceeding 35°C, will create challenging learning environments. Heat stress and lack of proper cooling systems will affect students’ focus and attendance. Droughts and shifting rainfall patterns during critical agricultural periods could strain food security, affecting children’s nutrition and learning capabilities, especially in rural areas.

21. In Tonga, extreme rainfall events and tropical cyclones will continue to be significant threats to school infrastructure, with damage to buildings and disruptions to access during storms. While rainfall variability may lead to localized droughts, the primary concern is the rising temperatures, with extended warm spells increasing risks of heat-related illnesses and discomfort in learning environments. Increased reliance on cooling measures will strain resources, particularly in remote areas with limited access to electricity. The heightened frequency of extreme weather events will also add stress to families, potentially leading to higher absenteeism and school dropouts.

22. Across all three countries, climate-induced disruptions to school infrastructure, health, and family livelihoods will affect learning continuity and quality. Shared vulnerabilities include flooding and extreme heat, which damage facilities, disrupt access, and create unhealthy or uncomfortable conditions for children. For rural areas, where children often walk long distances to school, these impacts are magnified. Food and water insecurity resulting from climate disruptions add burden, impairing children’s physical and cognitive development. ## B.3. Project/Programme Description

BRACE focuses on enhancing the climate resilience of the education sector and addressing risks and impacts from droughts, heat stress, and floods. In Component 1, BRACE will be implemented in Cambodia, South Sudan, and Tonga combining climate-resilient interventions at national, sub-national, and school levels. Component 2 will work directly with Ministries of Education in the three target countries to strengthen their access to climate finance through the preparation of easy-to-use guidance and engagement in National Adaptation Planning processes. Component 3 will strengthen coordination on climate change and education with a focus on financing to support sustainability of the interventions in Cambodia, South Sudan, and Tonga. BRACE will directly benefit 607,098 people in Component 1, including 392,185 people (188,373 female) in Cambodia, 201,242 (104,929 female) in South Sudan, and 13,671 (6,631 female) in Tonga. The nature of the work in Component 2 and Component 3 means the direct reach will be lower as the project will support and work with a select group of ministry officials, their partners and international organizations, but the indirect reach will cover all school-aged children in the three target countries of BRACE. Therefore, it is estimated that Component 2 will directly benefit 210 beneficiaries and Component 3 will benefit 1,000 beneficiaries. The project will achieve climate-resilient education systems and support children’s learning through a multi-pronged approach as set out in the project ToC outlined in section B.2. More information on the outcomes, outputs, and activities can be found in the feasibility study (Annex 2). Furthermore, the BRACE project will be complemented by parallel project finance from the Global Partnership for Education (GPE) and with UNESCO will help to address border drivers of the challenges that children face in being educated in climate-stressed environments, such as absenteeism and dropping-out, as the GPE and UNESCO support governments broader system strengthening initiatives of the education systems which includes tackling the broader causes of absenteeism and dropping-out.

### Component 1

**OUTCOME 1:** The education sector at national and sub-national levels in targeted countries is more resilient to the impacts of climate change.

The activities under this outcome will enable policies to be in place to support climate-resilient education to ensure that school facilities are safer and more climate resilient, that school safety systems are in place and educational continuity is protected, and that climate-resilient teaching and learning is supported in schools. These activities will address governance, social, informational, financial, technological, institutional, and gender and social inclusion barriers.

#### Output 1.1 Enabling Systems and Policies are in Place and Supported

The activities under this output will support Ministries of Education to incorporate climate change within their education sector plans and policies and to strengthen internal coordination within the Ministry of Education on climate change and cross-sectoral coordination with other line ministries. Girls and boys, including those with disabilities, will participate in policy dialogues on climate change and education so that their priorities are heard in policy discussions.

- **Activity 1.1.1** Facilitate preparation and endorsement of climate and education policies, plans, and guidelines.
- **Activity 1.1.2** Strengthen coordination on climate change within Ministries of Education and other relevant line ministries at national and sub-national level.
- **Activity 1.1.3** Facilitate integration of Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) into existing and/or new plans with education authorities.
- **Activity 1.1.4** Support children to participate in climate change and education policy initiatives.

#### Output 1.2 School Facilities are Safer and Greener

The activities under this output will focus on ensuring that school facilities are safer and climate-resilient for children, including boys and girls and those with disabilities, as well as educational personnel (teachers, school staff), and School Management Committees (SMCs), as well as caregivers.

In order to foster a society that is informed and capable of responding to climate change challenges, the climate change vulnerabilities in the education sector must be addressed. The education sector must integrate climate resilience into planning and operations, which includes constructing climate-resilient infrastructure, developing disaster risk reduction strategies, incorporating climate change into the curriculum, build pedagogical capacity and content knowledge among teachers and educators, and supporting communities to adapt to climate change impacts.

### Cambodia

Cambodia is one of the poorest countries among the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) characterised by limited educational opportunities, high income inequality and poor job prospects. The country’s vulnerability to climate change is linked to its characteristics as a post‐civil war, least developed, predominantly agrarian country, with 76% of the population living in rural areas. Along with the Philippines, Cambodia is regarded by the United Nations and the International Development Research Center as Southeast Asia's most vulnerable nation to the consequences of climate change. The ND-GAIN ranks Cambodia 149th most vulnerable country out of 187, with equally low rankings for vulnerability (130) and climate readiness (158). Cambodia faces severe climate change vulnerabilities, ranking as the second most vulnerable country globally to climate hazards according to the 2024 INFORM risk index.

Additionally, Cambodia ranks 46th out of 163 countries in UNICEF’s Children’s Climate Risk Index based on climate and environmental shocks, which is in the top third of countries with a high risk to climate change. The UNICEF media release found that Cambodian children are highly exposed to water scarcity, riverine flooding and vector-borne disease. Furthermore, the Global Climate Risk Index ranks Cambodia as 14th most climate risk prone country globally. The country is particularly vulnerable to the hazards of floods, droughts, heatwaves, cyclones, and seawater intrusion due to its geographic location. During these climate events, school infrastructure is damaged, children are increasingly absent losing learning and development opportunities and school communities suffer climate related health illnesses.

### South Sudan

As an LDC, South Sudan is recognised as being particularly vulnerable to economic shocks while having urgent and immediate adaptation needs. South Sudan is ranked by the ND-GAIN as the 8th most vulnerable country in the world to climate shocks, and the 175th most ready country to adapt to the impacts of climate change. South Sudan is also close to the bottom of all countries in terms of Human Development, ranking 185th out of 189 countries on the Human Development Index and an estimated 80% of the population live beneath the absolute poverty line. Conflict is also still extremely prevalent in South Sudan, and is a core driver of humanitarian needs.

This project focuses on adaptation needs for recipients in six counties extreme flooding, drought, temperature related wildfires and heatwaves which render learners unable to go to school. South Sudan’s topographic context – extreme lowlands and swamps – mean that schools and communities are extremely vulnerable to flooding, which is growing increasingly worse due to erratic and intense rainfall, leading hundreds of schools across the country to close and become damaged beyond repair.

### Tonga

Tonga’s school children, surrounding communities and the educational system is already - and will continue to - face sea level rise, droughts, floodings, and extreme weather including tropical cyclones and heatwaves. Climate change generates intense challenges to Tonga’s education system. Tonga does not have any climate change specific courses in schools and during stakeholder consultations, government stakeholders emphasized the need to guide and prepare students for climate professions.

A World Bank report, confirmed by consultations with government stakeholders, indicated that schools especially on the outer islands are highly vulnerable to climate risks and urgently need infrastructural support. Past climate events in 2018 and 2020, were highly disruptive of children’s education and associated with high financial costs. Although there are previous projects focused on school safety and continuity related to disasters, the climate lens has been missing.

Unfortunately, domestic and international funding sources are insufficient to meet the adaptation needs of the education sector and the dispersed school system. Most international donor-supported programmes focus on immediate development priorities with only a minor focus on climate change, and often with a mitigation focus.

Components 2 and 3 focus on mobilising climate finance and building knowledge and networks which are greatly lacking in the target countries. It’s clear that as climate impacts and risks increase, the viability of any investments in the education sector will be compromised. Stakeholders need to be engaged and coordinated in order to collaborate, access finance, prioritise climate actions and share knowledge and lessons with a view to strengthen the country’s climate resilience in the education sector.

# Introduction

1. The Building the Climate Resilience of Children and Communities through the Education Sector (BRACE) project aims to improve the adaptive capacities and climate resilience of children, their communities and schools, recognising that children and their communities are impacted by climate change differently depending on many intersecting factors including, but not limited to, their age, gender, disability, ethnicity, race, economic status, refugee status and gender identity.

2. The rationale for the BRACE project is to tackle the impacts of climate change on the education sector in three very different countries with diverse climate hazards and social contexts: Cambodia, South Sudan, and Tonga. By implementing an approach in three very different countries, BRACE will be able to demonstrate an adaptable model that can be replicated in other countries. BRACE will also support Ministries of Education to access climate finance, and support the global education community to better coordinate, collaborate and share lessons learned and experience with a focus on climate finance. If this intervention does not happen, the Business as Usual will result in increased climate vulnerability, school closures, children dropping out of school, gender inequality, and learning loss.

3. BRACE will be achieved via implementation of activities under three components, each with an associated outcome:
   - **Component 1**: Building Climate-Resilient School Infrastructure and Systems with outputs and activities leading to Outcome 1 – The education sector at national and sub-national levels in targeted countries is more resilient to the impacts of climate change.
   - **Component 2**: Enhancing Access to Climate Finance for Education, with outputs and activities leading to Outcome 2 – Education ministries of the target countries have improved access to climate finance to increase the resilience of the education sector to climate change.
   - **Component 3**: Coordination and Knowledge Sharing on Climate and Education, with outputs and activities leading to Outcome 3 – Education and climate stakeholders are connecting, coordinating, collaborating and cross-learning for enhanced climate action in the education sector.

4. For the BRACE Theory of Change, please see Appendix A.

5. This analysis considers the ways in which gender and social norms intersect with adaptive capacities and climate change across Cambodia, South Sudan and Tonga. The assessment sets out key data and analysis across five domains and includes recommendations for the project across all project components. The core approach is to embed gender equality and social inclusion (GESI) across project activities and operations. The Gender Equality and Social Inclusion Action Plan highlights GESI actions included in the project wide logical framework for the three countries as well as Components 2 and 3.

6. The five domains of analysis are:
   - **Domain 1**: Laws, Policies and Institutions
   - **Domain 2**: Social Norms, Roles, Responsibilities and Time Use

   - **Domain 4**: Access To and Control Over Resources and Services
   - **Domain 5**: Safety, Dignity and Well-Being

# Methodology

9. The gender assessment and action plan were prepared by the feasibility teams located in Cambodia, South Sudan and Tonga as well GESI specialists in the countries and regions. It was developed in line with the GCF Gender Policy, Gender Mainstreaming Guide, Save the Children International (SCI) Gender Equality and Inclusion Policies, and country-level SCI Gender Equality and Inclusion Action Plans. The teams used a combination of primary and secondary data collection methods as explained below.

10. The availability of data varied across countries given the different contexts. Where possible, data was collected across countries to show similarities and comparisons to facilitate the development and implementation of the Gender Action Plan.

11. **Primary data**:
    - Primary data were collected in South Sudan and Tonga.
    - **South Sudan**: Data were collected through existing gender assessments conducted across ongoing SCI South Sudan projects. This involved direct engagement through key informant interviews with national, state and local Government of South Sudan Ministries of Education and Gender, parents, teachers, civil society organisations and the wider community including girls and women. For ongoing education programs such as the South Sudan Joint Response, Norwegian Agency for Development Cooperation (NORAD), and the Education Cannot Wait (ECW) Multi-Year Resilience Programme rapid gender and needs assessments were conducted with girls, women, boys and men between October 2023 and June 2024. These assessments were carried out through focus group discussions and observations in two project sites: Maban County, Upper Nile State, and Akobo County, Jonglei State. Standard questions were used, adapted for local languages and for people with disabilities. Please see Appendix B for a consultations overview for South Sudan.
    - **Tonga**: Primary data were gathered through direct engagement using key informant interviews with national government officials, civil society organisations, women-led and gender-diverse groups, youth-focused organisations, international development agencies, donors, and academics. ## Cambodia

Cambodia is steeped in patriarchal traditions and discriminatory gender norms are heavily rooted. These societal norms have dictated girls’ and women’s subordination to men and boys in both private and public spheres. A gender policy assessment by MoWA revealed that traditional stereotypes and norms were the main barriers hindering Cambodia’s women from gaining broader opportunities for economic empowerment, reproductive health, participation in family and social decision-making processes, acting against domestic violence, and enjoying an education and career development.

According to the UN in Cambodia “social norms and beliefs that restrict what women can do and be, and give higher value and more power to men, are still pervasive. Gender norms are also embedded in government policies, such as the National Childcare Policy which reinforces the idea of women as responsible for care of children and the household. Social expectations of women and men are evident in day-to-day interactions in workplaces and communities and are frequently cited as barriers to women taking leadership roles. Harmful gender norms, such as the idea that men are entitled to sex regardless of consent, directly contribute to GBV”.

While family is the foundation of social life for most Cambodian people, the roles of women and men are socially and culturally constructed in Cambodian society with men often seen as the head of the household and main income earner, while women are primary caregivers and homemakers. According to the 2019 Cambodia Labour Force Survey, 76.4% of households were headed by a man, while 23.6% were headed by a woman. Almost the same gender distribution in household heads emerged across both urban and rural areas.

In terms of climate change, these discriminatory gender stereotypes can limit girls and women from developing the necessary skills and capacities to adapt to the changing climate and reduce disaster risk. Women and girls living in poverty, indigenous women, women belonging to ethnic, racial, religious and sexual minority groups, and women with disabilities are less able to adapt to changes in climatic conditions due to their disadvantaged socio-economic status, the lower positions they are in, limited education, cultural norms, and restricted involvement in decision-making processes.

Further, due to cultural norms, women are often seen as passive victims rather able to take action. In reality, women are often responsible for protecting the household’s property and belongings from climate-induced loss and damage as well as taking care of the children while men are responsible for finding or continuing income generating activities. “Consequently, women tend to become psychologically stressed, worrying that their children will drown, become seriously sick or be bitten by poisonous insects.”

The perception of women as victims is also considered a reason why there are fewer women in official roles in the climate change sector. This is exacerbated by women’s persistent poverty, limited educational opportunities, lack of decision-making power, and inadequate access to reproductive and sexual health services linked to pregnancy and childbirth.

With climate change negatively affecting water availability and quality, the gender bias in women and girls’ household responsibilities is even more evident in terms of domestic water consumption, using dirty water for cooking and cleaning with risk particularly high during menstruation or pregnancy.

“Restrictive gender norms are reflected in the vastly unequal distribution of unpaid domestic and care work, with women doing, on average, 90% of that work” and “almost 30% of employed women reported that their partners did not provide any additional assistance”, according to the UN in Cambodia’s 2022 gender equality assessment report. The report continues to reveal that climate change and disasters add to women’s unpaid work in affected regions. In the absence of other support, unpaid work may be taken on by elder women in the family or girls with possible negative impacts on their education.

Balancing livelihoods and unpaid domestic and care work can keep women in more vulnerable work, limiting the opportunities to expand businesses, advance their careers or take on leadership roles. Further, this time poverty can affect women’s health, further education, leisure or community engagement. Despite unpaid care and domestic work being one of the biggest obstacles to gender equality, public policies and development efforts rarely target it.

Traditional customs and Christian values have, at times, been interpreted to justify a more paternalistic social hierarchy, creating tension with the historical power dynamics of the "Fahu" system.

Like Cambodia and South Sudan, traditional gender roles often assign men as the primary providers and decision-makers within the family, while women are typically responsible for domestic duties such as cooking, cleaning, and child-rearing.

Men may take on roles involving community leadership, including serving as chiefs or elders, while women often play important roles in community gatherings and social events, such as organising ceremonies or contributing to village decision-making processes. Women often play supporting roles in community leadership, providing input during decision-making processes, organising community events, and helping those in need. Both men and women participate in social and cultural activities, although the nature of these activities may differ. For example, men may spend time attending community meetings or engaging in traditional ceremonies, while women may gather for social events or participate in church-related activities.

Men often allocate time to income-generating activities such as working in the fields, fishing, or engaging in paid employment outside the home. Women continue to be primary carers of children and elderly within homes while also taking on roles outside of the homes for employment or community engagement such as with church groups. Notably key informants have highlighted a shift in attitudes of gendered behaviour with increasing sharing of childcare and household chores. This was attributed to increasing education and interaction with other countries/globalisation.

Both men, women and gender diverse individuals engage in efforts to promote gender equality and challenge traditional gender norms through education, awareness-raising campaigns, and advocacy initiatives.

Aligned with local customs, individuals within the diverse SOGIESC community often engage in informal adoption of young people from extended families or communities, assuming parental responsibilities. Alternatively, some establish ‘chosen family’ units comprised of community members cohabiting and pooling resources. It’s anticipated that leitis will undertake unpaid labour to garner and uphold social acceptance, including tasks like cooking and providing food to the underprivileged within their local communities.

Around 34% of people are moderately deprived, with a further 20% experiencing severe deprivation. Men were less likely to meet the threshold for Time Use deprivation. People aged 30-59 were more likely than those in other age groups to meet the threshold for any deprivation.

The Tongan economy is heavily reliant on remittances from Tongans working overseas as well as foreign aid. The United States is the main source of remittances, followed by New Zealand and Australia.

During and after extreme weather events and natural hazards, social norms in Tonga traditionally assign gendered roles. Women, responsible for a larger share of unpaid domestic labour, often take charge of preserving water, food, medicine, and valuables. This aligns with their role in tasks like cooking, cleaning, and water treatment. Additionally, they are more likely to relocate children or themselves to shelters or safer areas. Conversely, men, who are more engaged in agriculture, are expected to reinforce homes and animal shelters, cover crops, save seeds, and move animals to safer locations. These distinct roles highlight the intersection of gender and disaster preparedness in Tongan society. The education sector is deemed to be feminised, and key informants noted that there has been a notable decrease in men undertaking educating roles in Tonga.

### Cambodia

Women have been found to have less power to voice their opinions and influence decision-making processes in both their families and in wider society. ### South Sudan

Women and girls have limited representation and voice in community decision-making processes at home including related to income generation, education and climate resilience. At household level, women and men report a high level of male dominated decision-making. Men are seen as heads of households and social norms privilege male decision-making in traditional, formal and community settings.

Young people, especially girls, are not adequately involved in planning and implementing climate resilience activities in schools.

At the community level there are inconsistent and tokenistic levels of inclusion of women in consultations and in decision-making fora, with progress made in certain communities and less so in others. As such, women’s leadership and authority in the community is earned through acquired respect and age.

There are some reports of young people being included in community consultations, but there is little evidence of systematic inclusion of voices of young women and men across the board.

South Sudanese women have been advocating for inclusive peace and demanding meaningful participation at all levels of decision-making, especially in politics. ### Tonga

There are no women in the Tongan parliament, and a few factors have been identified that hinder women’s participation in Tongan politics. The last four elections in Tonga have never resulted in more than 8% female representation in parliament, the most recent one resulting in none.

Firstly, the high cost of campaigning is a global obstacle, but in Tonga, it’s compounded by the cultural expectation of reciprocity. Voters expect gifts like food, kava, and even infrastructure improvements from candidates, making it difficult for women with fewer resources to compete. Secondly, traditional gender roles persist. Out of a total of 38,500 votes, 34,198 were cast for the male candidates and only 4,352 were cast for the 12 female candidates, down from 14% of total votes in 2017 to 11% in 2021.

A large-scale study by the Balance of Power program found that 92% of the 1,000 Tongans surveyed believe men possess the necessary skills and experience for parliament. This perception of male legitimacy as political leaders significantly disadvantages women candidates. Given these challenges, the low number of women elected in recent elections is unsurprising.

Two major pieces of research on voter’s perception of women as leaders conducted in 2016-17 and 2020-21, showed the majority of eligible voters believed key decision-making and leadership roles are best left to men and that roles such as household work and nurturing children are a women’s responsibility.

Across the 178 district and town officers throughout Tonga only 8 are female (4.5%) while 170 are male (95.5%) demonstrating the gendered imbalance in local decision making.

The education sector in Tonga however is known to have more females than males and this is reflected in the various decision-making spaces.

Despite the gender imbalance in government positions in Tonga, across the three countries, Tonga has the highest rates of women’s participation in UNFCCC processes on average. However, when analysing the data it’s important to note that at the Bonn conferences, Tonga only sent one delegate, which has often been a woman resulting in 100% of the delegation being women. ## Appendix B: Consultations Overview South Sudan

### Context and Objective

The consultations for the BRACE project were conducted with the primary aim of gathering comprehensive insights and feedback to inform the project’s approach to enhancing climate resilience through the education sector in South Sudan. This initiative is part of a broader strategy to integrate climate resilience into educational frameworks, ensuring that children and communities are better prepared to face climate-related challenges.

### Timeline

The consultations spanned six months, from January to June 2024. This period allowed for an extensive engagement process, ensuring that diverse perspectives and experiences were captured from various stakeholders across different regions.

### Geographic Scope

The consultations were conducted in four key counties:
1. Bor (Jonglei State)
2. Akobo (Jonglei State)
3. Maban (Upper Nile State)
4. Magwi (Eastern Equatoria State)

These locations were chosen to represent a range of climatic and socio-economic conditions, providing a holistic view of the challenges and opportunities for building climate resilience in the education sector. Project locations selected for consultations did not end up being final target locations for BRACE in South Sudan, after further consultations with government stakeholders requesting the specific inclusion of certain areas. The location for consultations were selected based on a range of climatic conditions, and areas that Save the Children were currently operational in or operating through partners, for ease of access.

### Levels of Engagement

1. **National Level:**
   - Consultations involved high-level stakeholders, including government ministries, national NGOs, and international development partners.
   - The focus was on aligning the BRACE project with national policies and strategies on climate resilience and education.

2. **State Level:**
   - Engaged state education departments and local NGOs operating at the state level.
   - Discussions centered on state-specific challenges and resource allocations for climate-resilient educational initiatives.

3. **County Level:**
   - County education officials, local leaders, and community organizations were consulted.
   - These meetings aimed to identify county-specific needs and to tailor interventions to local contexts.

4. **Community Level:**
   - Direct engagement with teachers, parents, students, and other community members through FGDs and community meetings.
   - These sessions provided grassroots insights into the daily challenges faced by communities and schools, particularly in relation to climate impacts.

### Methods of Engagement

1. **Focus Group Discussions (FGDs):**
   - FGDs were held in each of the four counties, involving various community groups.
   - These discussions were designed to be inclusive, encouraging participation from women, youth, and marginalized groups.
   - Topics included the impacts of climate change on education, existing coping mechanisms, and suggestions for enhancing climate resilience in schools.

2. **Consultative Meetings:**
   - Formal consultative meetings were held with stakeholders at national, state, and county levels.
   - These meetings provided a platform for detailed discussions on policy, strategic planning, and resource mobilization for the BRACE project.
   - Key themes included integration of climate resilience into curricula, teacher training needs, and infrastructural improvements for schools.

3. **Workshops and Dialogues:**
   - Workshops and dialogues facilitated more in-depth discussions on specific aspects of the BRACE project.
   - These sessions were instrumental in developing a shared understanding of the project goals and fostering collaboration among stakeholders.

### Outcomes of the Consultations

The consultations yielded valuable insights that will inform the BRACE project’s design and implementation. Key outcomes include:
- Identification of critical gaps in current educational practices related to climate resilience.
- Enhanced understanding of regional disparities and specific needs.
- Stronger alignment of project goals with national and local policies.
- Improved stakeholder buy-in and support for the project.
