# A GREEN CLIMATE FUND FUNDING PROPOSAL V.3.0

## Climate Context

The Caribbean region is currently facing the adaptation and mitigation challenges of climate change, evidenced by intensified storms and rising temperatures, and an energy sector heavily reliant on imported fossil fuels, contributing significantly to greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions.

### Climate

The Borrowing Member Countries (collectively “Barbados, Belize and Jamaica” or the “BMCs”) are facing significant climate change impacts, including intensified storms, floods, droughts, sea-level rise, and rising temperatures. These changes are affecting not only the availability and supply of energy resources but also the transmission, distribution, operations, and energy demand. The historical and projected impacts of climate change indicate a trend of rising temperatures, escalating the risk of heat stress and severe weather events, which are expected to continue and intensify. For instance, the International Energy Agency has noted that extreme heat boosts the demand for cooling, with sustained average daily temperatures of 30°C typically resulting in a 16% spike in weekly sales of electricity.

Moreover, the BMCs are committed to ambitious goals to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by increasing the share of renewables in their energy mix and improving energy efficiency across all sectors.

### Energy

The BMCs heavily rely on imported fossil fuels for electricity, which constitutes a significant portion of their annual greenhouse gas emissions. The energy sectors in these countries are critical to their GHG profiles, with other sectors such as road transport, industry, agriculture, and land-use change also being notable contributors. The Integrated Utility Services (IUS) Programme intends to drive investment in renewable energy and energy efficiency measures to reduce this dependency, which is increasingly important as the demand for electricity is expected to rise with economic growth. The integration of Distributed Energy Resources (DERs) is also a focus for enhancing the resilience and diversification of the electricity grid.

The introduction of an IUS model in BMCs is seen as a strategic step toward addressing various challenges in their energy sectors. These include reducing GHG emissions, decentralizing energy systems, enhancing resilience to climate change impacts, and decreasing dependence on imported fossil fuels. Additionally, the model supports adaptation to heat through providing cooling solutions to ensure comfortable indoor temperatures, using energy-efficient air conditioners and decentralized renewable energy supply to reduce peak electricity demand during times of high temperatures, thereby supporting the reliability of the grid. The Programme aims to democratize energy policy, increase access to clean energy, reform utility business models, and enhance capabilities for financing DERs. Furthermore, it seeks to foster regional learning and knowledge sharing while developing a sustainable market ecosystem for behind-the-meter solutions in the Caribbean.

## Programme Approach

The Governments of Barbados, Belize, and Jamaica aim to accelerate decarbonization efforts in their electricity sector and increase the resilience of the communities to climate change impacts. They aim to meet international climate change commitments by focusing on renewable energy (RE) and energy efficiency (EE) technologies. The proposed IUS Programme will be administered by electric utilities to support the deployment of these technologies. The Programme will involve education for customers, financing for installations, and repayment through monthly bills, overcoming the barrier of high upfront costs. This Programme constitutes the first of two phases for the overall IUS Programme in the region. While Phase I would provide support to the three countries to implement the Programme, Phase II will result in a broader implementation and scale-up to other member-countries of the Caribbean Development Bank (CDB).

This IUS model is a mechanism to directly implement demand-side RE and EE projects through utilities in an open market system.

The IUS Programme will include a “national project” in each country to be executed by the national electric utility and overseen by the Ministry and regulator responsible for oversight of the electricity sector. Each “national project” will include a series of sub-projects at the level of the utility’s customers, notably residential customers (i.e. households) and commercial customers (i.e. local businesses). Through each of these small-scale sub-projects, eligible RE, EE and/or other DER technologies will be installed at the premises of the interested utility customers. Utility customers will first receive education and awareness about the options available to them and then they will choose to implement the technologies through registered qualified service providers that work closely with the utility. The utility will provide the upfront financing for the installation of the sub-projects and support the service providers, while the customers will then repay the amount owed to the utility for the installation through their monthly bill. A major barrier for the uptake of these technologies has historically been the high upfront cost to install them, so this Programme is expected to alleviate that pressure.

## Programme Components

The IUS Programme is structured in a way that it not only addresses all the key risks and barriers assessed during the programme preparation phase but also supports the much-needed ecosystem development for a sustainable, scalable, and replicable growth of behind the meter energy solutions in the BMCs. The below figure outlines the key components of the IUS Programme.

![Figure 1: Structure of the IUS Programme](source: CDB/CPCS)

The Programme, through Component 1, establishes and promotes IUS as a core element of each Executing Entity’s business model. It underpins timely and effective delivery of country-level support and enables the BMCs to guide and support one another during and after the Programme. Component 2 aims to build on this foundation to scale up investments in distributed RE, EE and other DERs through widespread use of the IUS model by the Executing Entities in the BMCs.

Each programme component is broken down into sub-components, which themselves comprise several activities that support a robust programme design and delivery.

## Programme Benefits

The IUS Programme anticipates significant climate-related benefits:

- **Reduction in GHG Emissions:** The Programme is projected to make a significant reduction in the carbon footprint of the participating countries by enabling the avoidance of an estimated 601,550 tons of CO2 equivalent (tCO2e) over the lifetime of the Programme. This results in an estimated cost of USD 70 per tCO2e avoided, and for the GCF's contribution, the cost is approximately USD 43 per tCO2e avoided.

- **Increasing Resilience Against Heat Stress:** The Programme is projected to provide energy efficient air conditioners (AC) to about 9,444 residential and 1,211 commercial beneficiaries.

- **Decreased Burden on Utilities and Increased Renewable Energy Generation:** Energy efficiency interventions are expected to reduce energy consumption across various sectors, including residential, commercial, and public. The Programme anticipates considerable energy savings which translate into lower utility bills for consumers and decreased overall demand on energy infrastructure. A shift towards renewables is anticipated, with a targeted increase in the contribution of clean energy to the national grids. This includes the deployment of technologies like solar photovoltaic systems, solar water heating, and possibly wind and geothermal energy, depending on the country-specific strategies and natural resource availability.

- **Economic Development:** The transition to a greener energy sector is intended to stimulate local economies through job creation in the renewables sector, foster innovation, and provide opportunities for local businesses. Energy cost savings and the potential for new energy-related businesses are additional economic benefits.

- **Capacity Building and Knowledge Sharing:** A core outcome of the Programme is the enhancement of institutional capacities and regulatory frameworks in each participating country. This involves training programs, development of standards, and the establishment of best practices for EE and RE deployment.

- **Scaling Up of Investments:** By de-risking investments and establishing favourable conditions for private and international financing, the Programme aims to catalyse additional resources for the energy sector transformation. This will enable a scaling up of clean energy projects and attract further investments into the region.

- **Gender and Social Inclusion:** The Programme places an emphasis on ensuring that the benefits of the energy transition are equitably distributed, with specific measures to include women and vulnerable communities in the opportunities created by the shift to sustainable energy sources. The Programme aims to provide a more inclusive way to support sustainable energy investments, particularly for women who often face more economic barriers than men due to traditional divisions of labour and social norms. Through the Gender Action Plan (GAP), the Programme will address the following gender-specific needs: governance and participation (through targeted actions focused on policies, utilities, and other key actors), and access, education, and assets (through targeted actions focused on the marginalised beneficiaries).

## Barbados

### Historical
According to the World Bank CCKP, Barbados has experienced a significant warming trend, with mean annual temperatures rising by 0.45°C since 1960, at an average rate of 0.10°C per decade. From 1960 to 2022, surface temperatures increased by 0.81°C. This period also saw a marked change in temperature patterns, with hot days and nights increasing by 67 days per year from 1960 to 2003, while cold days and nights decreased by 21 days per year.

### Projected
Modelling conducted under the CMIP6 framework, featured in the World Bank CCKP, projects a significant rise in the mean annual temperatures of Barbados under a high emissions scenario (SSP3-7.0). Temperatures are anticipated to increase by 1.05°C (0.88°C-1.33°C) by 2030 and are projected to reach 29.45°C (28.07°C-30.68°C) by the end of the century, from a baseline of 25.4°C (24.95°C-25.73°C) in 1995. Days with temperatures above 30°C are projected to increase by 74-175% across scenarios by the 2100s from 1995 baseline of 122 days (90 days-149 days). Days with temperatures above 35°C are projected to increase an average of 6 days per decade by 2100s (SSP3-7.0), with higher-than-average increase in days from April to September. Nights with temperatures above 26°C are projected to increase by 307-430% across scenarios from 1995 baseline of 7 days (3 days-13 days) by the end of the century. Beginning in the 2030s, Barbados is anticipated to face a significant escalation in heat stress risks, even under the more optimistic SSP2-4.5 scenario.

Modelling conducted under the CMIP6 framework, featured in the World Bank CCKP, projects a significant rise in the mean annual temperatures of Belize under a high emissions scenario (SSP3-7.0). Temperatures are anticipated to increase by 1.05°C (0.88°C-1.33°C) by 2030 and are projected to reach 29.45°C (28.07°C-30.68°C) by the end of the century, from a baseline of 25.4°C (24.95°C-25.73°C) in 1995. Days with temperatures above 30°C are projected to increase by 74-175% across scenarios by the 2100s from 1995 baseline of 122 days (90 days-149 days). Days with temperatures above 35°C are projected to increase an average of 6 days per decade by 2100s (SSP3-7.0), with higher-than-average increase in days from April to September. Nights with temperatures above 26°C are projected to increase by 307-430% across scenarios from 1995 baseline of 7 days (3 days-13 days) by the end of the century. Beginning in the 2030s, Belize is anticipated to face a significant escalation in heat stress risks, even under the more optimistic SSP2-4.5 scenario.

### Historical

## Climate Problem

The Caribbean region is currently grappling with two significant challenges: climate change, characterized by rising temperatures, and an energy sector heavily dependent on imported fossil fuels, which substantially contribute to greenhouse gas emissions.

## Goal

The IUS Programme aims to address the climate problem through a paradigm shift impact in the BMCs energy sector. This paradigm shift can be achieved IF the project establishes and operationalizes an innovative Integrated Utility Services (IUS) model by facilitating access to concessional financing, capacity building, and regulatory reforms.

## Paradigm Shift

The Programme aims to be a long-term sustainable, scalable, and replicable solution to address the BMCs, and, by extension, the region, climate challenges.

## Outcomes

1. **Outcome 1**: Reduced GHG emissions through widespread adoption of RE, EE, and other DER technologies.
2. **Outcome 2**: Enhanced climate resilience and adaptation of beneficiaries to heat stress.
3. **Outcome 3 (intermediate)**: Increased access to financing for clean energy investments.

## Outputs

1. **Output 1.1**: Strengthened regulatory and institutional frameworks facilitating the deployment of distributed RE, EE, and other DER technologies.
2. **Output 1.2**: Enhanced capacity of customers to invest in distributed RE, EE, and other DERs through the IUS Model.
3. **Output 1.3**: Increased technical capacity of utilities, TSPs, and other stakeholders to deploy and maintain distributed RE, EE, and other DER technologies.
4. **Output 2.1**: Installation of distributed RE, EE, and other DER technologies among a critical mass of utility customers.

## Root Causes and Barriers

- **Unclear legal and regulatory framework**: BMCs need clearer legal or regulatory guidance on issues like DG tariffs/FIT.
- **Limited customer awareness and capacity**: Customers often lack awareness of the benefits of the proposed technologies.
- **Limited availability of TSPs providing end-to-end solutions**: Few can deliver comprehensive solutions.
- **Limited access to low-cost finance**: Difficulty in securing financing due to being considered high-risk.
- **Risk adverse stakeholders**: Both small electricity customers and financial institutions are hesitant to invest in behind-the-meter solutions.
- **Limited technical standards**: There is a need for globally accepted technical standards.

# B.5. Justification for GCF funding request

## B.5.1.

While the Caribbean region has embarked on significant efforts to enhance financing options for RE and EE projects, access to financing remains constrained, highlighting notable challenges across Barbados, Belize, and Jamaica. While some public and private financing exists in Barbados, Belize, and Jamaica, it is primarily focused on businesses and does not cover the diverse technologies included in the IUS programme. Additionally, there is a shortage of commercial banks offering loans for distributed RE, and those that do often charge high interest rates due to the perceived high risk of lending to local households and businesses (particularly the types of lower-income groups and smaller businesses that the IUS model and Programme are particularly well suited to support) to invest in these technologies. EE projects, in particular, are rarely considered for financing. One significant gap is the upfront cost barrier versus long-term savings, which is particularly challenging for lower-income households and businesses. Although RE and EE technologies promise substantial savings over time, the initial investment required can be prohibitively high. This financial challenge is a major deterrent for both public and private investors, who may not have the capital or willingness to invest heavily without immediate returns.

On the public side, capital is often constrained and must be allocated to competing priorities, leaving limited resources for RE and EE projects. Governments in these countries face numerous pressing needs, making it challenging to prioritize and fund energy transitions without substantial external support. Additionally, the lack of a clear regulatory framework and successful demonstrative cases further deters private investment.

## Funding structure

### B.5.2.

To make the proposed programme (and its underlying projects and sub-projects) feasible, CDB is requesting concessional financing from the GCF. These concessional resources are needed in part because the programme will assist electric utilities to launch and institutionalize IUS – a new and innovative approach to supporting investment in distributed RE, EE and other DER technologies/measures among the utilities’ customers – as part of their core business models. The process of launching an IUS scheme in each participating BMC and implementing it at scale involves risks and associated costs that the utilities and partner governments are not able to bear on their own. The following types of GCF financing are therefore critical to the success of this programme.

### B.5.3. Line of credit/Loans

To launch and sustain their initial IUS schemes, partner utilities will need concessional loans to make RE/EE sub-projects appealing to customers, and to enable a more inclusive set of sub-project beneficiaries (including lower-income and vulnerable/marginalized groups). Despite the strong savings and short payback periods associated with distributed RE, EE, and other DERs, Caribbean households and businesses, wary of risk and often financially constrained, might hesitate to invest— a situation exacerbated by COVID-19's economic fallout. Recognizing this, CDB aims to encourage investment in DERs by ensuring initial modest on-bill rates to demonstrate value. Given the region's utilities' similar aversion to risk, introducing the IUS model with affordable rates and favourable terms is crucial. CDB will thus facilitate the transfer of GCF's concessional benefits to utilities, ensuring they, in turn, offer customers more accessible costs for RE, EE, and DER technologies than market rates. Note that several mechanisms are included in the programme design to ensure transfer of this concessionality to the end beneficiaries, including by establishing maximum spreads that can be charged by the electric utilities.

That being the case, CDB is proposing that the GCF Secretariat provide a concessional line of credit amounting to USD 20.8 million. CDB in turn expects to blend the GCF resources with USD 16.2 million in co-financing. As outlined in the Integrated Financial Model (see Annex 3), this would enable the Bank to extend a concessional line of credit to each electric utility/national Executing Entity. Even after the utilities have placed a spread on these resources to cover overhead costs and a risk premium – rates that will be determined jointly with national counterparts, notably the regulators during PY1 – the concessional GCF-CDB blended lines of credit will enable the utilities to provide financing to customers/end-beneficiaries at rates (tentatively 4.5-6.0%) that are competitive relative to the financing provided by traditional/commercial banks (whose products on average start at around 6.5%) while remaining strategically balanced – low enough to support greater social inclusion and incentivize investment, yet not so low as to distort the local markets. Additionally, this financing will allow access to funding for EE technologies, which are not as readily available as those for renewable energy.

### B.5.4. Grant financing

The programme will facilitate capacity building and strengthen the execution capabilities of the regulatory authorities, the Executing Entities, and other key stakeholders through the TA grant financing. CDB will support the partner BMCs, Executing Entities, and other stakeholders to better understand the links between their business operations and climate change, as well as apply this understanding when developing, appraising, financing, and implementing climate change-related sub-projects. These non-revenue-generating activities are only viable at full concessionality. CDB is therefore requesting grant financing to support the implementation of these activities.

### B.5.5.

The GCF is uniquely suited to provide the above-mentioned types of financing for the IUS programme. CDB is not able to provide the necessary package of financing through its own resources. The Bank has modest concessional financing at its disposal through its Special Development Fund (SDF) window, and cannot use these resources to provide the volumes of financing that the three participating countries need to launch and implement IUS at scale. CDB also works with other partners to mobilize funds for climate action – including bilateral development partners – but they are typically unable/unwilling to provide such large amounts of funding for new/innovative approaches such as IUS. Indeed, many partners have a lower risk appetite than is required for the proposed programme. Given its risk appetite, emphasis on innovation and ability to provide large-scale financing (including grant financing), the GCF is therefore uniquely positioned to provide financial support to the proposed IUS programme alongside CDB co-financing. Without the GCF’s support, the proposed programme could not be financed/approved – and without this programme, the participating countries would not be able to launch and implement IUS. Indeed, their fiscal space (while already historically constrained) has become even more limited since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, as well as the subsequent geopolitical instability and macroeconomic developments – notably the worldwide increase in interest rates that have significant increased debt servicing costs in almost all developing countries. Discretionary resources with which to address the financial and non-financial barriers outlined in Section B.2 are simply not available.

### B.5.6. Co-financing

The CDB expects to provide co-financing amounting to about USD 16.2 million as part of the blended line of credit facility that will be available to the BMCs for sub-project installations.

# Independent Technical Advisory Panel’s Assessment of FP275

## Proposal Name

Scaling up the Deployment of Integrated Utility Services (IUS) to Support Energy Sector Transformation in the Caribbean (Phase 1) Programme

**Accredited Entity**  
Caribbean Development Bank

**Country/(ies)**  
Barbados, Belize and Jamaica

**Project/Programme Size**  
Medium

## I. Assessment of the Independent Technical Advisory Panel

### 1.1 Overview

1. This funding proposal, submitted by the Caribbean Development Bank (CDB) as accredited entity (AE), seeks to establish and scale an Integrated Utility Services (IUS) Programme in three Caribbean countries – Barbados, Belize and Jamaica – with the goal of accelerating decarbonization and enhancing energy resilience. The proposal represents Phase I of a larger regional initiative and is focused on enabling regulated national utilities to evolve their business models to provide distributed renewable energy (RE), energy efficiency, and other demand-side energy services directly to customers.

2. The IUS model builds on the concept of utilities acting as energy service companies, bundling financing, installation and maintenance of clean energy solutions, such as solar photovoltaic (PV), battery storage, efficient lighting and appliances, into a service offer that can be repaid through on-bill financing. The utilities would serve as key delivery agents, supported by regulatory reforms, technical service providers and capacity-building activities. CDB serves as the central coordinating entity and lender, channelling GCF resources and co-financing from its own balance sheet.

3. The programme is structured around two interlinked components:

4. **Component 1 (Technical Assistance)** focuses on enabling conditions. It provides grant financing to support the creation of regulatory frameworks, institutional readiness, customer awareness, and technical capacity among utilities, regulators and technology service providers. CDB will establish a dedicated technical assistance team, supported by three national teams, to oversee implementation, stakeholder engagement, and knowledge transfer. These upstream, non-revenue-generating activities are essential to market formation and require full concessionality.

5. **Component 2 (Loan Component)** provides a concessional financing facility for the actual deployment of distributed RE, energy efficiency, and distributed energy resources (DERs) at the customer level. A USD 20.8 million loan from GCF will be blended on a pari passu basis with USD 16.2 million from CDB to establish credit lines to the participating national utilities. Utilities, acting as executing entities (EEs), will on-lend these resources to eligible subprojects – residential, commercial and public sector – covering capital expenditures for clean energy technologies. The subloans are expected to be offered at interest rates in the 4.5 to 6.0 per cent range, which is below prevailing commercial rates and made possible through the concessional GCF-CDB blend.

6. Each utility will retain the credit risk associated with the customer loans and is expected to mobilize an additional USD 25 million in in-kind contributions, largely in the form of advanced metering infrastructure. CDB has committed to entering subsidiary agreements with each utility to ensure proper fiduciary controls, eligibility criteria and mechanisms for monitoring and reporting. Regulatory engagement will be critical, as the design and terms of the on-lending will be finalized in collaboration with national regulators during the first programme year.

7. While the Caribbean region has significant technical potential for distributed RE and strong public support for energy affordability, uptake has been hampered by high upfront costs, credit access constraints, and underdeveloped regulatory environments. This programme attempts to address these barriers by empowering the utilities that are trusted and well-established actors, to become the primary facilitators of behind-the-meter clean energy deployment. By integrating concessional financing, technical assistance and regulatory reform, the programme is designed to create a replicable, utility-led delivery model for the clean energy transition in the context of small island developing States (SIDS).

8. The proposal also positions this initial phase as a pilot for a broader regional roll-out, with Phase II expected to extend the IUS model to other CDB borrowing member countries. Tools, data systems, regulatory approaches and institutional lessons developed under Phase I will be leveraged to reduce costs and streamline future implementation efforts. Knowledge-sharing and regional cooperation are embedded into the design, with the intention of promoting replication and scale.

9. While distributed RE, energy efficiency and other DERs often offer compelling savings and relatively short payback periods, uptake in the Caribbean remains constrained. Many households and businesses are risk-averse and face limited access to affordable finance. Similarly, utilities in the region have historically been conservative in adopting new business models. The IUS Programme seeks to address this by ensuring that financing is offered on favourable terms, with modest initial on-bill repayment rates that help demonstrate value to end users. A key element of the programme design is the structured transfer of concessionality from GCF to utilities, and from utilities to customers. Several mechanisms are included to safeguard this pass-through, including caps on the spreads that utilities can apply to the blended GCF-CDB credit lines. This is essential to making clean energy technologies more accessible than traditional financing channels, while maintaining market discipline.

10. In summary, the IUS Programme proposes an innovative model to unlock distributed clean energy investments through utility leadership. Its success will depend on several factors, including the utilities’ operational readiness, the effectiveness of the regulatory and institutional reforms, and the extent to which concessionality is passed through to end users. While the approach is novel for the region, it rests on a sound rationale and responds directly to the structural barriers facing clean energy deployment in Caribbean SIDS.

### 1.2 Impact Potential

**Scale: Medium**

11. The proposed IUS Programme seeks to deliver both mitigation and adaptation benefits through the utility-led deployment of distributed RE, energy efficiency and other demand-side energy services in Barbados, Belize and Jamaica. These three Caribbean SIDS face increasing climate risks such as intensified storms, prolonged droughts, sea level rise and rising average temperatures, which are already affecting the reliability and cost of power generation and distribution. The programme responds to these structural challenges by enabling households and businesses to access and finance distributed energy solutions that are otherwise difficult to adopt due to cost, credit constraints and regulatory barriers.

12. **Mitigation Potential:** The mitigation impact is based on avoided grid electricity consumption through the installation of rooftop solar PV devices, solar water heaters, efficient air conditioning, lighting and battery systems. The AE estimates that the programme could avoid approximately 601,550 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent (t CO₂ eq) over the lifetime of these technologies, using grid emission factors published by the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change Harmonized Grid Emission Factor data set and adjusted for each country’s national context and nationally determined contribution trajectory.

13. This figure reflects differentiated assumptions about technology lifespans, ranging from 5 years for lighting to 25 years for rooftop solar installations, and incorporates projected technology uptake across both residential and commercial users. Importantly, the mitigation potential varies significantly by country due to differing grid carbon intensities and market baselines. Jamaica, which has a relatively carbon-intensive grid, accounts for over half of the projected greenhouse gas savings (341,197 t CO₂ eq), followed by Barbados (215,219 t CO₂ eq) and Belize (45,134 t CO₂ eq).

14. The independent Technical Advisory Panel (iTAP) finds the mitigation estimation methodology to be sound and in line with Independent Fiscal Institution best practices, as detailed in annexes 3 and 22. However, as with many distributed energy programmes, realized emissions reductions will depend heavily on the rate of actual subloan uptake, sustained use of the technologies, and ongoing maintenance over time. Reliance of the IUS Programme on utilities to manage origination, installation and monitoring provides a plausible delivery channel, but performance should be closely tracked in implementation.

15. **Adaptation Potential:** The IUS Programme also articulates a clear, though modest, adaptation impact. The primary adaptation impact is framed around increasing access to efficient cooling technologies – particularly air conditioning systems – which can help mitigate the health and productivity risks associated with intensifying heat stress across the region. Based on uptake projections, the AE estimates that approximately 10,655 individuals (5,328 women and 5,327 men) will benefit directly from these adaptation measures, primarily through improved thermal comfort and energy cost savings in homes and small businesses.

16. An additional 30,000 indirect beneficiaries are projected, based on broader awareness, demonstration effects and potential market spillovers. While the indirect beneficiary figure is not backed by rigorous market modelling, it is a reasonable proxy for the broader ecosystem impacts expected through regulatory reform, stakeholder engagement and replication.

17. The iTAP notes that the adaptation logic is credible but would benefit from a more detailed vulnerability analysis and a clearer articulation of the causal pathways linking cooling access to resilience outcomes, particularly for marginalized or climate-sensitive populations. Annex 2b provides some baseline data but monitoring and evaluation at the implementation stage will need to track adaptation benefits more rigorously, ideally with disaggregated reporting and engagement of health or resilience-related institutions.

18. **Enabling Environment and Structural Impact:** Beyond direct mitigation and adaptation impacts, the IUS Programme contributes to long-term market development by supporting policy reform and institutional strengthening under Component 1. This includes establishing a regulatory framework for behind-the-meter solutions, enhancing utility and technology provider capacity, and building tools and systems that can be leveraged for future DER deployment. The emphasis on local stakeholder outreach and regional knowledge-sharing is appropriate and consistent with the goal of scaling the model across additional borrowing member countries in a potential Phase II.

19. The AE has also proposed several mechanisms to ensure that concessionality from GCF is passed through to end beneficiaries, including limits on utility spreads and regulatory oversight of retail interest rates. This is particularly important given the AE’s own limitations in offering long-tenor, low-interest financing from its balance sheet. The concessionality is expected to bring end-user loan rates down to approximately 4.2 to 6.0 per cent, below prevailing commercial offerings in the region, thereby enabling access for customer segments that would otherwise be excluded.

20. **Risks to Impact Delivery:** There are several risks to impact realization. First, the financial profile of many potential end users, especially low-income households, raises the possibility of loan default despite the economic case for investment. While utilities are experienced in customer screening, repayment performance will need to be monitored carefully, particularly in the context of inflationary pressure and income volatility. The iTAP felt that the responses of the AE concerning the ability of the EEs to perform the role of credit underwriters were not entirely convincing. We note that low-income households – those who would benefit most from energy efficiency and solar solutions – may present greater credit or payment risks. Utilities are not structured like banks and generally do not have strong underwriting capabilities. This dynamic can lead to higher default rates and, ultimately, regulatory pushback.

21. To mitigate the risk of defaults at higher rates than expected, or non-payment, the programme places emphasis on technologies that offer clear, near-term financial benefits to customers. This design choice is efficient, as it increases the likelihood that energy cost savings will offset repayment obligations from the outset. The use of on-bill financing also provides a degree of transparency by presenting both the savings and the repayment charges on the same utility bill, which may be particularly useful for customers with limited financial literacy. That said, it remains to be seen whether this level of transparency alone will be sufficient to ensure sustained repayment, particularly in lower-income segments. The technical assistance component includes customer outreach and awareness-raising activities, including the development calculators of energy savings, intended to reinforce repayment behaviour by illustrating the medium-term financial advantages of participation. These are helpful features, though their actual effectiveness in reducing credit risk will depend on the quality and reach of implementation.

22. In addition, political changes could disrupt alignment between national designated authorities, regulators and utilities. Although upstream work by development partners (e.g. Deutsche Gesellschaft für Internationale Zusammenarbeit and the Caribbean Community) has helped build institutional readiness, the IUS Programme remains exposed to shifts in public sector commitment or regulatory support. This risk is partially mitigated by the programme design, which includes technical assistance to regulatory bodies and deep utility engagement.

23. The proposal presents a robust mitigation case, a plausible adaptation narrative, and a credible enabling environment for long-term structural transformation in energy service delivery. While actual outcomes will depend on effective execution, customer uptake and institutional continuity, the programme design provides a sound foundation for achieving meaningful and scalable climate impact in the Caribbean SIDS context.

24. The iTAP assesses the impact potential of the IUS Programme as medium.

### 1.3 Paradigm Shift Potential

**Scale: Medium**

25. The proposal offers a well-considered approach to addressing entrenched structural barriers in the Caribbean energy sector and exhibits several characteristics consistent with the GCF definition of paradigm shift. The IUS Programme is designed to catalyse systemic transformation by altering the roles of utilities, consumers and regulators in how distributed energy services are delivered, financed and valued.

26. The proposed IUS Programme aims to advance structural transformation in the energy sectors of Barbados, Belize and Jamaica by embedding distributed RE, energy efficiency and other demand-side technologies within utility operations. The central innovation of the programme lies in positioning regulated utilities as facilitators of customer-level DER deployment, blending financing, installation and service provision into a single utility-led offering. This shift from the traditional supplier model towards a more service-oriented role is both operationally novel and aligned with the broader decentralization of energy systems.

27. The IUS Programme also intends to empower customers through simplified access to clean energy technologies and behavioural tools, enabling greater participation in the energy transition. By integrating on-bill financing and user-facing applications (e.g. energy savings calculators), the programme seeks to normalize investment in DERs among households and micro, small and medium-sized enterprises (MSMEs) that have historically faced high barriers to participation.

28. In parallel, the IUS Programme includes a robust enabling environment component aimed at updating the legal, regulatory and administrative frameworks governing behind-the-meter DERs. This includes support for permitting processes, energy accounting modernization, and the development of performance-based incentive structures. These reforms are critical for anchoring the model in long-term institutional practice and ensuring it can be sustained and replicated without ongoing external concessional support.

29. The iTAP notes that while the programme’s absolute scale is relatively modest, both in terms of greenhouse gas abatement and financing volume, its design is intentionally structured to serve as a model for replication. A future Phase II is envisioned to extend the approach to other CDB borrowing member countries. Knowledge-sharing efforts, digital platforms, and regional institutional engagement (e.g. through the Caribbean Centre for Renewable Energy and Energy Efficiency) are embedded in the design to support regional uptake.

30. However, while the IUS Programme is well-conceived from a distributed energy market development perspective, its paradigm-shifting potential must be assessed considering broader sector planning trends in the region, particularly in Barbados. ## 1 Introduction

The Caribbean Development Bank (CDB) is in the process of implementing the “Scaling up the Deployment of Integrated Utility Services (IUS) to Support Energy Sector Transformation in the Caribbean” (Phase I) Programme in the Caribbean, which is a Green Climate Fund (GCF) funded activity. This Phase of the project will have three Borrowing Member Countries (BMCs) participating in the programme and they are as follows: Barbados, Belize, and Jamaica. These three BMCs have pledged to reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions through the adoption of renewable energy (RE) to facilitate energy transition and expanding the scale of energy efficiency (EE) measures.

The GCF is committed to maximising the impact of its funding for adaptation and mitigation while ensuring environmental, social, economic and development co-benefits are advanced and a gender-sensitive approach is taken[^1]. With its updated 2020 gender policy, the GCF is committed to promote and integrate gender equality in its investment criteria. The Gender Policy has three main objectives which are as follows:

1. Utilise a comprehensive gender approach applied by the institution and its network of partners which include accredited entities (AEs), national designated authorities (NDAs) and focal points to support climate change interventions and innovations.
2. Promote climate investments that advance gender equality and minimise social, gender and climate related risks through climate change mitigation and adaptation measures.
3. Contribute to shrinking the gender gap caused by climate change-exacerbated social, economic and environmental vulnerabilities through mainstreaming gender equality in climate investments.

The gender policy’s application to the IUS Project would entail:

- Gender inequality is addressed/reduced and through enhanced stakeholder engagement can generate sustainable livelihoods opportunities, promote health and wellbeing, and increase resilience to climate-induced shocks and risks for men and women.
- An enabling environment among GCF stakeholders built on existing policy commitments, social and environmental standards and redress mechanisms is supported and sustained in order to achieve gender equality.
- The comprehensive, systematic, and participatory collection of sex-disaggregated data and the execution of qualitative and quantitative gender analysis is supported to effectively monitor, report and evaluate project impact.

[^1]: “Gender Policy 2020-2023” retrieved from [GCF Gender Policy](https://www.greenclimate.fund/document/gender-policy)

In accordance with GCF gender policies and guidance, the gender assessment is done as a critical element of the project proposal to outline how the project while attempting to meet its goal of supporting the target countries’ transition to renewable energy states, will benefit vulnerable groups and how in particular will vulnerable groups be able to participate in this project. The gender assessment will elucidate on factors that will impair vulnerable groups’ ability to benefit fully and identify areas of entry to address these issues while advancing gender equality and supporting a Just Transition.

The Assessment builds on the previous work in the baseline assessment and seeks to provide a deeper understanding of how the proposed technologies (solar water heaters, rooftop solar photovoltaic, efficient cooling and HVAC, and LED lighting) will impact populations of the target countries as well as identify barriers to uptake.

The Assessment is expected to inform:

- the gender mainstreaming approach for the GCF proposal;
- capacity-building activities in the overall proposal;
- gender-sensitive monitoring and evaluation tools that may be needed; and
- Institutional policy, programming and operational actions/steps that may be needed in the short, medium and or long-term to support the IUS model implementation in an equal, inclusive and just manner.

### 1.1 Limitations

This assessment utilised a mixed method approach i.e. a desk-based research and stakeholder engagement. However, this process was still hindered by a few factors including:

- Outdated data – the region has limited human resources funding and infrastructure available to execute frequent data collection and analysis activities which results in the use of data at least 5 years old in this assessment. Despite the recent efforts of Caribbean Centre for Renewable Energy and Energy Efficiency (CCREEE) to narrow this gap, some of the essential questions for the gender assessment still are not generally covered or readily available.
- Data availability – access to data is a weak point in the Caribbean as many governments and agencies possess updated data but have issues with processing, analysing, and publishing said data for public access, also due to limited human resources and infrastructure. Due to demand also for consultations and engagement, there was limited time to follow up in detail with the Statistics Departments of each country to inform this draft assessment.

### 3.2.2 Usage

#### Rooftop Solar Photovoltaic

Within the Maya indigenous communities in the Toledo district (found in the southernmost part of the country) exist technical capacity to deploy solar energy technologies. Three Maya women were trained in solar energy technology at the Barefoot University in India.<sup>92</sup> From their experience, the benefits of women being involved in the field could be seen. With this capacity gained and material support, they were able to provide the community of Santa Teresa solar power over a two-year timespan. Additionally, through a project called Indigenous Female Solar Engineers Scaling up Solar Energy to Machakilha and Graham Creek, a collaboration between Plenty Belize and the Ministry of Rural Transformation, these women will be helping to provide these communities with solar powered electricity while also equipping these communities with the knowledge to maintain the technologies on their own.

This ongoing project has a strong emphasis on ensuring all residents of the community are empowered through skills-building and other capacity building workshops made available to all. Another emphasis of this project is the promotion of and support women in non-traditional roles with women who were trained at Barefoot University serving as models for other women and girls of the nation. The solar electrification provided by this project generate several direct household benefits for all members. These benefits address children’s need for education through the provision of light and power for technologies that aid in education. For the adults in the household, access to electricity provide them with new opportunities for their livelihoods or enhancements to their livelihoods which in turn, enhances their ability to make decisions to increase their quality of life and implement climate change adaptation measures.<sup>93</sup> Alongside the direct household benefits, the project provides these communities with communal benefits such as 1) small shops with the ability to run a refrigerator/freezer ran by one or two systems and these refrigerators serve to enhance food security and food safety through the ability to store foods and extend food’s shelf life, 2) a medium size power system installed at the primary school which would enhance the school’s ability to provide quality education to these children, and 3) a solar water pump to provide potable water which would improve the quality of health in these areas by providing clean water and reduce the time spent by primarily women in fetching water for household use.

Access to electricity provides numerous benefits to those living in rural communities. It provides cleaner energy to use for lighting and other tasks and reduces exposure to smoke and other pollutants released from the burning of coal, firewood and kerosene that are the typical biomass or fossil-fuel-based energy sources typically used in rural communities. This allows them to save money as the need to purchase fuel is reduced and the savings can be used to increase the quality of their lives in other ways. With reliable access to electricity, persons in very remote areas are now able to utilise mobile/satellite phones and this provides many benefits to the community as it increases their security to an extent as calls for help can be sent out faster than persons having to journey out of the community, and this also applies to requesting medical aid. Additionally, having enhanced communication increases their access to information which would ideally expand their capacity for participation and to exercise rights in the context of energy as well as climate change mitigation. Similarly, the Garifuna communities in the Stann Creek District also utilize the combustion of fuels to meet their energy needs as well and face similar issues.

The abovementioned issues and concerns primarily affect those living in Toledo and Stann Creek districts. Those living in Cayo, Orange Walk, Corozal and Belize City districts which constitute the more urban areas of the country, enjoy better connections to electricity, thus reducing their need to rely on the burning of fuels to provide energy to carry out domestic duties or to support their livelihoods. In turn, they are less likely to be exposed to harmful pollutants released from the combustion of these fuels. A notable exception to this would be for the island of Caye Caulker with 1,700 persons residing on the island that would have utilised a diesel generator to supply the island with power. #### LED Lighting

This reduction in monthly expenses can provide single income especially female-headed homes with some relief as their financial burdens are lessened which could possibly translate to better mental health as persons would have less issues to worry about. At the same time, these savings though small, could be utilised to improve other aspects of the household.

BEL carried out a renewable energy project nationwide, aimed at replacing 31,000 streetlights with LED lights in 2016.<sup>96</sup> This project benefited areas across the country that did not have well lit streets, noting that some lights were installed in strategic areas frequented by people on their way to work or home to help deter criminal activities. Lighting can be used as a passive measure to reduce crime as persons would feel less inclined to commit criminal acts when their identity is more exposed.<sup>97</sup> Additionally, women would feel a bit more empowered to participate in society, particularly in sectors that may have them working late at night as the light provides a sense of safety.

#### Solar Water Heating

The Development Finance Corporation (DFC) has partnered with the Belize Biltmore Plaza to promote the adoption of renewable energy in businesses. While a lot of the promotion and awareness efforts for SWH has been targeted at the tourism sector, SWH can also be beneficial to the domestic household. The use of a SWH can lead to the reduction of electricity consumption as less electricity is used to heat water needed for showering and other domestic tasks. Through this reduction, persons could possibly save up to USD 597.58 (BZD 1204) per year depending on the usage.<sup>100</sup> This could lead to increased financial ability through the savings made from using a SWH. ### 3.2.4 Barriers

#### 3.2.3.1 Financial Considerations

Rising costs of living is not the only factor that inhibits the financial ability of Belizean people, particularly women to afford a range of energy sources and technologies. Employment greatly impacts the finances available to persons whether they are employed in seasonal sectors, working in underpaid jobs or working in the informal sector. Many persons operate in the informal sector, particularly in rural areas as they may lack qualifications (education) or fail to meet requirements (possessing a valid form of national identification) or simply unable to afford the costs of travel. Many places of employment require persons to possess a valid form of national identification, however, it must be noted this could be a barrier of entry to low-income persons as the national identification card costs USD 2.48 (BZD 5) to issue for the first time and USD 12.91 (BZD 26) to replace while the driver’s license costs USD 44.68 (BZD 90) to issue and USD 29.79 (BZD 60) to renew yearly. These costs may be untenable for some as they balance costs for themselves and unemployed dependents.

The UNICEF 2015 Multi-Indicator Cluster Survey for Belize found only 37% of women and 40% of men have a secondary level education. It also notes that two in five women (2 in 5) have only obtained a primary school level of education and a similar rate can be seen in men. These factors alone or working in conjunction together leave a noted proportion of persons (35.3% or 64,268 persons out of the 182,084 employed persons) with only informal work as a means of generating income.

Working in the informal sector may provide persons with opportunities and certain freedoms particularly rural women who may not have the ability to access more formal employment due to ineligibility or conflicting responsibilities (such as family care). Despite these benefits, informal sector work can lack the security and integrity afforded to formal employment. This can have far-reaching consequences for persons as the lack of an employment letter or self-employed forms could pose as a barrier to accessing loans and other financial tools from financial institutions. Having access to additional finance will be important to purchase renewable energy technologies. The average cost of a solar water heater in Belize ranges from USD 992.25 (BZD 1998.71) to USD 1984.50 (BZD 3,997.42) which is 7-14.1% of the average yearly income (USD 14,112/BZD 28,426.13) in Belize. Noting this level of expense, access to financial aid will be a necessity for low-income female-headed households and other vulnerable groups to access these technologies, without which they would otherwise be left behind in the transition to green energy.

Similar barriers are experienced by indigenous communities, especially those in rural parts of the country. These communities, particularly the Maya, are located in areas that are quite difficult to access, often without fully developed roads and telecommunication infrastructure and/or coverage. These conditions pose a significant hindrance to the economic empowerment of indigenous people. The barriers posed by limited transportation options and long travel times severely limit economic opportunities, be it seeking employment or selling goods as examples of how livelihoods could be impacted. This leaves formal and informal agriculture as a source of livelihood for many and depending on the way agricultural activity is managed, many may be ineligible to apply for loans from financial institutions. Obtaining loans could entail needing valid identification, financial records, business plans, marketing plans and some rural indigenous farmers, especially female farmers, may not have the capacity to develop these critical documents to meet the scope of the institutions.

Persons with disabilities (PWDs) face their own unique barriers and plight when it comes to access to finance and the cost of living. Some PWDs live in squalor as they subsist on whatever aid they receive from relatives or organisations or government subvention. Many PWDs are unable to work due to (i) a lack of accommodations in the workplace to meet the needs of their disability and (ii) their limited access to education because of their disability thus the lack of necessary qualifications. Some PWDs live with relatives on whom they are entirely dependent financially, thereby creating an additional set of possible challenges to caregivers.

Given this financial dependence, PWDs have limited economic power. In addition to the lack of finances due to unemployment, the cost of caring and treating for disabilities can be quite expensive - this could range from the price of obtaining mobility aids to requiring frequent medical treatments and/or the price of medicine to treat chronic conditions. PWDs’ ability to implement renewable energy technologies is thus lowered due to these financial demands as they form additional financial hurdles that PWDs must deal with.

#### 3.2.3.2 Housing

The state and quality of housing has been an issue identified in Belize even within urban areas of the country in relation to the implementation (installation) of RE/DER technologies identified for the IUS project particularly for solar water heaters, and rooftop solar. It must be noted that the project entails rooftop solar (although there is a possibility of this technology being implemented in other ways) and efficient cooling through air conditioning, there exists a requirement for homes to meet certain standards in order for these technologies to be effective. Figure 3-16 shows the characteristics of housing across the country derived from the MICS survey. From the MICS survey it can be seen that a low but still significant number of households would not be able to support some of these technologies efficiently and would require adjustments or support to bring their houses up to standard. Only 95.7% of urban households and 88% of rural households have finished floors, roofing and walls. 7.9% of urban households and 12% of rural households have rudimentary roofing which is of concern due to these types of roofs being unable to support the solar water heaters or solar photovoltaic and may even suffer damages from them.

From the survey, 67% of households are headed by men with the remaining 33% headed by women. The Mestizo/Spanish/Latino ethnic group accounts for the largest ethnic group for household heads at 49.1%, followed by Creole at 25.5% while the indigenous ethic groups of Maya and Garifuna account for 9.1% and 5.7% respectively.

The state of housing quality is especially of concern for PWDs in situations where they are living on their own and typically lack the finances to secure proper housing or to maintain and upkeep their homes. Another group of major concern, because of housing quality and housing infrastructure, is indigenous communities living in rural areas, especially those who live in traditional housing. Traditional indigenous housing structures are not typically suited to RE, EE and DERs and require an adapted approach (either installation or the technology itself). Rooftop solar, in particular, may compromise the structural integrity of the roofing over time without adjustments.

### Livelihoods

A critical element to consider when assessing the barriers and the capacities of persons to implement the target technologies is understanding the source of their income generation. Livelihood plays a major role in a person’s ability to build capacity and empower themselves and would have implications for the climate change adaptations, in this case the implementation of RE/EE/DER technologies they can afford and are capable of implementing. While livelihoods could be impacted by a person’s ability to access education and opportunities, it influences the other barriers to accessing these technologies such as housing and finance. Income from livelihoods would then determine what technologies a person can afford, what loans they are eligible and what other related measures they are able to adopt for the successful and effective implementation of the target technologies.

One of the biggest factors that affects a person’s livelihood and their options for livelihood is their ability to access education and assets. Having access to necessary resources and education, empowers one to capitalise on opportunities and enhances their ability to participate in climate change adaptation measures in this instance. From the highest education level attained by heads of households, it could be seen that almost half (49.2%) heads of households have only primary school level of education as their highest form education, 26.7% with a secondary school level, 16.9% with a tertiary school level and 7.2% with no formal education. It is critical to acknowledge that affects the kinds of livelihoods persons engage in and it is reflected in the occupations held by persons as seen in the Belize Labour Force Survey. From the 2022 survey, services and sales workers had the largest share of employed persons at 58.1 and generally female-dominated (39.7% females to 18.4% males) while elementary occupations followed with 35.5, still male-dominated (21.5% males to 14% female). A large percentage of services and sales jobs are associated with the tourism sector (with it accounting for 28% of employed persons) which, in turn, has significant implications for income generation for those tied to the sector. The seasonal nature of the tourism sector is of concern due persons’ sources of income decreasing during the off-peak season which receives less visitors to the country (the high season for tourism in Belize is from December to April which coincides with its dry season) as this will affect the level of loan they might qualify for, the financial decisions they need to make during and before the off-season and other issues that may result from the lowered economic ability during this time.

Traditional gender norms are at play in the share of men and women in the tourism sector. Despite it being one of the more important sectors in the country, women still dominate this sector as jobs related to catering to and providing services to persons which are typically perceived as activities for women. This perception of some jobs being deemed for women, and some being deemed for men continues to perpetuate the labour market segmentation and impacts career/livelihood choices for men and women. This is seen above with the share of women employed in sales and services jobs accounting for double the number of men in this field. This implies that these traditional gender norms still hold root in society with the way persons choose careers. The domination of women in this field has one major consequence which is on a yearly basis; many women see reduced income between May to November when the country receives less visitors and business slows down. The gender division of labour can also be seen in the share of women and men in clerical support worker roles (13.5% for women and 5.6% for men). Meanwhile, in more technical/physical fields, men dominate more as seen in the following breakdown: plant and machine operators 9.1% men to 0.6% women, craft and related trades works 19.3% men to 3.8% women and skilled agricultural and forestry workers 7.8% men to 0.8 women%. #### 3.3.2 Usage

In general, women tend to face a disproportionate burden of labour compared with men, through their responsibilities taking care of children and other household tasks, in addition to any labour performed outside the home for earned income. In terms of women’s abilities to access financial assets and resources, infrastructure services can play a role in lessening the added burdens placed on women though their dual roles as caretakers and paid employees in the labour force.

Ensuring proper services through sustainable energy provisions can provide both cost and time-savings to consumers, especially women who already may be operating in a deficit in each of these areas. These economic and time savings may allow women to accumulate capital which can have additional benefits in terms of resource allocation toward better health, added income and education for women in most contexts.

**LED Lighting**

In recent years, the Jamaican Public Service (JPS) has been working on energy conversion projects and initiatives in Jamaica, including those for light-emitting diode (LED) lighting and high-pressure sodium (HSP) lamps. Currently, LED lighting and public buildings targeted for energy efficiency retrofits consume only 7% of Jamaica’s total generated electricity. In 2018, the JPS, converted 63% of the island's streetlights over to LEDs. In real terms, this covered a total of 67,000 – out of the 105,000 streetlights on the nation’s roads. In 2022, the company was also reported to have converted all existing high-pressure sodium (HPS) lamps with LEDs within Jamaica. These are more energy efficient, have a longer service life and added intelligent-technology capabilities. JPS is continuing work on its streetlight general maintenance and repair programs in the interim.

Difficulties with the HPS lighting, including vandalism, have been an issue. These problems are confirmed with statistics showing that an average of 620 repairs per month were conducted by JPS, due to high rates of maintenance repairs due to crime. Efforts by JPS on street lighting is a part of Jamaica’s Energy Policy, to facilitate the introduction of energy-saving devices e.g., LED, solar panels, and solar street lighting under Goal 1. Applications from persons for new streetlights can be made to the Ministry of Local Government, as Jamaica Public Service (JPS) must be authorized by the Parish Council to install new street lighting infrastructure and outside of Jamaica’s National Energy Policy, there are no noted implementing frameworks that support the energy transition to LEDs.

**LED Lighting for Support for Women and Vulnerable Groups**

Provision of improved lighting services can support various vulnerable groups, in the urban and rural context. Poor lighting services can keep women and girls out of public spaces, exacerbating their senses of vulnerability and insecurity, and placing restrictions of their ability to safely pursue independent living. Ample street lighting has been seen to reduce the prevalence of crime in areas with sufficient lighting. Increased Street lighting can offer important economic advantages in addition to safety benefits. Better lighting services can enable longer business hours in some cases where work hours transition from daylight hours, this is especially the case for women who operate in the informal economy in roles such as street vendors.

By creating safe gathering spaces, lighting services have also been shown to support the reduction of social isolation, boost community cohesion, and decrease the number of people, especially women who stay indoors during the night. For other vulnerable populations, improved lighting services has also shown to assist with children’s education in terms of how well they perform and function by supporting their visual, cognitive, and behavioural abilities through focus, alertness, and mood management. It also makes it easier for those with visual disabilities to see better and allows a person with hearing impairments to make lipreading easier.

Problems and challenges, however, do exist in transitioning energy systems and improving lighting capacity. In 2021, the theft situation rapidly worsened in Jamaica, hampering government efforts to illuminate crime-ridden areas. The power company disclosed in 2019 that the thefts were harming its investments in public lighting. Within the first 5 months of the year, 525 streetlights have been stolen. The US Department of State reports that the municipalities of Montego Bay and Portmore have some of Jamaica's worst rates of violent crime. These locations are where streetlight theft was most prevalent. Theft also occurs in the Northern towns of Moneague and Claremont in St. Ann, Jamaica's largest parish.

In such environments, the lack of proper public lighting due to theft can further exacerbate feelings of insecurity and vulnerability, especially for women who may need to navigate these areas during nighttime hours. Moreover, women are often primary caregivers and responsible for the safety and well-being of their families. Insufficient lighting in their communities can hinder their ability to protect themselves and their children, further compromising their sense of security.

As most of the country has already been converted to LED lighting, additional challenges can also occur during the repair and replacement process of stolen LEDs. The JPS may be reluctant to constantly invest in replacement, especially in high-crime areas as mentioned before. It may not be deemed feasible to fix streetlights, leaving the areas that need lighting the most, without. The safety of JPS employees is also a concern as installing and fixing streetlights in high crime areas is another potential constraint as employees become hesitant to enter areas for fear of persons stealing the lights from their possession and for their own personal safety.

**Solar Water Heating**

In 2009, it was estimated that more than 5,000 solar water heaters (SWH) were installed in Jamaica. As of 2020, Jamaica’s use of renewables accounted for approximately 11 per cent of its energy: 6.5% wind, 3.5% hydropower, and 1% solar power.

The SWH market in Jamaica has the potential to be expanded due to the high levels of insolation and urbanism. The country receives an average daily peak of 4.6 hours of sunlight for solar panel absorption. The combination of good climatic conditions and Jamaica’s readiness and urgency to diversify its energy sector will create favorable demand for SWH. However, there are specific barriers to SWH adoption which include limited access to capital, incomplete information and data, lack of policies governing the injection and transitions of renewable energy resources into the power grid and economic regulatory distortion. Each of these factors pose challenges to promoting gender equality and women’s empowerment efforts due to the limited access to capital available for women, a lack of available sex disaggregated data to inform decision-making and the limitations on gender mainstreaming in policy across the energy sector more generally.

SWH systems in the Caribbean typically have short payback periods and a high net present value (NPV) which means over time, not only will systems pay for themselves but also generate profits or in this case immense savings. They can also be extended depending on incentives, local electricity rates, and system sizing. Prices vary based on sizes offered, materials used and other factors, but they generally begin at USD1,000 not including installation. This cost can deter home and business owners from investing. These issues of costing can pose a significant barrier for women-headed households who, in general, de facto face income constraints. It is estimated that 47% of children live in single-parent homes with their biological mothers. To address some of the issues around this cost prohibitive barrier to access, financial institutions have begun offering segmented renewable energy financing. Although the direct purpose of environmentally conscious financial initiatives are typically intended to support energy efficiencies more generally, conservation and alternatives, they can be utilised for the installation of SWH. The Development Bank of Jamaica, Exim Bank, National Housing Trust and the National People’s Cooperative Bank of Jamaica Limited all offer financial services specifically mentioning SWH.

Heated water may be considered a luxury for some, but it is a part of many persons’ everyday life. While the water is mainly used by everyone in the household for showering, women tend to use warm water more for themselves as well as for their children and household chores. As the current levels of water heating currently account for up to 30 per cent of a domestic light bill in Jamaica, more sustainable methods of water heating should be considered.

SWH can provide financial benefits along with environmental sustainability. It lowers the carbon footprint and is expected to reduce electricity bills since the fuel is free sunlight. Due to low operating and maintenance costs, solar water heaters over the long-term cover the huge initial fixed cost. When all costs for purchase, installation, maintenance and operation are taken into account after the payback period. the SWH systems are expected to provide hot water for free with bare maintenance costs as sunlight is free. Thereafter, electricity bills can be reduced by 30%, making it a cost-effective option for low-income homes including single-mother homes, persons with disabilities and retired persons.

## Efficient Cooling and Refrigeration

Energy-related behaviours and cooling energy consumption are influenced by gender, noting variations in consumption among different genders. A general gender comparison study conducted by researchers in China noted that, particularly for women, using the air conditioner frequently is a regular practice that affects cooling energy use. However, thermal preferences have more noticeable influence on cooling energy consumption for males than females. These differences exhibit themselves in common habits such as daily average hours of air conditioning use, the use of electric fans in place of air conditioners, and the occupancy ratio in room and these habits have an impact on cooling energy consumption for both men and women. Women are more likely to provide unpaid care for young children, elderly individuals, and those with disabilities, who spend more time indoors due to physical and mental conditions and would therefore require more cooling in poorly ventilated homes. In comparison, men are shown to be more likely to labour outdoors or in hot locations, making them more sensitive to the effects of climate change from an outdoor labour perspective which can also have similar implication towards health and livelihood management.

### Barriers

Some of the specific challenges women face in terms of access to affordable energy, and more specifically with their roles as typically the primary care providers in the home, include having access to energy for maintaining domestic work, access to water and energy options that are both safe and affordable, and help ease the burden women already face by taking on more hours of labour, through their domestic duties and those outside the home. Various barriers also affect uptake and use of technologies.

#### Institutional

A weak enabling policy environment for SWH also poses a barrier to implementation. While Jamaica has multiple policies on renewable energy, there seems to be only one with stipulations specifically for SWH. Jamaica’s National Energy Policy 2009-2030 provides incentives for the installation of SWH and solar lights in the public and private sectors and communities are included under Goal 1: Jamaicans use energy wisely and aggressively pursue opportunities for conservation and efficiency. The incentives, include concessional loans and import duties on EE and RE, and the exemption of lithium-ion batteries from the General Consumption Tax.

Lack of public awareness also limits the usage of SWH systems. There are currently no specific outreach campaigns for SWH in Jamaica. There have been efforts to increase awareness of the merits of renewable energy more generally, however, in order for women to have access to this renewable source of energy, financial incentives for system installations, as well as low-interest rates loan programs, should be implemented.

Another barrier that arises in the administrative process involved when applying for the installation of new lights or the replacement of lights. The process includes many steps and can take a long time to implement, posing an obstacle to persons applying. This process makes it more difficult to have consistent street lighting. The lack of street lighting increases the risk of domestic violence and other forms of gender-based violence to occur during times where sunlight is absent. With a high rate of violence within the country, the lack of this infrastructure increases the risk and probability of these events occurring and further burden the already burdened police and security services in the country.

#### Social

Without increasing the provision of support through the addition of reliable and sustainable services and infrastructure, it is more difficult for women in low-income households to improve their positions and livelihoods, their ability to earn decent livelihoods, and to improve their socio-economic situation and outlook. Without target actions in these areas, the imbalance women face in their economic status from abilities to engage in income-generating activities and benefit from time time-savings, may consequently lead to further negative impacts across all areas mentioned including health, housing, and education for both women and the families they provide for.

As primary consumers and users of domestic energy, for CDRs, e.g. washing, household cleaning and personal care, cooking etc., women need to be involved in energy efficiency and renewable energy projects from their direct and indirect impacts on levels and usage of household energy consumption. Women may also inform their children's future energy consumption and conservation practices through their own examples and sustainable behaviours in the home. They may also engage children in discussions around energy usage, incorporate energy-conscious practices into daily life, or utilise educational resources and involve children in energy-saving initiatives. These means of engaging with their environment impacts the way future generations will learn to engage in efficient and sustainable energy use and practices in a household.

There are other barriers faced in implementing rooftop solar, in particular. Due to the lower discretionary income in low- to medium-income communities, hefty upfront expenses for rooftop solar can be a significant deterrent. Even when upfront costs are decreased, low-income persons do not always benefit fairly from such offers. Single-family homeowners also frequently encounter housing-related obstacles to implementing rooftop PV, such as unsuitable rooftops, and shoddy or outdated electrical wiring. The best roofs for solar have a solid foundation, no leaks, and do not require much maintenance when first installed. Families with lower incomes are more likely to reside in older homes than those with higher incomes, necessitating more expensive home renovations to accommodate on-site solar arrays.

The focus of apartment or townhouse owners is one of the structural barriers. Moreover, adding to this barrier is the unknown element of who bears responsibility for applicable, and any damages that installation or usage may cause to the property.

## 4.2 Cross-cutting Stakeholder Findings

Despite being from different countries and operating in different contexts, stakeholders shared similar insights and concerns with regard to the issues discussed during direct engagement via key informant interviews and group interviews as well as focus group discussions. Below are some key findings that emerged and are applicable across all countries.

### 4.2.1 Finance

- A major hurdle to accessing loans particularly for women is time. Banking transactions typically require a person to allocate a fair of amount of time and effort to engage these services and women especially those from female-headed households are usually constrained for time due to their domestic duties and responsibilities.
- Location plays a role in hindering access to financial tools, particularly for many who reside in rural locations (especially those living in indigenous communities). The nearest financial institution is usually very far away from where they reside, and the distance acts a deterrent. This particular barrier can also involve time - the time it takes to get to and from the bank/financial institution and the cost to get there and back.
- Banking processes can be tedious and the requirements to engage in their services such as proof of address, form of national identification, lack of collateral often eliminate or discourage persons from seeking these options.
- Persons working in the informal sector and low-income persons utilise informal saving mechanisms such as syndicate, partner, boxhand, etc. to pool financial resources that allow persons to conduct large transactions when they are unable to meet requirements imposed by financial institutions. In some cases, these pooled resources may be used to meet downpayments requested by financial institutions.

### 4.2.2 Gender Mainstreaming Capacity

- Efforts are being made by energy ministries to enhance their gender mainstreaming capacities. The Ministry of Energy and Business of Barbados noted that work on a gender equality strategy and action plan for the electricity sector is being advanced that could have bearing on this project (however, access to this document has not been realised).
- Many NGOs, particularly the disability-focused NGOs noted that they are rarely consulted for any project development or implementation, and it could be seen in the results of the project as their vulnerable groups do not benefit fully from these works.
- Gender mainstreaming capacity is low among government stakeholders, noting many do not possess gender policies and rarely engage with the gender bureaux of their countries. However, it must be stated that with increased engagement with international funds which require gender to be involved in any work they fund, capacity is slowly being developed and more awareness on gender is being raised.

### 4.2.3 Employment in the Energy Sector

- The energy sector is traditionally not one in which women participated significantly in the past but in more recent years, this has been changing with women involved across several areas. Numbers remain relatively low as men still dominate this field. Feedback received via stakeholder interviews (both KIIs and FGDs), suggest that a few technical roles in the Energy Ministry are now occupied by women.

### 4.2.4 Engagement with Vulnerable Groups

- NGOs’, in their responses, indicate that they are rarely consulted by project actors on energy projects.
- Many government stakeholders noted that there are no explicit policies that guide stakeholder engagement to ensure its gender-responsiveness or inclusiveness.

### 4.2.5 Indigenous Persons (Belize)

- Many of the indigenous people are found in far/remote or rural areas of the countries and exhibit the highest rates of poverty in the country and share similar vulnerabilities and challenges with access.
- Respect must be paid to governing systems employed within the different communities and knowledge of these systems is critical to successfully navigating engagements with local indigenous populations.
- Recognising and acknowledging that a “one size fits all” approach will not work when engaging indigenous communities as they hold varying traditions and practices.

## 4.3 Barriers Across the Region

From the analysis of the three countries, a pattern of barriers has emerged. Despite differences in location, culture, economies, etc. the people particularly vulnerable groups all face similar barriers when it comes to accessing renewable energy technologies. While in Barbados, we see the most support for the adoption of renewable energy technologies by the population, vulnerable groups still encounter comparative issues faced by persons in countries with less of a supporting framework.

There is relatively poor access to and availability of sex and age-disaggregated data across the three countries and the Caribbean region in general, particularly on energy use, demand, needs and uptake. This results in challenges in differentiating energy usage among men, women, gender-diverse persons, PWDs, indigenous persons, children and the elderly. Current available energy data usually is household rather than individual specific and does not engender intersectional analysis. Also available to some extent is data/information on implications for the design and implementation of energy services, for education campaigns, and about financial measures. This means that existing and future actions utilising current data are less effective as the approaches would tend to be more generic. A generic approach is not desirable for many reasons including the likelihood that investments will not address the practical or the strategic gender needs that do exist amongst various groups and that it would make it difficult to also differentiate impacts across groups of beneficiaries.

Economic barriers are heavily present in all three countries as strict requirements for loans imposed by banks is the norm across the region and women are challenged to enhance their access and control of such resources because of reduced economic ability due to traditional divisions of labour or burdens imposed by unpaid care work. In Belize, indigenous people exhibit the highest rates of poverty while also being located in the most rural areas of their respective countries and with the greatest collective need for modern and accessible energy solutions. Not only do they have to navigate the loan requirements, but they must also contend with the challenges that time and distance imposes on them.

Socially, we can see in the three countries that work in the energy sector is skewed more towards men than women. Even the small percentage of women working in the sector mostly take on roles traditionally designated to women such as clerical and administrative work with few women taking on technical roles in the field. This gendered division of employment is heavily influenced by patriarchal norms and patriarchal norms’ influence is not only restricted to formal work. The gendered division of labour can be seen in the allocation of domestic work which is skewed disproportionately to women and is a common occurrence in the region. This imbalance of domestic labour impairs women’s economic ability through the limited finances they are able to attain due to the resulting time-poverty that comes from them performing the majority of domestic, primarily unpaid care work.

# 5 Key Conclusions

Based on the engagement with stakeholders and the research, there is both appetite and increasing need for renewable energy technologies, particularly affordable, easily maintained and those that generate multiple co-benefits. From available data and limited consumer analysis, demand is primarily rooted in potential cost and income savings and the additional potential for income- generation that may arise. As a result, the climate change adaptations benefits derived from these technologies are often viewed as additional benefits and does not serve the main motivation for driving the interest/desire of households and businesses for technologies. For government actors, a combination of these factors appears relevant, including the combined mitigation-adaptation co-benefit.

Despite this demand, many persons across the three focus countries are restricted from investing in these technologies primarily due to financial barriers. Uptake of these technologies for low-income persons is often restrained by limited financial resources or inability to access additional finance from financial institutions due to various factors such as limited collateral, time conflicts, and competing priorities. This project’s ability to market and distribute these technologies in a way that minimises strain on low-income persons will be key. Doing so in ways that respond to social, cultural and livelihoods needs is crucial to meet multiple SDGs.

Consideration must be given to involving vulnerable groups in the further development and implementation of this project. The goal of this project supporting a Just Transition cannot be accomplished without involving those typically left behind and who are generally underrepresented in the development and expansion of the sector and in reaping the benefits from significant public sector and public finance investment. Moreover, the project should be actively gender-responsive paying close attention to intersectional gender needs as well as intersecting energy demand.

For Belize with large rural populations and significant indigenous sub-populations, customer outreach is crucial. Coupled with the financial issues mentioned, these communities also deal with the issues related to their location meaning their isolation and distance from the rest of the country and from technology hubs. Likewise, the needs of PWDs require greater attention and a specialised response.

Noting the lack of gender mainstreaming capacity amongst the government stakeholders who will be involved in the project and critically the lack of engagement with most of the executing entities, there are clear gaps that will need to be bridged. Efforts to boost gender awareness and sensitivity in ways that shape implementation, sustainability and scaling-up will be critical in the pre-implementation period of the project, as well as the first year of implementation.

**Key chapter takeaway**

While the demand and need for an IUS project is clearly present in the target countries and there exist enabling environments of varying degrees to support the project. At present, given the capacities of key stakeholders and state of policies, the ability of this project to successfully support and promote a Just Transition is highly questionable and will need several high level as well as practical interventions to ensure it is able to fulfill this goal. Key opportunities to enable the participation of low-income and single-headed households could be tapped into as well, leaving fewer persons behind in the energy transition in the short and medium-term. Gender and social considerations are relevant in all countries.

# 7 Gender Action Plan

In the preparation of the proposal for the “Scaling up the Deployment of Integrated Utility Services (IUS) to Support Energy Sector Transformation in the Caribbean (Phase I)” Programme, it is essential to ensure the programme meets both the Green Climate Fund’s (GCF’s) and Caribbean Development Bank’s (CDB’s) requirements for gender equality. As a result, a gender assessment was done and from this assessment, emerged areas of concern and weakness that need to be addressed to ensure gender is adequately mainstreamed in this programme so it can support a Just Transition.

Gender mainstreaming is the process by which gender equality can be achieved through the integration of a gender perspective into key processes, policies and frameworks that usually adopt a gender-neutral stance, and by creating the necessary provisions and relevant supporting documents to facilitate the removal of gender inequalities. Within the context of the IUS programme, this entails ensuring the services and financing models employed by the programme are gender-responsive and provide benefits to marginalised groups as it pertains to the requirements of the GCF and CDB.

For the GCF, its updated 2020 Gender Policy (GP) seeks to ensure the institution and its network of partners adopt a comprehensive gender approach to support climate change interventions and innovations, advance gender equality and minimise social, gender and climate related risks through the promotion of climate investments and support the closing of the gender gap through mainstreaming gender equality in climate investments.

Applying the GCF GP to the IUS Programme would involve:

- Addressing and reducing gender inequality resulting in sustainable livelihoods opportunities, improved health and wellbeing, and increased resilience to climate-induced shocks and risks for men and women.
- Creation of an enabling environment among programme stakeholders founded on existing policy commitments wherein social and environmental standards and grievance redress mechanisms are supported and sustained in order to achieve gender equality.
- Support the comprehensive and systematic collection of sex-disaggregated data and execute qualitative and quantitative gender analysis to monitor, report and evaluate project impact.

GCF’s 2018 Indigenous Peoples’ Policy seeks to guide the institution’s engagement with IP in climate change policies and action while acknowledging IP as a distinct stakeholder group that needs to be involved. The policy is guided by the following principles:

- Develop and implement free, prior and informed consent.
- Respect and enhance the rights of IP to their lands, territories and resources.
- Recognize key international human rights and principles.
- Respect the right of IP under voluntary isolation.
- Respect and recognize traditional knowledge and livelihood systems.
- Enhance the capacity for IP issues within GCF.
- Facilitate access to GCF resources for IP.
- Respecting the system of self-governance.

In the context of the IUS Programme and its GAP, the IP policy would apply to the following areas:

- Programme actors properly seek free, prior and informed consent when engaging with IP throughout the programme’s lifespan, and
- Ensure that adverse impacts are avoided or minimized and compensate for residual impacts.

The CDB’s Gender Equality Policy and Operational Strategy (GEPOS: 2019) outlines CDB’s commitment to advancing gender equality through recognising the equal rights, responsibilities and opportunities of women, men, girls and boys which include the equal power to shape their own lives and contribute to society. The policy seeks to achieve gender equality in BMCs based on the following action pillars:

- Equitable access to infrastructure services for all,
- Economic empowerment of all,
- Education and training for all,
- Eliminating gender-based violence for all, and
- Resilience for all.

The guiding principles of the GEPOS that are applicable in the IUS programme include:

- Advocacy, dialogue and partnership,
- Intersectionality, participatory and inclusive perspectives and approaches,
- Institutional strengthening in BMCs,
- Enhancing leadership for and increasing compliance with gender equality, and
- Results monitoring.
