# Institutional Framework and Gender Assessment

An institutional framework for coordinating and ensuring oversight of gender equality and women’s empowerment is in place, including the Ministry of Gender and Family Promotion, a gender monitoring office, and the National Women’s Council. All ministries and public institutions have gender focal points. However, there is a divergence between the frameworks in place and implementation on the ground due to challenges such as lack of financial resources and technical capacity.

The gender assessment is based on a review of available secondary sources and discusses the context of gender issues in the agriculture sector, including key informant interviews and focus group discussions. Women’s economic autonomy is severely restricted by cultural norms. The gender assessment also highlights the prevalence of gender-based violence and existing mechanisms to address it.

A large proportion of Rwandans work in subsistence food production. Women are involved in farming and contribute to the agricultural value chain despite having less control over productive assets. Women’s participation in agricultural markets and access to services is limited compared to men due to cultural norms.

Biomass fuel is the predominant source of energy for cooking, and women spend significant time collecting it, limiting their engagement in productive activities. Almost a third of households in rural areas are headed by women, who are poorer and more vulnerable.

The gender action plan includes activities aligned to the project’s outputs, performance indicators, sex-disaggregated targets, timelines, and responsible institutions. A gender specialist in the project management unit will guide the implementation of the gender action plan.

The gender action plan strengthens the gender sensitivity of the project to enable both men and women to access benefits, such as improved cookstoves and establishment of enterprises to improve resilience to climate change effects.

In addition to supporting the establishment of enterprises, the AE is recommended to facilitate skills development activities that support women to move from low-value subsistence crop production. Rapid social analysis and in-depth consultations will be undertaken to update the gender action plan.

## 3.2. The Poverty Situation of Women in Rwanda and Social Protection Measures

### 3.2.1 Brief Analysis of the Poverty Situation of Women

Rwanda has achieved impressive sustained economic growth since the 1990s, with considerable reduction in poverty and important gains in health, education, and other development outcomes. Income inequality statistics have decreased in recent years. The government is committed to gender equality, women empowerment, and promoting women's rights. Rwanda’s legal and policy framework provides a strong basis for promoting gender equality and the empowerment of women. The 2003 Constitution mandates gender equality, and it is mainstreamed in all government policies. Rwanda is the first country to achieve the target of 50% of parliamentarians being women.

Despite these efforts, female-headed households are more likely to be poor than male-headed households. Women are significantly less likely than men to be in decent paid employment, operating mainly as dependent family workers, and working longer hours when domestic work is considered.

### 3.2.2 Poverty Reduction Strategy Through Social Protection Programmes

Extreme poverty among male and female households has dramatically reduced due to various poverty reduction initiatives, including Vision 2020 Umurenge and other social protection interventions. The social protection program aims to ensure that all poor and vulnerable people are guaranteed a minimum income and access to core public services.

### 3.2.3 Social Protection Measures Promoting Economic Activities of Women

Women play a crucial role in the agricultural economy, producing food for both family and market. Interventions should be gender-responsive to address gender issues and support women farmers effectively.

### Gender Disparities in Employment

In rural areas, both men and women are primarily employed in agriculture, with a higher percentage of women (82%) involved in skilled agriculture compared to men (63%). Ownership of land is vital for women's empowerment. Female-headed households owning farmland decreased from 89% in 2013/14 to 82% in 2016/17. The average land size cultivated by female heads remained constant at 0.5 ha. Livestock ownership is slightly higher among male-headed households (60.3%) compared to female-headed ones (57.3%).

### Participation in Markets

Despite policy efforts, women's participation in input and output markets is low. This limits their ability to handle agricultural products and access agribusiness opportunities. Women also have less decision-making power regarding agricultural income, which can demotivate them from engaging in cash crop production.

### Agricultural Employment

In 2014, agriculture employed 68% of the labor force, with women constituting 66% of this workforce. Men occupy more paid jobs in agriculture (25%) compared to women (19.7%).

### Crop Production and Land Consolidation

Land consolidation affected 29.6% of crop-producing households nationally, with higher impacts in Northern and Western Provinces. Male-headed households more often had plots protected from erosion or with irrigation.

### Cash Crops and Horticulture

Few Rwandans engage in cash crops due to limited land. Coffee and tea are highly commercialized, with most of the harvest sold.

### Input and Equipment Use

The purchase of improved seeds and chemical fertilizers varies by region, with a notable gap between male- and female-headed households. Male-headed households are more likely to purchase these inputs.

### Access to Loans

Access to loans is a significant barrier, especially for women. SACCOs are the main source of loans for women, while men primarily use commercial banks. In rural areas, SACCOs are more popular.

### Agricultural Extension

Agricultural extension services are crucial for professionalizing producers. However, there are few female extension officers, which can limit women's access to these services. Strategies are needed to increase female participation in extension roles.

### Farmer Promoters

Farmer promoters are community leaders trained to share agricultural best practices. However, women are underrepresented in these roles, with only 12-16% being female across different provinces.

### Challenges and Recommendations

Women face challenges in accessing agricultural services and opportunities due to limited information, social status, and competition for training and resources. Addressing these issues requires targeted strategies to improve women's representation and participation in agriculture.
