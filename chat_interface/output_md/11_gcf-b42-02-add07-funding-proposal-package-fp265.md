### B.1. Climate context

#### Context

Approximately 80% of the 4.16 million people living in northern Ghana are smallholder farmers depending on traditional, small-scale, rainfed agricultural systems to generate household incomes and maintain food security. Smallholder farming households in northern Ghana, comprising of Upper West Region (UWR), Upper East Region (UER), and North East Region (NER), frequently experience severe food insecurity for between three to six months annually during the dry season. They are highly vulnerable to the degradation of natural ecosystems and changes in climate. The main drivers of degradation are inter alia: i) deforestation for wood and charcoal production and clearing for agricultural activities; ii) wildfires to clear natural vegetation for agriculture; iii) overgrazing by cattle; and iv) soil erosion and fertility loss because of unsustainable farming practices.

The results of the climate change vulnerability assessment demonstrate that these administrative Regions of northern Ghana, which have high levels of poverty, are the most climate vulnerable in the country, and therefore, are a priority target for climate change adaptation interventions.

#### Climate analysis

##### Current trends

Most of the rainfall in northern Ghana occurs between June and October; this is also the period with the largest variation in total monthly precipitation volume. Historical trends show a substantial change in rainfall patterns with a reduction in the amount of rainfall during the early wet season and an increase during the late wet season. In northern Ghana, rainfall patterns have become increasingly erratic with the start of the wet season becoming more difficult to predict. The overall trend has been prolonged dry seasons, an increased frequency and intensity of drought conditions, and a shorter rainy season with increased occurrence of extreme rainfall events with severe consequences for smallholder farmers.

##### Climate change projections

- **Temperature:** Under RCP 8.5, the annual average temperature of Upper East Region is predicted to increase from 28.2°C to 33.1°C by 2085. Maximum and minimum temperatures are also expected to increase in all the regions of Ghana, with northern Ghana expected to experience the largest changes.
  
- **Potential evapotranspiration:** GCMs suggest that potential evapotranspiration (PET) will increase across Ghana throughout the 21st century under both RCP scenarios. This increase will be most apparent in northern Ghana where PET will increase by 8.5% by 2085.

- **Rainfall:** Ensemble climate projections suggest a reduction in early wet season rainfall and an increase in late wet season rainfall. The wet season will be shorter and rain will be more likely to occur less frequently but more intensely during high volume events.

- **Extreme events:** Extreme rainfall events are likely to become more frequent and intense over time, with an increase in flood risk and more frequent dry spells.

#### Climate change impacts

- **Impact on food systems:** Climate change is impacting dry season food production. Few smallholder farmers have access to irrigation infrastructure and are highly dependent on rain for their livelihoods. High temperatures contribute to reduced water-retention capacity and frequent drying of water bodies.

- **Impact on water resources:** Decreasing wet-season length, coupled with rising temperatures, is increasing the frequency in which surface water bodies such as dams and streams are drying up. Extreme rainfall events often result in severe flooding, destroying crops and damaging assets and public infrastructures.

- **Impact on local infrastructure and livelihoods:** Extreme climate events may undermine the effectiveness of existing rural infrastructure. According to EPA, 2007 floods in northern regions affected and displaced more than 300,000 people, damaged or destroyed almost 26,000 houses, and contaminated 634 drinking water facilities.

- **Impact on ecosystems:** Uncontrolled bushfires in northern Ghana are reducing the capacity of agroecosystems to provide critical ecosystem services. The frequency and intensity of uncontrolled bushfires is increasing, driven by rapid population growth and climate change.

### B.2 (a). Theory of change narrative and diagram

#### Problem statement

The agriculture sector in northern Ghana is dominated by smallholder farmers practising mostly subsistence farming. Given that smallholder farms are almost entirely rainfed, agricultural production is strongly dependent on the availability of natural resources, making them highly vulnerable to changes in their environment and the climate. The impacts of these changes to climate on smallholder farmers include increased frequency and severity of floods, increased frequency and intensity of droughts, shortened agricultural production periods, and more frequent and severe uncontrolled bushfires.

#### Preferred adaptation solution

The preferred adaptation solution would see a reduction in the vulnerability to climate change of smallholder farmers in northern Ghana through the implementation and adoption of climate-resilient agriculture, ecosystem-based adaptation approaches, alternative climate-resilient livelihoods, and locally led investments in climate resilient rural infrastructure. These interventions will complement one another to increase the resilience of smallholder farming communities to climate change impacts.

#### Project objective

The proposed project objective is to enhance the climate resilience of vulnerable smallholder farming communities in northern Ghana by improving food security and enhancing the agro-based rural economy.

#### Project outcomes

The project’s outcomes will strengthen institutional and regulatory systems for climate-responsive planning and development, enabling government agencies and local institutions to effectively integrate climate adaptation into policies and strategies. The project supports the adoption of climate-resilient agricultural practices, alternative livelihoods, and access to financial resources, ensuring farmers can sustainably adapt to climate impacts.

#### Barriers to the adaptation solution and project objective

Several barriers to the preferred adaptation solution have been identified for northern Ghana:

- **Barrier 1:** Limited institutional, technical and financial capacity for planning, budgeting, implementing and monitoring climate change adaptation interventions.
- **Barrier 2:** Limited knowledge and awareness of climate resilient methods and ecosystem linkages in disaster impact.
- **Barrier 3:** Inadequate access for communities to climate information and best practices for applying early warning data in agricultural practices.
- **Barrier 4:** Limited access for local governments and communities to financial resources to finance adaption interventions.

#### Project approach to overcoming the barriers to adaptation

The proposed project will overcome the abovementioned barriers through five interconnected outputs. Barrier 1 will be addressed by strengthening the technical and institutional capacity of regional and district government structures. Training on climate-resilient agricultural practices will be provided to extension officers and local communities, addressing Barrier 2. Barrier 3 will be addressed by the new data information and management system established under the project. The project will contribute to overcoming Barrier 4 by increasing the capacity of the most vulnerable smallholder farmers to form farmer-based organisations, strengthening financial management capacity, and facilitating linkages to agricultural credit and insurance.

Together, these interventions will overcome the barriers to climate change adaptation in northern Ghana, contributing to a paradigm shift within the agricultural sector towards climate-resilient sustainable development.

![Theory of change diagram](#)

The project will work at three levels: community level on planning and implementation systems, regional and national level regarding investments in weather forecast capability, and establishing a national action plan for drought and flood hazard management. Specific efforts will be made to support women farmers and female-led households who are particularly vulnerable to climate impacts.

The relevant outputs will be supported by the two executing entities (EEs), namely the Environment Protection Agency (EPA) of the Ministry of Environment, Science, Technology and Innovation (MESTI) and the Ghana Meteorological Agency (GMet) of the Ministry of Communication and Digitization.

### B.5. Justification for GCF funding request

Building the climate-resilience of the Ghanaian agricultural sector is vital to protect the livelihoods of smallholder farmers in northern Ghana. Shifting rainfall and temperature regimes are expected to reduce already suboptimal crop yields. Consequently, food security and income generation of the country’s most vulnerable people will be negatively impacted. These negative impacts will be compounded by — and further enhance — severe environmental degradation resulting from rapid population growth, dependence on natural resources and the use of unsustainable agricultural techniques. Without effective adaptation action, smallholder farmers will likely become increasingly vulnerable to the current and future effects of climate change, with serious repercussions for both the northern and Ghanaian economy. However, climate change adaptation in Ghana is hampered by a lack of financial resources, institutional and technical capacity needed to implement effective adaptation interventions.

Without investment into adaptation interventions, the livelihoods of ~10 million men and ~12 million women (~75% of the population) who are reliant on agriculture will remain at risk to the threats of climate change. However, the lack of financial resources hampers climate change adaptation in Ghana. The agricultural sector in Ghana is reliant on foreign funding, with ~65% of total public expenditure in support of food and agriculture funded by external sources. These include, among others, the World Bank, USAID and the European Union. The need for external funding is because of the current state of the Ghanaian economy. Despite a period of rapid economic growth between 2000 and 2013, graduating Ghana to lower-middle-income status, the country’s economy has seen a decline in growth over the past seven years. Increasing debt, high inflation and currency depreciation have contributed to the deterioration of the economy, leading to a current account deficit of -9.3% and a debt-to-GDP ratio of 58%. It is estimated that without interventions, the cumulative damages of weather events in four of the eight implementation districts will reach US$680.2 million by 2050 (elaborated in Annex 3: Economic and Financial Analysis). These damages will further constrain the GoG’s fiscal space for financing adaptation and resilience interventions. Additional challenges to Ghana’s economic outlook include high domestic financing costs, technical problems in the oil and gas sectors, energy problems related to state-owned enterprises and continued weak commodity prices. These factors have caused an unfavourable fiscal climate for the Ghanaian government, resulting in its inability to fund or secure private-sector loan financing for large-scale investments in innovative climate change adaptation measures. Therefore, given the current economic situation in Ghana, the contribution of funds by the GCF is critical for the successful implementation of the proposed project. Without investment into climate-resilient agriculture and EbA interventions, the livelihoods of the ~75% of the population of northern Ghana that are reliant on smallholder farming are at risk to the threats of climate change.

Funds are being requested by the GoG in the form of a grant without repayment contingency, given the focus of the project on the most vulnerable communities in Ghana. The grant funds will be used to provide the inputs necessary for project implementation while the EPA and MOFA will bear a large share of the project staffing costs. Grant finance is needed to fund the strengthening of technical and institutional capacity to develop, implement and coordinate climate change adaptation at regional and district level in northern Ghana because of inadequate financial resources. Although District Assemblies generate some revenues on their own (i.e. through district taxation), they barely manage to collect sufficient funds to cope with the many development priorities they face. This is especially so in northern Ghana where many of the inhabitants depend on subsistence rain-fed agriculture for their livelihood. The limited budgets are spent on priority projects, such as health, road construction and education with inadequate resources available for additional activities such as climate change adaptation. Given the current economic situation in Ghana, the contribution of funds by the GCF is critical for the successful implementation of the proposed project.

Given the focus of the project on the most vulnerable communities in Ghana, there is currently limited investment from the private sector in these approaches. Consequently, northern Ghana has limited access to the financial resources necessary for the adoption of appropriate climate-resilient practices to adapt to climate change. Smallholder farmers are unable to leverage private sector finance as financial institutions are unwilling to grant loans to them for agricultural activities because of the high-risk nature of rain-fed agriculture in the region. Instead, the banks employ joint liability lending structures and lend to registered farmer-based organisations (FBOs).

The sustainable development of northern Ghana’s agricultural sector requires substantial investment into adaptation practices such as climate-resilient agriculture and EbA to build the climate-resilience of vulnerable farming communities. The initial returns of such investments are largely at the individual level, with returns being re-invested by farmers into agricultural production and used to cover their basic needs, leaving limited scope for cost recovery for early investors. A GCF grant is, therefore, being sought by the GoG to invest in climate change adaptation and bring about a paradigm shift in the agro-based rural economy of northern Ghana. Through this project, GCF support will accelerate northern Ghana’s transition towards climate-resilient sustainable development, facilitating economic growth and securing the livelihoods of vulnerable smallholder farming communities.

The Government of Ghana has secured co-financing to the value of US$6,987,681 through in-kind contributions and inclusive of a cash co-financing of US$250,000. In-kind contributions consist of staff time, offices and equipment that will be provided by the EPA and Directorate of Crop Services to implement this project.

### B.6. Exit strategy

The project will work at three levels: community level on planning and implementation systems as well as at the Regional and National level regarding investments in weather forecast capability that will serve the Northern Sector, complementing investments in forecasting capability in the South of the country as well as establishing a national action plan for drought and flood hazard management. The community level integrated ecosystem-based adaptation approach will inform the District level planning and budgeting systems. The project will build capacity and awareness of District planning officers to scale up the project approach. Combined with associated community training, extension services and awareness-raising, this will increase opportunities for knowledge and technology exchange between communities, and thereby promote autonomous upscaling of these interventions.

Fundamental aspects of the project’s sustainability are listed below.

- The project will create capacity in District Authorities to replicate and maintain climate adaptation activities after the initial investment period, through the integration of climate change adaptation into regional and district medium-term development plans as well as through the consolidation of a country-based financing mechanism to channel climate finance at the local level.
- Beneficiary communities will receive extensive training on climate-resilient agricultural practices and the implementation of on-the-ground EbA interventions. In addition to building the adaptive capacity of these rural communities and allowing them to become self-sufficient under climate change conditions, an enabling environment will be created for the long-term maintenance and upscaling of proposed project activities. This will be further supported by the introduction of alternative climate-resilient livelihoods that add value to agricultural products, thereby further promoting sustainability. This will include improved storage, processing equipment and access to finance.
- A grassroots approach to building climate-resilience will ensure that activities are needs-based, locally appropriate and supported by local communities. An extensive community engagement process — including the development of Community Climate Action Plans — will be carried out. Throughout this process women and youth will be targeted, particularly through the introduction of alternative climate-resilient livelihoods, to ensure their participation and support for project interventions. This will promote the long-term commitment of community members after the project’s implementation period has elapsed.
- The project will increase the access of smallholder farmers to financial resources by establishing FBOs that can access credit and insurance for farming and non-farming livelihood activities. In addition, the project will provide smallholder farmers in the FBOs, training in business and financial management. This enterprise approach will ensure that communities are equipped with the financial resources, business knowledge and financial capacity required to sustain and upscale project interventions after the support phase. The project will develop bankable deals for de-risking finance for access by smallholder farmers through the Agri3 Fund currently being established as a partnership between UNEP, Rabobank and the Dutch Government.

An extensive knowledge-sharing programme will support the project’s M&E activities, which will allow non-beneficiary communities to learn from the experiences of nearby target communities. In addition, annual knowledge-sharing conferences will function as a platform for discussions on the lessons learned and sharing of new ideas and research on EbA to ensure the sustainability of interventions under future climate change conditions. Subsequently, this knowledge base will be used as a resource to leverage additional funds from public and private sector investors to maintain and replicate project interventions after the project’s funding period, while further strengthening cross-sectoral collaboration.

#### 2.1 Project Background

3. In northern Ghana, where approximately 80 per cent of the workforce depends on rain-fed, subsistence agriculture, smallholder farmers face major challenges due to climate variability. Prolonged dry seasons cause food shortages and water scarcity, while rising temperatures reduce water retention and increase pests and crop spoilage. At the same time, extreme rainfall leads to flooding, crop loss, infrastructure damage, and reduced reservoir capacity, heightening the risk of dam failure and downstream flooding.

4. In response, the project aims to build climate resilience among smallholder farmers in northern Ghana by improving food security and strengthening the rural economy. It focuses on climate-resilient agriculture and water storage, landscape restoration (e.g. riverbank rehabilitation, agroforestry), and climate-resilient rural infrastructure. Activities will be implemented at the community level (planning and capacity-building), regional level (weather forecasting), and national level (drought and flood action plan). Implemented over seven years in eight climate-vulnerable districts, the project will support 120 communities and provide early warning advisories to the wider population. Led by the Ghana Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), in partnership with the Ministry of Food and Agriculture, Ghana Meteorological Agency (GMet) and Water Resources Commission, the project will promote sustainable, climate-resilient livelihoods through an integrated, ecosystem-based approach.

5. The proposed funding for the project is USD 70,198,822, utilizing grant instruments. The GCF contribution to the project is USD 63,211,141 in grant. Co-financing will be provided by the Government of Ghana.

### Project Overview

The project aims to enhance the climate resilience of vulnerable smallholder farming communities in northern Ghana by improving food security and enhancing the agriculture-based economy. Specifically, the project will improve climate data and early warning systems in the Northern region of Ghana under component 1. Component 2 will focus on adoption of climate-resilient agriculture and water storage, and investments in EbA, nature-based flood-risk reduction, and climate-resilient agricultural practices, benefiting 15 communities from each of the 8 districts from 3 regions in northern Ghana. ## Efficiency and Effectiveness

**Scale: Medium**

The proposed project enhances climate resilience through Nature-Based Infrastructure (NBI) and Hybrid (combined nature-based and conventional) interventions. These include reforestation, climate-smart agriculture, ecosystem restoration, and improved water storage/irrigation systems. The project’s effectiveness is evaluated under three Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) to assess economic feasibility and resilience benefits.

### Impact of Climate Scenarios

Economic analysis demonstrates that NBI and Hybrid interventions significantly reduce climate risks. For example, in Lambussie, flood damages to agriculture under SSP3 decline from USD 67.2 million to USD 60.7 million by 2050, while generating additional income (USD 0.27 million from tree planting and USD 2 million from new agricultural practices). The interventions also mitigate costs from floods (buildings and crops) and droughts, underscoring their dual role in risk reduction and livelihood improvement.

### Economic and Financial Viability

**Benefit-Cost Ratios (BCR).** Discounted BCRs range from 1.55–2.95 across districts (Lambussie: 1.55–2.36; Lawra: 1.63–2.59; Jirapa: 1.65–2.62; WA West: 2.05–2.95), confirming strong economic viability, cost-effectiveness, better resource allocation, and resilience to uncertainties (e.g., climate shocks), therefore outweighs the costs.

**Net Present Value (NPV).** The NBI scenario yields NPVs of USD 22.4 million (under SSP1), USD 18.4 million (SSP3), and USD 23.5 million (SSP5). The Hybrid scenario performs even better, with NPVs of USD 68.8 million (SSP1), USD 58.8 million (SSP3), and USD 68.5 million (SSP5). The results demonstrate consistent positive returns confirming the projects' economic viability regardless of climate future. The Hybrid approach generates 2-3 times greater economic value than NBI alone, highlighting the benefits of integrated solutions. While SSP3 shows slightly reduced NPVs, the returns remain robust and demonstrate the interventions' effectiveness even under adverse conditions. The Hybrid scenario under SSP1 yields the highest NPV (USD 68.8M), suggesting optimal outcomes when sustainable development aligns with integrated infrastructure.

**Internal Rate of Return (IRR).** The IRR ranges overall between 17.4% and 37.9%; this range highlights the project’s potential to deliver robust financial and socio-economic benefits, especially in climate-vulnerable communities.

**Financing and Leverage.** With a GCF grant of USD 63.2 million, the Government of Ghana commits USD 6.99 million in co-financing (USD 250,000 cash; USD 6.74 million in-kind). The resulting leverage ratio of 0.11 reflects Ghana’s financing constraints while demonstrating stakeholder engagement.

The project is economically viable across all climate scenarios, with Hybrid solutions offering the highest returns. By integrating nature-based and conventional infrastructure, it not only mitigates climate impacts but also generates co-benefits for communities and ecosystems.

### Risk to Effectiveness and Efficiency

**Limitations of the Exit Strategy and the Need for Strengthened Financial Access and Institutional Support.** The project’s exit strategy presents a comprehensive framework for sustaining climate adaptation through capacity building, community engagement, financial inclusion, and knowledge sharing. However, several risks could undermine its long-term effectiveness if not proactively addressed. District Authorities are expected to lead future implementation but the risk of limited fiscal resources and competing local priorities may hinder the mainstreaming of climate actions into development planning. Similarly, training and alternative livelihoods will be introduced but their sustainability depends on access to markets and continuous financing. Community engagement among women and youth may vary in depth and consistency thus risking weak ownership of interventions. Strengthening access to finance both during and beyond the project period is essential to sustain and scale these interventions. The GAP is based on the proposed project’s logical framework and is focused on avoiding and minimising the negative impacts of the project’s activities on the local communities and maximising the positive impacts and social benefits, particularly on women, IPs, and marginalised groups.

## 2. Methodology

This GA primarily used qualitative data collection methods, including informant interviews with multiple stakeholders and partners (See Annex 7h: stakeholder engagement plan) as well as mixed community consultations in seven local communities across four districts, namely Jirapa, Lawra, East Mamprusi, and Yunyoo-Nasuan (Figure 1). The method involving open community meetings proved particularly effective as it allowed for the provision of information by individuals through open discussions, accompanied by immediate expressions of approval or disapproval and corroborated by testimonies or cases from other group members. This facilitated an accurate representation of the gender-related challenges faced by their respective communities. The GA has also made use of: i) a desktop review of available literature; ii) gender equality studies conducted by research institutions and donor agencies; and iii) details made available by the GOG.

To elicit informed and relevant responses from community members, a brief overview of the project’s components, objectives, and anticipated outcomes was shared with participants prior to engaging in discussions. The questions posed to the community members focused on the lived experiences of individuals and communities regarding the effects of climate change and their gendered responses to these challenges. This data collection method was preceded and supplemented by a review of existing literature on gender dynamics and equality in Ghana — particularly within the rural and agricultural sectors — to establish a framework aligned with the project’s objectives and anticipated impact. ## 3. The Northern Ghana Context

The three northern regions of Ghana — the Upper East, Upper West, and Northern regions — are threatened by harsh agro-climatic conditions, resulting in limited agricultural outputs per capita. Communities in these regions have limited livelihood options besides smallholder farming, with less service provision and urbanisation than in the south of the country. Approximately 70% of the ~4 million people living in northern Ghana depend on traditional, small-scale, rainfed agricultural systems to generate household incomes and maintain food security. These agricultural systems are vulnerable to changes in rainfall patterns and extreme climate events. Furthermore, a large proportion of northern Ghanaians rely on climate-dependent ecosystem goods produced in the agro-ecological landscapes in which their livelihoods are embedded. This reliance on climate-sensitive livelihoods makes rural communities in Ghana vulnerable to the effects of current and future climate change. Ghanaian women are considered particularly vulnerable to climate change because of their limited access to education, land resources, and credit. However, given that they account for approximately half the population, women play a substantial role in socio-economic development. Therefore, by building the capacity of women to adapt, the project will substantially improve the overall resilience of rural communities in northern Ghana.

This GA examines the gender situation in Ghana, focusing on women from rural communities within the Upper East, Upper West, and Northern regions. The objectives of the GA are to: i) identify relevant cultural and policy matters that contribute to gender inequality in Ghana; and ii) determine potential opportunities for addressing gender inequality through project interventions. Addressing gender inequality is necessary to addressing climate change, because of the substantial role women play in the adoption of climate change adaptation practices. Lessons learned from the implementation and evaluation of similar projects in the region have also been incorporated into the GA. The findings have been integrated into a GAP to ensure adequate consideration of gender issues in the design of project activities, targets, indicators, monitoring, and evaluation protocols.

### 3.1. Existing gender inequality in northern Ghana

Ghana has a long history of commitment to gender equality, dating back over 50 years to the establishment of the Affirmative Action Act — shortly after achieving independence in 1957. Gender equality is further embedded in Ghana’s constitution, which states that “all persons shall be equal before the law” and “a person shall not be discriminated against on the grounds of gender, race, colour, ethnic origin, religion, creed, social or economic status”. More recently, Ghana has highlighted the importance of empowering women and promoting gender equality as part of their commitment to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Despite these efforts to ensure gender equality, women in the northern regions of Ghana still face several gender-specific barriers limiting the capacity of communities to adapt to climate change.

The ability of men and women to adapt to climate change is dependent on their: i) involvement in policy development and decision-making; ii) integration into local committees that are focused on climate change adaptation; and iii) capacity to implement Ecosystem-based Adaptation (EbA) practices. Building the capacity of both men and women will lead to increased adaptive capacity at the household and community levels.

Barriers to achieving gender equality and building the adaptive capacity of women in Ghana include, inter alia: i) strong patriarchal family structures; ii) limited influence in decision making; iii) increased out-migration of men leaving women with an increased labour burden; iv) greater difficulty translating labour into secure incomes; and v) limited financial independence. Approximately 68% of women in rural Ghana are employed in the agricultural sector. However, less than 20% of northern households are headed by women and, therefore, the role of women in farming decision-making is limited. These challenges are compounded by a lack of policy frameworks directed at integrating social and gender equality in rural labour markets. This makes women particularly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. Addressing these barriers to gender equality would lead to numerous advantages to rural communities, building their resilience. For example, through stakeholder consultations (see Annex 7h: Stakeholder Engagement Plan) it was noted that if women are provided access to fertile land, improved technologies, and financial services, they are likely to invest in the land, leading to increased production — and potential increased income — which benefits not only themselves, but their families and communities too. Furthermore, women are most likely to invest in health and education which will improve the overall situation within households and communities.

The following sections detail the gender dynamics currently experienced in Ghana and their implications for building the adaptive capacity of women — and their communities — to the effects of climate change.

### 3.2. Poverty and employment

There is a considerable disparity in depth and severity of poverty across Ghana, with greater poverty rates in the northern than southern regions. In addition to lower economic activity and development, northern Ghana underperforms relative to the south in terms of social development. Child mortality is relatively high in the north, while data on indicators relating to education, sanitation, water, health, security, and governance suggest that the northern regions have experienced less development compared with the southern regions. In addition to the north-south development gap, there is a notable gap in poverty levels between rural and urban areas. Poverty levels in rural areas (38%) are far higher than those in urban areas (11%), with the gap growing over the past two decades. There is also a gendered pattern between poverty levels and employment in Ghana. Although the unemployment rates in Ghana are only marginally higher for women than men (~6% compared to ~5%), women’s employment is often concentrated in low-income activities, including unpaid family labour and small household farms. As a result, women’s control over their income is precarious and poverty rates among working women are generally higher than those of working men. This challenge is compounded by the gender wage gap of 0.75, with women earning 75 cents to the dollar compared with men. This ranks Ghana at 26th out of 144 countries in terms of the gender wage gap. However, in terms of overall economic participation and opportunity, Ghana ranks much higher at 30th place, with a gender gap index of 0.805.

### 3.3. Access to and control over resources

A major factor influencing the economic and social status of a group of people is their access to and control over assets. In rural communities such as those in northern Ghana, this often centres on the land tenure. Approximately 70% of the northern population of Ghana are reliant on small-scale subsistence farming for their livelihoods. Therefore, control over the natural resources and land-use practices strongly influences income potential, social status, and power. In Ghana, ~80% of the total land is controlled under a customary land tenure system, meaning that land is largely vested in male-centred kinship institutions and customary authorities. The customary authority then grants community members access to customary lands. In the patriarchal culture, which dominates most of northern Ghana, the land-use rights are passed through the male lineage. Women’s customary rights to access land are therefore restricted in favour of men’s, despite women’s rights to own and inherit land being protected under The Constitution. As of 2014, ~80% of women aged 15-49 did not own land. Furthermore, the social status of women and the stability of their marriage can influence their access to agricultural and natural resources from family-owned land, with divorcees and widows often facing discrimination when trying to access land.

### 3.4. Education

Access to education is fundamental to social and economic development. Ghana’s Education Sector Strategic Plan (ESP 2010–2020) aims to provide equitable access to quality basic education. Access to primary education is relatively high, with a 92% net enrolment ratio in 2016 and an equal gender ratio. Overall enrolment begins to decrease at the junior high school level — dropping to 50% — and continues to decrease at the tertiary level, with the progression to senior high school declining to only 25%. Gender disparity is, however, still great among secondary education, with a ratio of 0.98. The Northern region has the highest level of gender disparity at the senior high school level, with 42% female enrolment. It is only at the tertiary education level that gender disparity is notable, dropping to a ratio of 0.67, ranking 122nd out of 144 countries.

### 3.5. Political participation

Women’s political participation is considered a catalyst for gender equality and sustainable development in Ghana. Since achieving independence in 1957, Ghanaian women have been afforded equal rights to participate in political activities and decision-making at every level. Despite this, cultural structures and traditional practices involving male dominance and patriarchy still impede women’s participation in decision making. Cultural perceptions of women being inferior to men have long been used to rationalise the differential roles between men and women in society. Consequently, women are underrepresented across all government structures. Currently, only 14.5% of parliamentary seats are held by women, with less than 30% of those holding ministerial or chief executive roles. Ghana, therefore, ranks 95th out of 144 countries in terms of political empowerment, with a parity score of 0.112. However, despite women’s political representation following a positive trajectory — increasing by 0.4 over the past decade — women remain underrepresented at the highest levels of political participation.

As part of their commitment to the Sustainable Development Goals Agenda 2030, Ghanaian gender advocates have set forward a gender checklist to measure their progress towards achieving gender equality in decision-making. The checklist sets targets for women’s representation across various sectors, including inter alia: i) 60% of parliamentary seats; ii) 60% of ministerial portfolios; iii) 60% of metropolitan, municipal and district Chief Executive posts; iv) 50% of vice chancellorships and university professorships; and v) 60% of state corporations Chief Executive Officer positions. The Minister of Gender, Children and Social Protection has officially called on all state institutions and non-governmental organisations to support Agenda 2030.

### 3.6. Gender and climate change

The gender inequalities described above disproportionately affect the adaptive capacity of women to current and future climate change, as opposed to men. The effects of climate change on households and communities will be exacerbated if the adaptive capacity of both men and women are not strengthened in parallel. Overlooking either gender will affect production and income within a household, having knock-on effects on families, children, and the community. Although traditional knowledge has established coping mechanisms within communities to the harsh climatic conditions experienced in northern Ghana, it is unlikely to adapt quickly enough to overcome the effects of future climate change.

Women — along with children and the elderly — are among the most vulnerable groups to the effects of climate change. This is largely because women and female-headed households have fewer resources to cope with reductions in food and water security and the effects of extreme weather events such as droughts and floods. This is particularly pertinent in rural communities, where people are often reliant on climate-sensitive natural resources for their livelihoods. Several factors contribute to the vulnerability of women to climate change including, inter alia, the effects of: i) migration; ii) water availability; iii) decreased agricultural yields; and iv) reliance on climate-sensitive livelihoods. These are described in more detail below.

- When climatic conditions become less favourable for agricultural practices, men often migrate out of rural areas in search of work in other sectors. Women are left behind to not only continue with their domestic duties but also to continue the agricultural work on their land. Much of this agricultural work comes in the form of subsistence farming, with little or no pay, leaving women without the financial resources to adapt to climate change.
- Women have limited rights to own or control land. Without ownership of the land they are working on, women are unable to make the necessary decisions or access the necessary funds to improve agricultural practices, leading to reduced agricultural productivity and food security. This affects not only the women themselves but also their households and potentially the wider community.
- Water scarcity associated with shifting rainfall patterns and drought can have a substantial impact on women, most of whom are responsible for the collection of water for household uses such as drinking, cooking, and cleaning. When water security is threatened, extra effort is required for women to collect water — with women having to travel further to collect water. Similar pressures are observed with regards to the collection of fuelwood which will become scarce as climate conditions worsen. This compounds the additional workload placed on women maintaining both domestic duties and agricultural responsibilities, with little capacity to adapt to growing climatic pressures.
- Many rural Ghanaian women are reliant on natural resource-based livelihoods that are climate-sensitive. As the availability of natural resources becomes threatened by climate change, so do income generation and food security.

## Intersection of Vulnerabilities

The intersection of vulnerabilities exacerbates gender inequality in Ghana. Rural women with disabilities face barriers such as limited mobility and stigma, restricting economic participation. Young women in informal labor often have lower financial literacy, leading to underpayment. Gender norms and household hierarchy may limit their participation in training. These overlapping forms of marginalization restrict access to land, finance, and decision-making opportunities. Climate change further strains their ability to maintain food security and support household well-being.

## Gender-based Violence (GBV)

Rape and domestic violence are widespread in Ghana, particularly when married women are suspected of infidelity. In 2016, 27% of Ghanaian women experienced domestic violence. Cultural beliefs often treat domestic violence as a private matter, hindering justice. Magistrates may block legal proceedings, advocating for family unity. Reporting sexual abuse is also challenging due to fees for necessary documentation. Instances of GBV or SEAH during project implementation will be reported through DOVVSU as detailed in the project’s GRM.

## Situational Analysis — Northern Ghana Target Areas

### Gender and Agriculture

Gender dynamics in agriculture show distinct labor divisions. In the Upper West Region, men handle land preparation, while women focus on planting and selling produce. In the Ping community, men lead in major crop cultivation, while women engage in livestock farming and petty trading. Challenges include limited land ownership for women, addressed by initiatives like the Women in Agriculture Programme.

### Alternative Livelihoods

Alternative livelihoods are crucial in northern Ghana. In the Bogni community, dry season farming faces challenges, leading to migration for income. Women engage in beekeeping and brewing as alternative livelihoods. In Sumniboma 1, shea butter production and livestock rearing support incomes, despite barriers like limited capital.

### Access to and Control Over Land

Access to land varies across communities. Initiatives like FBOs help women gain control over agricultural activities, though challenges remain. In some communities, women have access to land but lack formal ownership due to customary land tenure systems.

### Access to Finance

VSLAs are common in northern Ghana, enabling savings and investments. In Tuggoh, VSLAs support farming inputs and household expenses. In Ping, VSLAs cater to different demographics, fostering social unity. These mechanisms empower individuals, particularly women, to manage finances and contribute to economic resilience.
