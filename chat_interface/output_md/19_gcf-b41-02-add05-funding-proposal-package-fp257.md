# B. PROJECT/PROGRAMME INFORMATION

## B.1. Climate context

14. Sub-Saharan Africa stands at a crossroads with an unprecedented opportunity and challenges for agricultural transformation, driven by the demands of a rapidly growing population of 1.5 billion and the pressures of a changing climate. The vicious cycle of poverty and environmental degradation in Africa is evident in low crop productivity, deforestation, land degradation, conflict, migration, and vulnerability to climate shocks, which perpetuate persistent food insecurity and poverty. The effects of climate change are expected to be severe in Africa, where the capacity to adapt and respond to a changing climate is weak—exacerbating the continent’s food insecurity challenge.

15. A great deal of attention has been paid to the impacts of climate change on crop cultivation, with extensive research on how climate variability affects crop yields, and management strategies to address these challenges. However, food security is influenced not only by changes in crop production but also by the entire crop value chain, including post-harvest stages. There is, therefore, a crucial need to consider how climate change impacts the full agriculture value chain, including production, aggregation, storage, processing, and distribution.

### Harvesting and Post Harvesting Value Chains

16. The RE-GAIN programme will support the adaptation of the harvest and post-harvest management innovations in seven African countries — Burkina Faso, Ethiopia, Kenya, Malawi, Tanzania, Uganda, and Zambia; strengthening food security by addressing the climate change impacts on post-harvest food losses. For the purpose of the RE-GAIN Programme proposal, the post-harvest value chain is considered to start at the point of harvest, and spans across three key stages.

#### Climate Risks for Post-Harvest Value Chains

17. Climate change-driven (or exacerbated) food spoilage and contamination in post-production stages of the value chains compound the climate change-driven productivity and yield losses during cultivation. This results in a double-blow to farming communities from climatic factors such as rising temperatures, higher moisture levels (warmer air holds more moisture), increased precipitation, more frequent or intense heavy rainfall events, flooding.

18. Climate change is thus a threat-multiplier to crop value chains. Climate change impacts in the post-production value chain, which result in substantial post-harvest losses and reduce the output that farmers have left to sell, depress incomes and jeopardize livelihoods of small-holder farmers (IFPRI, 2022). This perpetuates vulnerability to climatic shocks and stresses and prolongs the cycle of persistent poverty.

19. In the face of climate change-driven post-harvest losses, farmers have an impetus to keep less of the reduced output for their domestic consumption and to offset the volume losses (during harvesting, field-drying, on-farm storage) by selling more of what remains in the market. This worsens household food insecurity in countries that already have markedly high levels of undernourishment.

### Climate change impact on food loss

20. In all seven countries, historic trends in temperature (such as annual average/mean surface temperature, and the number of days with extreme heat – i.e., temperatures over 35°C) have displayed an increasing trend over time, with the rate of increase accelerating in recent decades. Historic rainfall trends display far greater variability, and for some countries a measure of uncertainty regarding the direction of the trend.

21. By and large, future projections of rainfall (such as annual average/mean precipitation, and the number of consecutive wet days, and – as a corollary – the number of consecutive dry days) suggest a projected rise in rainfall levels and rainfall days across most of East Africa, with the exception of Zambia (where projections display a slight drying signal, consistent with regional trends for Southern Africa).

24. All seven RE-GAIN countries are recognized as having low adaptive capacity and low readiness for climate change impacts, in terms of their relative global standing. Stakeholder inputs across all 14 crops in the seven countries points to consistent, common factors affecting adaptive capacity in all fourteen value chains (of eight different crops). This consistency is both understandable (given common or similar barriers in all seven countries’ agricultural value chains) and valuable (as it offers opportunity for RE-GAIN’s interventions).

## Component 1: Food Loss-Reduction Solutions (FL-RS) Demand Side Development to Increase the Adoption of FL-RS by Farmers

### Outcome 1: Increased Adoption of FL-RS by Smallholder Farmers

The first project component focuses on enhancing demand for, and adoption of, FL-RS in the target communities. It is this adoption of FL-RS that will ultimately reduce food losses and result in the core benefits of the programme for smallholder farmers – namely improved food security, increased resilience to climate shocks, and reduced GHG emissions. The approach for achieving this Outcome is grounded to two programme Outputs that will directly support the farmers through awareness, training, technology transfer and market linkages. However, given the integrated nature of the project approach, achieving the full impact of the Outcome will also rely on the supply-side interventions under Outcome 2, and the strengthening of the enabling environment under Outcome 3.

#### Output 1.1: Smallholder Farmers Supported to Adopt FL-RS

At the heart of the demand-side development is the direct support provided to smallholder farmers to not only know what FL-RS options are available, but also to understand the need for adopting such options – particularly when considering the impacts of a changing climate – as well as how to optimise the use of FL-RS. Activities under this project will involve technical support targeting several core themes:

- Raising awareness among farmers of the impacts of climate change on food loss and the availability of improved, climate-resilient FL-RS options;
- Training farmers on the use of improved FL-RS, as well as adapting existing post-harvest food loss-reduction technologies to climate change;
- Training farmers on best practices for complementary crop management (including use of weather/climate information) to maximise the efficiency of FL-RS;
- Actively demonstrating the effectiveness of FL-RS systems in local contexts; and
- Supporting the transfer of technologies and boosting immediate availability of FL-RS solutions in target communities.

Training activities will cover three key stakeholder groups, namely: i) smallholder farmers; ii) extension workers and agro-dealers; and iii) agricultural traders and processors.

The activities under Output 1.1 will focus specifically on short-listed FL-RS interventions, tailoring the approach to each community’s individual context. AGRA will train and engage VBAs (Village Based Advisors), who are community-based, and are usually young people who are lead farmers, unemployed youth and/or entrepreneurial farmers to provide extension services and agribusiness linkages. This extension model has been well developed and is operational in these target countries. They will play an important role in training smallholder farmers on FL-RS solutions. An evaluation of proposed physical FL-RS was conducted across all seven target RE-GAIN countries to identify those with the highest potential to reduce harvest and postharvest food losses and protect the harvests against growing impacts from climate hazards. The output of this assessment established a shortlist of FL-RS options tailored to each country and crop, as presented in Section 5 of Annex 2 – Feasibility Study. These solutions include: Communal storage structures; Warehouses; Harvesting machinery; Hermetic bags; Mechanical multi-crop threshers and shellers; Metal and plastic silos; Moisture meters; Storage protectants and control agents; Storage structures; and; Tarpaulins and plastic sheets. Further details on the nature of interventions per country are available in the Appendixes to Annex 2.

#### Activity 1.1.1: Conduct Awareness-Raising Activities Targeting Smallholder Farming Communities

The limited awareness on post-harvest food loss management at both the value chain and policy level, combined with inadequate communication on the importance of post-harvest food loss reduction has led to the limited adoption of available FL-RS as well as inadequate integration of food loss reduction strategies into relevant policies and strategies by governments in Africa. This has, in turn, resulted in inadequate investment in solutions that reduce food losses. Approximately 5,350 VBA will be in raising awareness, each targeting 200 to 250 farmers, resulting in a total reach of ~1,110,000 people trained.

To establish a foundation for increasing demand for FL-RS, awareness and capacity building focused actions and activities will be undertaken in each of the target communities across the seven countries, using local institutions, chiefdoms and community networks to increase the understanding of postharvest food losses among farmers, local stakeholders in the agriculture value chain, and the public. The awareness raising will target several key topics, as outlined below.

- RE-GAIN programme and its objectives to reduce food losses and for climate change adaptation.
- Major drivers and causes of harvest and postharvest loss in the target locations, with emphasis on climate-food loss nexus.
- Impact of postharvest losses on food security, income, economy, and the environment (incl. climate change) and the importance to reduce FL.
- Available best practices and improved technologies and methods (e.g., timing of harvesting, methods and technologies for harvesting, storage, etc.) to reduce harvest and postharvest losses and their benefits (food security, income environment).
- Role of different actors (local government, extension services, farmer organisations, agro-dealers, financial institutions) to provide access for FL-RS across scales.
- Cross-cutting themes: climate change awareness, climate smart agriculture, farm management, marketing, product quality management, access to finance, gender and youths, etc.

The nature of awareness raising activities will be tailored to agricultural context of each country, accounting for the target crops, the availability of local information dissemination systems (including mass media such as radio, television, digital platforms and social media, as well as brochures, flyers and local extension services), cultural considerations and local languages. The campaign will collaborate with local governments and farmer organisations, build on existing outreach systems to maximise reach and participation. AGRA will closely monitor the awareness campaigns, assessing impacts and adapting outreach strategies as needed.

#### Activity 1.1.2: Train Smallholder Farmers on the Use of Improved Climate-Resilient FL-RS

Training will be provided to develop the capacities of smallholder farmers on improved crop management and storage techniques to reduce food losses and to maintain quality of produce, increase farmers' income by reducing losses and improving marketability, and improve supply of financial services and FL-RS to smallholders and other value chain actors. AGRA will procure the services of suitable local entities (for example, NGOs, VBAs) to facilitate training in target communities. Training will then be provided through various channels — such as onsite demonstration of best practices, agricultural extension services, lead farmers and Village-Based Advisors (VBAs), farmer cooperatives, industry associations, and digital platforms — depending on the local context and the existing networks of the local service provider. Approximately 5,350 VBA will be engaged for training, each targeting 200 to 250 farmers, resulting in a total reach of ~1,100,000 people trained.

The nature and content of the training will be tailored to each group and demand as outlined below. Training will include baseline topics, including an introduction to the RE-GAIN programme and the core concepts of food security, post-harvest food losses and the impact of climate change – building on and leveraging material from the awareness campaign (Activity 1.1.1). The sequencing of the training will also, where feasible, follow Activity 1.1.5 to enable integration of the equipment demonstrations (Activity 1.1.6) into the training of farmers. In addition to this baseline, technical training will be provided on specific subject matter tailored to the local context of each community. A list of potential training topics has been identified, which will be refined for the local needs in each target community during the first year of implementation, once the communities are identified. This list includes:

- Major triggers and incidences of food loss and the impact they have on food security and health.
- Identification of the optimal timing of harvesting, thrashing and transport.
- Methods to reduce contamination of produce by mycotoxins and other diseases at harvest and storage.
- Use of available weather forecast data.
- Key reasons of food losses during harvesting and postharvest management and storage.
- Major impacts of climate change on agriculture, with focus on food loss.
- Technical approaches on maintaining crop quality during harvesting, postharvest handling and storage.
- Approaches and solutions to prevent pest attacks, and proper storage methods.
- Types of storage facilities and their advantages and disadvantages.
- An overview of FL-RS options and suppliers.
- Approaches to measuring and keeping optimal moisture content in crops to prevent aflatoxin contamination.
- Best harvesting methods and tools, including mechanization to reduce food losses for various target crops.
- Proper use and maintenance of physical FL-RS, including operation and maintenance of machinery, and their environmental and safety aspects.
- Record-keeping, financial literacy and access to finance.
- Benefits of and processed for farmer aggregation.
- Packaging and marketing of crops.
- Methods and materials for proper on-farm storage, safe and proper use of pesticides and fungicides, pre-storage crop treatment and preparations, and monitoring storage losses and quality of crops during storage.

All training will be prepared with specific attention to gender responsiveness and opportunities for youth development, as well as environmental considerations for proposed solutions. The Gender and Inclusion Action Plan (Annex 8) stipulates how this will be achieved, with a strong focus on ensuring the VBA / Farmer Based Organisation (FBO) training content is gender-responsive and youth-oriented, and that these VBAs / FBOs are suitably trained on engaging specifically with women and youth. In addition to this targeted engagement, the Gender and Inclusion Action Plan also specifies that all aspects of the Programme’s awareness raising and capacity building initiatives must include dedicated modules / components focussed on gender and youth inclusion and empowerment. These will seek to tackle issues of acceptance / sensitization of male counterparts by focussing on the benefits of working with / supporting women and youth-led initiatives. This approach extends to the training activities outlined below, which will ensure agricultural traders, agro-dealers and extension workers are equally exposed to this content.

#### Activity 1.1.3: Train Agricultural Traders and Processors on Complementary Services for FL-RS

Building on the training of farmers, the programme will provide additional training to agricultural traders (agro-dealers, aggregators etc) and processors on complementary services that support and maximise the effectiveness of FL-RS. **Interventions at a smallholder farmer level**

Model 1 promotes the local supply of FL-RS interventions by using conditional procurements to effectively subsidise interventions at smallholder farmer level (referred to hereafter as ‘smart-subsidies’). At its base, the smart-subsidy model enables agro-dealers to provide FL-RS to smallholder farmers at a discounted rate by using GCF funds to procure one item for every two items procured and sold by an agro-dealer. The subsidy is passed down to the smallholder farmers as a discount on the purchase price.

- to stimulate production and manufacturer capacity by placing pre-emptive orders of FL-RS, however, maintaining control over risk through a conditional release of funds to the manufacturer; and
- to reduce the cost of interventions at smallholder farmer level, additional profitability will drive additional demand and facilitate knowledge sharing about the benefits of interventions.

## Barriers to Public or Private Finance Alternatives

157. Low productivity, aggravated by harvest and postharvest loss, has been the major driver of climate vulnerability as farming is expanding to fragile ecosystems, wetlands, and mountains, causing deforestation, land degradation, and drying of water resources, exposing farmers to extreme events.

158. In response to these climate change threats on food security, the RE-GAIN programme aims to enhance the climate resilience and adaptive capacity of smallholders by promoting the widespread adoption of FL-RS in seven African countries. AGRA, as the lead agency, is requesting GCF grant funds to support the roll out of a suite of physical and management interventions alongside capacity building and enhanced financial and market access.

159. Experiences to date show that countries rarely invest in climate change adaptation, less so in reducing food loss. This is largely due to the constrained fiscal space in the public sector, which limits opportunity to invest in catalytic programmes such as RE-GAIN using public funds.

160. Looking to the private sector as an alternative, it is highly challenging to secure finance from conventional sources in the market for such resilience-building interventions unless market demand is created and appropriate market linkages are put in place.

161. Based on the stakeholder mapping AGRA conducted during the country strategy development, existing interventions and support focus mainly on last mile delivery, with few actors willing or able to invest in unlocking the systemic challenges that would facilitate agriculture and food systems transformation.

162. Building the necessary capacity to develop and implement effective adaptation plans and investments requires substantial investments in education, training, and institutional strengthening, which, although catalytic, are generally considered public goods with limited direct return.

## Consideration of Alternative Funding Models

163. In the process of designing the RE-GAIN programme, numerous other alternative finance models were considered. While each option has some merit, each faces considerable barriers that prevent these models from working on their own.

- Farmers pay cash for the hermetic technologies and for using the FL-RS services if they have savings and or alternative sources of income.
- Farmers can get the FL-RS on credit from the supplier of the technology (agro-dealers) and/or service providers if these MSMEs have the liquidity to give credit to their customers.
- Service providers offering FL-RS could get equipment financing through wealthy individuals, family and friends or through financial institutions or equipment vendors.
- While credit options are possible, borrowers would need to offset the risk for lenders through co-contributions as well as the provision of some form of guarantee or collateral.
- Extension services could be paid for with Government budget. However, most Governments are constrained in their budgets for extension services.

### Environmental Co-benefits

Programme interventions to reduce post-harvest food loss will have several environmental co-benefits.

- **Reduced land degradation and improved water use efficiency:** Reducing post-harvest food loss will increase the net production of agricultural land, thereby reducing the area of land and amount of water needed to produce sufficient food to meet demand. ## D.6. Efficiency and Effectiveness

### Financial Structure – Adequacy, Reasonableness, and Return on Investment

Affordability, access to finance, market access, financial literacy, and consumer awareness are key challenges for smallholder farmers seeking to adopt practices that reduce food loss in the agricultural value chain. Key to viably addressing these challenges is RE-GAIN's programmatic approach that combines demand-side, supply-side, and enabling environment components.

The combination of both financial and non-financial approaches effectively ensures market failures resulting from both the supplier and consumers side of the agricultural value chain are addressed.

The two key financial models—1) catalytic grants for smallholder farmers and 2) catalytic grants for agricultural MSMEs—use concessional funding to incentivize private sector manufacturers and financial institutions (FIs) to:

- Provide physical solutions that are within the affordability envelope of farmers while remaining commercially viable to private sector suppliers.
- Provide tailored financial products that are affordable to farmers, while mitigating credit risk to financial institutions.

By focusing on a 5-year period, the program aims to demonstrate long-term market viability to suppliers (both physical and financial), build the necessary credit history required to access financial products, and encourage adoption of physical solutions by farmers.

Non-financial interventions drive adoption of these solutions from farmers by focusing on building capacity and awareness through training, educational materials, and village-based agent (VBA) demonstrations, addressing information gaps and promoting financial literacy. These interventions are critical to creating linkages between private sector manufacturers, MSMEs, and Small hold farmers.

This combined approach of addressing both supply and demand side constraints is expected to mobilize US$ 144.95 million worth of physical solutions and reach 553,900 smallholder farmers households over the next 5 years. Given the programme cost of US$ 105 million this represents a catalytic effect or return of 1.38 times the original investment from the GCF. The financial return on investment exceeds 1 time the original investment across each of the seven countries. This return excludes the long-term impact of the programme’s ability to demonstrate the market viability of these physical solutions to both users (farmers) and suppliers (manufactures and FIs), which will catalyse new private sector markets while addressing food-loss and associated climate-related challenges.

### Economic Efficiency and Effectiveness of the Proposed Project/Programme and Return

The proposed programme is efficient and effective, with a total investment of US$ 105 million expected to generate significant climate adaptation outcomes. By reducing post-harvest food losses, the programme contributes directly to increased food availability, with co-benefits of reduced greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions; projected reduction of around 0.1 gigatonne of CO₂-equivalent emissions. This mitigation co-benefit is coupled with economic and social benefits such as enhanced food security, improved household incomes, and better health outcomes through reduced aflatoxin exposure, improved food safety, and availability.

An economic analysis for the programme was undertaken using a cost-benefit analysis approach. Programme costs and benefits were assessed over a 10-year period and using country-specific social discount rates as presented in Annex 3. The analysis weighed up the total programme costs against the benefits associated with the uptake of equipment by smallholder farming households. These benefits included the income gains associated with the value of food that would otherwise have been lost in the absence of the programme interventions, the health benefits from reductions in aflatoxin exposure, the improved nutritional status (particularly of infants) from avoided food losses, and the climate benefits associated with reduced greenhouse gas emissions from food production. The results of the economic analysis suggest that, under the current set of assumptions, the total package of interventions is cost-beneficial, with the total programme investment generating an economic Net Present Value (ENPV) of US$ 69 million, with a benefit to cost ratio (BCR) of 8:1 and an Economic Internal Rate of Return (EIRR) of around 56%. On this basis, there is a clear economic case for implementing the proposed programme. Additional analyses were conducted to test the sensitivity of the findings to changes in the underlying assumptions, including around the choice of discount rate, the shadow price of carbon, and the proportion of existing food production for which the quality (and safety) improves as a result of programme interventions. The results of these analyses are presented in Annex 3 and show that, at a programme level, the economic summary statistics are robust to changes in the input values.

At a country level, both the ENPV and BCR is positive across all seven countries. The highest net benefits are experienced in Burkina Faso and Ethiopia and can be explained, at least in part, by the choice of crops used to illustrate the impacts of interventions, and which have comparatively higher prices; the relatively high prevalence of aflatoxin-related illnesses in Burkina Faso.

# G. GCF POLICIES AND STANDARDS

## G.1. GCF Policies and social risk assessment

### Risk Categorisation
The programme is assigned a Category B risk rating as per the GCF Revised Environmental and Social (E&S) Policy definition. The rationale for the risk rating is as follows:
- The Programme will target established smallholder farms and MSMEs and as such will not be associated with any greenfield development or land acquisition.
- The Programme is designed to facilitate the adoption of FL-RS solutions by smallholder farmers and MSMEs through existing supply chains in the countries. As such, the Programme will not directly be involved in the manufacturing, transportation and/or installation of the FL-RS solutions.
- The E&S risks associated with the Programme are mainly associated with the regions targeted by the Programme in each in-scope country, the contextual circumstances of the existing smallholder farms targeted to participate in the interventions and the in-country partner(s) chosen to facilitate the interventions.
- The risk assessment conducted on the Programme highlighted limited adverse E&S risks and impacts that are few and can be readily addressed through actionable mitigation measures.

### Programme E&S Risk Assessment
AGRA conducted two desktop assessments, namely a country and region contextual E&S risk analysis for each in-scope country and focus crops and an intervention level E&S risk assessment taking into account the envisaged Programme activities. The key E&S risks stemming from these assessments are summarised as follows:

#### Overall Programme E&S Risks
- AGRA procures the services of local NGOs and/or private sector actors to support the building of the intervention ecosystem. Reputational risks may exist associated with the Programme partners chosen due to poor E&S performance and/or reputation.
- AGRA will preferentially identify and partner with locally available manufactures of FL-RS technologies for inclusion into the Programme. Reputational risks associated with manufactures due to E&S related risk factors such as:
  - Sub-standard labour and working conditions including potential for poor occupational health and safety practices.
  - Limited pollution control measures and waste management.
  - Road transport health and safety risks associated with transporters of FL-RS technologies to agro-dealers.
- AGRA will partner with locally available financial institutions to provide financing at preferential rates to either manufacturers or smallholder farmers / MSMEs. Reputational risks associated with financial institutions due to poor and/or unethical lending processes which may lead to smallholder farmer indebtedness.

#### Contextual E&S Risks
- Conflict areas were identified in certain regions in Burkina Faso and Ethiopia where the Programme is intended to be implemented may lead to challenges in meeting Programme objectives including potential health, safety and security risks to Programme personnel.
- Depending on the country, maturity and oversight of labour regulations, reputational risks associated with labour and working conditions may exist on the target smallholder farms and/or MSMEs. These may include the presence of child and/or forced labour practices on these farms, poor health and safety practices, non-compliance with labour laws and non-alignment with the requirements of international standards such as those set out in the ILO and IFC PS 2.
- AGRA conducted a desktop Indigenous Peoples (IPs) screening and vulnerability assessment of the Programme. Of the seven countries assessed, no recognized IPs were identified in Zambia and Malawi. However, potential IPs were identified in specific regions within Burkina Faso, Ethiopia, Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda. There are no circumstances in which the Programme will result in any form of displacement of IPs, affect access to land or natural resources, nor will the Programme interventions be likely to include activities that significantly alter the physical or social context of the local areas in which the interventions will be implemented. However, the identification of the IPs underscores the need for careful and proactive consideration of any risk that might arise during program implementation and requires tailored approaches to address the unique circumstances and challenges of indigenous communities in the identified areas. In this regard, the Programme will proactively work with IPs and their representatives to ensure that the programme (i) does not cause any significant harm to their way of life and livelihoods and (ii) enhances or complements their existing livelihoods. Further, using appropriately translated ESS and programme information to local languages, IPs will also be continuously engaged before and during programme implementation, with feedback and suggestions from leaders and representatives used as proactive measures to ensure a successful implementation of the programme.

#### Intervention Level E&S Risks
- The Programme includes the introduction and promotion of novel FL-RS technologies. The innovative technology introduced to smallholder farmers and/or MSMEs may lead to unintended health, safety and/or environmental risks such as injury or increased pollution generation as a consequence of recipients not being adequately trained on their correct use and/or waste disposal.
- AGRA procures NGOs to create awareness and demonstrate the FL-RS in collaboration with manufacturer and agro-dealers. These trainings are conducted at selected smallholder farms. NGO staff may not have adequate technical competencies to provide training on unintended health, safety and/or environmental risks associated with the FL-RS technologies. NGOs may deliver training that is not culturally suited to the target audience and may be biased from an inclusion and gender perspective.

### Programme E&S Risk Management Framework
AGRA has in place a centralised Environmental and Social Management System (ESMS) that will be applicable to all interventions initiated in AGRA’s focus countries. The ESMS is designed to ensure that potential negative E&S impacts of AGRA interventions are minimized, while positive effects on communities and the environment are enhanced. It follows the guidance of IFC Performance Standard 1 and includes an E&S policy and procedures, identification of risks and impacts, management programs, organisational capacity and competency, emergency preparedness and response, stakeholder engagement, grievance management, monitoring, review and reporting.

In addition, AGRA has developed a Programme E&S Action Plan (ESAP), which provides a framework for E&S action across the entire RE-GAIN Programme. Once the specific communities and beneficiaries have been selected, the Programme ESAP will be adapted where appropriate to be specific to the E&S risks and impacts associated with the individual country, region, crop and combination of FL-RS applicable to the specific intervention envisaged. The Programme ESAP sets out mitigation measures for each of the risks identified in the E&S risk assessments conducted.

Full details on Programme E&S risk identification and mitigation are provided in Annex 6 of the Funding Proposal.

## G.2. Gender assessment and action plan

To ensure that the RE-GAIN Programme is designed, financed, implemented and monitored in a gender-responsive and socially inclusive manner, a comprehensive Gender Assessment and Gender and Inclusion Action Plan has been developed (Annex 8). These outputs comply with the GCF’s Gender Policy, as well as AGRA’s own Gender and Inclusion Strategy, ESMS Manual and Safeguarding of Vulnerable Persons and Youth Policy.

RE-GAIN’s Gender Assessment considers the various factors (including those exacerbated by climate change) that influence gender inequality in each of the seven target countries’ agriculture sectors and identifies opportunities for women, men and youth to contribute to inclusive resilience building within post-harvest value chains. These opportunities are captured in a Programme-wide Gender and Inclusion Action Plan, which defines activities, indicators and targets specific to women and youth in their capacities as smallholders, cooperative networks, MSMEs and FL-RS vendors.

The Gender Assessment combines extensive literature review, carried out on a country-by-country basis on the key contextual factors that enable and/or impede gender equality progress at a national level, with primary findings drawn from 28 national and local stakeholder engagements spread evenly across the seven countries.

Using these information sources to examine the disparities (and underlying drivers) between women, men and youth within the context of agriculture and climate change in each of RE-GAIN’s countries, several clear, common and consistent themes on gender and inclusion emerge: Improved, inclusive access to finance for women and youth is a fundamental enabler for increased and more equitable participation in FL-RS. The upfront cost of many solutions is a primary barrier to adoption by women and youth. It is therefore critical that inclusive and diverse finance options and advisory services are made available to these groups. Inclusive and accessible training and information on FL-RS for women and youth is necessary to empower and capacitate them as farmers and business owners. This includes both technical and financial advisory support on: FL-RS tools, equipment and structures; the benefits of changing current practices or preferences; and on financial management and marketing – including options available, budgeting, saving, repayment processes, pricing, market quality standards and control etc. Such training must be designed in a gender sensitive and highly context specific manner. This must be coupled with sensitization of male community members, to overcome persistent disparities between women and men’s access to financial, technical, infrastructural and capacity building resources. The deliberate and targeted appointment of extension service delivery partners is fundamental to achieving many of these recommendations, meaning the selection of RE-GAIN’s implementation partners must incorporate a specific technical competency on demonstrated experience of working with women, youth and other marginalised groups. Adopting a dual top-down bottom-up approach to amplify women and youth’s voices on FL-RS is needed to create sustainable shifts in the way women and youth engage in, and benefit from, RE-GAIN’s support. This means that on-the-ground, community centred efforts must be coupled with more inclusive, enforceable FL-RS policies. These recommendations, together with RE-GAIN’s overarching goal, outcomes and outputs, were used to develop the Gender and Inclusion Action Plan which includes both qualitative and quantitative targets over the five-year period. Implementing the measures set out in this Plan will result in 1,284,321 women (~50% of the total beneficiaries) benefitting from RE-GAIN’s support. A dedicated RE-GAIN Gender Expert will oversee the delivery and M&E of the Gender and Inclusion Action Plan, and ensure continued compliance with related processes, including application of the grievance mechanism, safeguarding protocols and ESMS. The cost of this expert has been budgeted for the full five years, along with related expenses required to deliver all activities.

Output 2.1 will build partnerships for market linkages and provide training to suppliers and service providers. At the heart of the programme is Output 2.2, in which temporary financial mechanisms will be put in place to support smallholders and MSMEs to adopt FL-RS. According to the detailed programme budget, a total of USD 20.42 million of GCF funds will be used to subsidize tarpaulins, plastic sheets, metal and plastic silos, and hermetic bags for smallholders farmers to protect harvested crops, and USD 12.34 million from GCF will be used to subsidize larger items for which service providers take loans – mechanical multi-crop threshers, moisture meters, communal storage structures, storage protectants and control agents. This is to be achieved through two innovative business and financial models:

- **Model 1** will promote the local supply of FL-RS interventions by using conditional procurements to effectively subsidize interventions at smallholder farmer level, facilitated by 5,350 village-based advisers across the 35 priority regions. This model will enable agrodealers to provide FL-RS to approximately 540,000 smallholder farmers at a discounted rate, with GCF funds used to procure one item for every two items procured and sold by an agrodealer. The subsidy will be passed down to the smallholder farmers as a discount of 33 per cent on the purchase price, also explained by the PSAA entity as a “Buy two, get one free” promotional offer. The aim of this model is to make these interventions more affordable for farmers, and to stimulate production and manufacturer capacity by placing pre-emptive orders.

- **Model 2** will match MSMEs and cooperatives with suppliers and financial institutions, facilitating the signing of multi-stakeholder agreements to allow the MSMEs to purchase more expensive equipment for post-harvest handling. The MSMEs will become service providers, operating the equipment on a fee-for-service basis with smallholder customers. Under each agreement, AGRA will commit to an upfront co-payment of 30 per cent of the purchase price, with the MSME taking a loan from the financial institution to pay the other 70 per cent out of its service fee earnings. In the event of a successfully completed loan repayment, the equipment is owned by the MSME. If the MSME defaults, the supplier is obliged in terms of the agreement to buy back the equipment, accounting for depreciation. The establishment of the service operations will be facilitated through the business development support under Output 2.1. The aim of this model is to reduce borrowing costs to MSME service providers, through subsidies on the purchase price, and to reduce credit risk to local financial institutions.

The subsidy mechanisms have been carefully designed to achieve these effects, ensuring that the benefits of the subsidies do reach the targeted smallholder farmers without leakages, that systems are in place to ensure agrodealers’ compliance with subsidy conditions, and that contingencies are in place to deal with market price fluctuations or supply chain disruptions. In response to a query from iTAP about the possibility of renting-to-own, AGRA confirmed that variations on the model will be possible, using the equipment as collateral for the loans – to be specified in the four-way agreement. The programme has already engaged the following financial partners: AgLeaseCo in Zambia, Equity for Africa (EFTA in Tanzania and EF-KEN in Kenya), and Coris Bank in Burkina Faso, the last of which AGRA has previously implemented an equipment finance scheme.

### Impact Potential Scale: High

The programme will target two of the GCF results areas for adaptation, aiming to enhance the resilience of (a) the most vulnerable people and communities and (b) health and well-being, and food and water security, in the seven participating countries.

Climate change. The funding proposal highlights the vulnerability of all seven countries to climate change, with negative impacts experienced and anticipated – not only on crop production and yields but also on volumes of crops that remain of sufficient quality for consumption or sale at harvest and beyond. Historical trends are shown, based on data from the World Bank Climate Change Knowledge Portal, with temperature indicators displaying an increasing trend over time, and the rate of increase accelerating in recent decades. Historical rainfall trends display far greater variability, and for some countries a measure of uncertainty regarding the direction of the trend. The perceived historical increase in the frequency of intense rainfall events is not shown as a key climate variable.

Data from the same portal, using two future emissions scenarios, indicate that future projections of rainfall suggest a projected rise in rainfall levels and rainfall days across most of East Africa, likely to increase flooding. The exception is Zambia, where projections display a slight drying signal, consistent with regional trends for southern Africa. Continued trends of increasing variability in rainfall patterns are expected, as are increasing heat extremes. AGRA acknowledges that the impact of climate change on post-harvest food loss has not been well studied, either in terms of observed or projected impacts, but suggests that “[o]f the range of climate hazards that affect harvest and post-harvest value chains of cereal crops and legumes, scholarly publications and country experts pointed to variable, erratic, and unpredictable rainfall (including an increase in heavy rainfall events), high temperatures (extreme heat), and flooding as the priority hazards of concern”.

Adaptation impact. The programme aims to yield significant adaptation benefits for vulnerable smallholder farmers and their households across the seven countries, through reducing climate change-induced food losses. This will enhance farming communities’ resilience to climate change by improving management at harvest and post-harvest stages, maintaining higher levels of food availability for consumption and sale than would be the case without the GCF–AGRA investment. The intended impacts of the programme are considered complementary to the broader adaptation strategies for agricultural resilience within each target country, which tend to be focused more on primary production. The programme will target vulnerable members of society, aiming to increase the resilience of rural livelihoods through investing in developing local markets and building up farmer cooperatives, youth and women’s groups, and MSMEs, leveraging partnerships with financial institutions and private sector actors to mobilize significant investment beyond the proposed programme.

Through these results, the programme aspires to directly benefit over 2.6 million people and indirectly benefit a further 2.7 million people, distributed across the seven target countries. The 35 priority regions were selected on the basis of crop profiles and vulnerability to identified climate hazards. Smallholder farmers adopting new climate-resilient practices with FL-RS will be direct beneficiaries, experiencing improved food and nutrition security as well as increased food quality and market connections that improve their incomes. MSMEs and agrodealers will experience improved livelihoods as a result of increased business through the programme subsidy and stimulation of demand. The indirect beneficiaries include local buyers, whose access to affordable and good-quality grains and legumes will improve as a result of the programme interventions, and market players such as suppliers and processors, who will benefit from improved market linkages.

At GCF outcome level, the programme will measure the number of beneficiaries reached, the number of farmers adopting innovations that strengthen climate change resilience, the number of people with new climate-resilient livelihood options, and the number of vulnerable farm households with improved food security, using farmer surveys employing the Food Insecurity Experience Scale. Since the innovations and livelihood options in question are predefined and relate to specific FL-RS, these key indicators should deliver credible tracking of results. Although food losses before and after programme interventions will not be quantified, a co-benefit indicator of the percentage of produce meeting quality standards will measure the percentage of grain rejected due to quality concerns associated with afla/mycotoxins, with the aim being to halve the rejection rate.

Overall, it can be anticipated that food loss reductions will be significant, at least during the programme implementation period (i.e. while the subsidies are in operation), improving resilience through better nutrition, greater income from crop sales and enhanced business incomes along the value chains for technical solutions.

Overall, impact potential is rated as high.

### Paradigm Shift Potential Scale: Medium

Paradigm shift relates to the degree to which the proposed programme can successfully catalyse impact beyond the GCF one-off investment of USD 75.6 million over five years, together with the AGRA co-finance. The iTAP recognizes the hard work and careful thought that have gone into designing the programme in a manner that intends to elicit a paradigm shift in the harvest and post-harvest development pathways for the seven target countries and continentally in Africa. A clear focus on achieving scale and sustainability is embedded in the programme design, both through the comprehensive value chain approach to removing barriers to scaling of FL-RS and through the work with governments on country-level enabling environments in Component 3. The design of the business and financial models, however, relying as it does on a large subsidy that will be abruptly withdrawn at programme end, does not seem optimal for achieving sustained demand or long-term market transformation.

Enabling environment for scaling up. Component 3 of the programme seeks to create an enabling environment for the uptake of FL-RS. This will involve working with governments on new policies, such as stimulating tax exemptions, subsidies or reduced interest rates for FL-RS investments. RE-GAIN will provide technical assistance to local and national government entities to build their institutional capacity for scaling FL-RS through partnerships, platforms, and support on tools, guidelines and monitoring. RE-GAIN will also work with governments and other development partners to promote additional private sector investment in post-harvest infrastructure, including storage facilities, processing units and cold chains, as well as helping regulatory bodies to develop and implement quality standards and certification processes for FL-RS products and services. Support will also be provided to enhance the capacities of extension services and climate advisory services to monitor food loss in relation to climate conditions and the impact of FL-RS in reducing loss. Although the RE-GAIN investments aim to prolong grain shelf life in the absence of good rural road access and renewable-powered cooling systems, engaging with wider government adaptation and development initiatives will enable complementarity in this regard.

Innovation in institutional markets. A welcome innovation is the focus on building smallholder farmers’ market access by linking them to institutional markets for staple crops – such as schools, universities, armies, hospitals and prisons – as well as to technical and financial partners supporting public sector feeding schemes. This will be achieved by facilitating purchase agreements with farmer cooperatives or producer groups, and, where no such organizations exist, by using aggregators. Furthermore, the signed purchase agreements can be used as collateral to obtain credit from financial institutions. Technical assistance includes support by village-based advisers to wheat flour millers, enabling them to avoid unnecessary food loss from overprocessing to refined flour, and instead to switch to producing healthier wholegrain bread flour for school-feeding markets. RE-GAIN will leverage the institutional markets through government procurement processes and work with partners such as HarvestPlus, the Partnership for Child Development, Save the Children and the World Food Programme.

Sharing lessons beyond the target countries. Evidence generated and lessons learned in FL-RS from the target communities and countries will be used to develop capacity, influence policy and promote best practices across Africa. Activity 3.1.4 has budget set aside to work actively to leverage the lessons from the programme in other contexts, building on the extensive networks of AGRA. AGRA will facilitate collaboration with continental and regional bodies and national governments, including African regional climate institutions and regional economic communities. This will include field days and media events bringing together the private sector, government officials, farmers from non-programme areas, and development partners to showcase success stories and encourage replication. Knowledge products will be developed and widely shared, and a business case will be developed for investments in FL-RS manufacturing, supply, service provision and financing.

Exit strategy for subsidies. The funding proposal states: “Long-term sustainability is at the core of the programme design, with GCF investments seen as catalysers of change within local systems. A key aspect of this GCF intervention is having an exit strategy through the creation of an enabling environment to shift away from the smart-subsidies, catalysing further investments in FL-RS under Component 2”. Once the programme ends, however, there will be nothing in place to manage this transition away from the subsidy. Prices are likely to go up at least 30 per cent as soon as the subsidy is removed, which may lead to a fall-off in purchasing and thus lower adoption rates for the FL-RS. The iTAP wonders whether a subsidy that was smaller, more gradually phased out, or both, would be less disruptive, and enable continued growth in sales and loans. In sharing examples of successful market stimulation, AGRA made no mention of other similar price subsidy programmes. Arguably, support to local manufacture might be a more appropriate level at which to utilize a subsidy – to stimulate long-term sustainable production of inputs, rather than providing a one-time subsidy of inputs, for which there is no ceiling on imports.

Local manufacturing not prioritized. The programme does not prioritize direct support to local manufacturing of FLRS, which could have been an alternative focus for subsidization in the form of seed capital or expansion grants to local manufacturers. Although business development support is part of the programme, this consists of training workshops and provision of market intelligence, and is aimed at businesses that will buy machinery and operate it on a fee-for-service basis. Unlike some of the other AGRA programmes cited, such as the SaYeTech support to artisans in Ghana to manufacture threshers, the RE-GAIN programme does not include any such direct support to local manufacturing.

The response to a question from iTAP about import substitution was that while “AGRA fully agrees that long-term sustainable domestic production of inputs is critical for maximizing the impact of the programme,…for the first years of programme implementation, RE-GAIN will prioritize delivering its support to the target groups over import substitution to ensure timely programme delivery and to stimulate and prime the market for local manufacturing capacity.”

## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 Programme Background
A great deal of attention has been paid in recent decades to the impacts of climate change on crop production, i.e., on growing risks to agricultural productivity. Scholarly investigations and public and private research have invested heavily in identifying and – where feasible – quantifying the ramifications of climate change on crop yields, yield stability over seasons, and in exploring plausible management options for the emerging challenges (CGIAR, 2023). As governments and societies look at how to minimize the risks of climate change, the impact of these changes on food production is increasing, fueling concerns about food security and livelihoods for current and future generations.

Food security, however, is affected not only by changes in crop production but by changes occurring throughout the crop value chain, including during post-harvest phases (Akoth, 2020). It is therefore crucial to examine the impacts of climate change on a crop’s value chain, including production, aggregation, storage, transportation, processing, and distribution. Each stage comprises several sub-processes, and climate change may plausibly affect many or all of the sub-processes too.

With the lion’s share of research and resources for resilience interventions in the agricultural sector having been focused on production, the RE-GAIN project is an effort to give dedicated focus to harvest and post-harvest stages of the value chain – specifically, harvesting, post-harvesting handling and storage, processing, transportation, and logistics. As summarized in Table 1-1, the International Fund for Agricultural Development (IFAD) report highlights a range of climate change concerns in the post-production stages of value chains and potential adaptation interventions that could increase resilience against such climate change concerns (IFAD, 2015).

| Value Chain Components | Climate Risk Issues | Risk Management Interventions |
|------------------------|---------------------|-------------------------------|
| Post-harvest management | Rising losses in harvest volume; declining safety, market quality and nutritional value due to increasing temperatures, humidity, pests and diseases. | Improve knowledge sharing on harvesting techniques to reduce losses. Incentivize waste reduction measures and value addition for by-products; provide renewable energy sources to cover changing requirements for cooling, drying, milling, and threshing. |
| Siting of processing facilities | Extreme climate events (such as floods, heatwaves, and storms) may damage processing facilities; shifting climatic conditions may render some sites redundant or increase transportation costs. It could create a sustainable environment to pests and diseases, affecting both product quality and its suitability for consumption. | Use hazard exposure and crop suitability maps to inform the siting of processing facilities; retrofit processing facilities with protective features; insure processing facilities against extreme climate events. |
| Energy in processing | High dependence on local bioenergy (wood, charcoal, dung, crop residues) has trade-offs with better soil management; rising temperatures require more energy for cooling. | Provide renewable energy sources (such as solar photovoltaic panels for cooling/drying/milling/heating, wind, biogas); equip processing facilities with energy-saving appliances (e.g., solar lighting, solar charging, efficient cook stoves); adopt pollution control measures. |
| Water in processing | Declining and more irregular water supplies; growing competition with other domestic or industrial users. | Re-site facilities closer to more suitable water sources; increase water storage and distribution capacity (water harvesting, communal ponds, groundwater recharge); introduce demand-side management. |

## 1.2 BRIEF PROGRAMME DESCRIPTION

There is a clear gap in knowledge, data, and interventions designed to target the impacts of climate change at the harvest and post-harvest stages of the value chain, despite the mounting evidence of the ramifications on food loss and the impact this has on land use changes and associated climate change mitigation. The majority of the current programmes designed to tackle climate-induced food loss focus on the pre-harvest stages of the value chain.

To address the pressing need for broader implementation of solutions aimed at reducing climate-related harvest and post-harvest food loss, the proposed programme is designed to raise awareness and build capacity to promote the adoption of Food Loss Reduction Solutions (FL-RS). It will do this by creating institutional capacity, facilitating the uptake of FL-RS by end users and service providers, increasing options of solutions’ availability, and enabling practical application through policy interventions. This will include enhanced financial access for farmers and Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs), empowering them to invest in climate-friendly FL-RS and incentivising vendors, manufacturers, and suppliers of climate-adapted FL-RS, fostering a robust market ecosystem.

A key focus is on strengthening the capabilities of countries to develop climate-resilient post-harvest infrastructure, both through providing physical solutions alongside capacity building along the value chains. This includes investing in strategic frameworks and implementation plans, including a regulated quality-based pricing system and tax exemptions on imports, for reducing food loss. By enhancing access to markets, the programme will encourage farmers to adopt FL-RS products and services, thereby boosting their climate and economic resilience.

### Target Countries Overview

During the 2023–2027 period, AGRA plans to target 28 million farmers across 15 Sub-Saharan African countries, 40% of which will be women. The RE-GAIN Programme focuses on AGRA’s activities in seven target countries, as shown in Figure 1-1 below. The RE-GAIN Programme is designed to combat food loss during the post-harvest stages and to boost climate resilience by fostering awareness and by building capacity for the adoption of Food Loss Reduction solutions (FL-RS). The programme aims to transfer these solutions to end users and service providers for practical application while facilitating financial access to farmers and Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs) to invest in climate-resilient FL-RS. The programme plans to incentivize vendors, manufacturers, and suppliers to adopt these solutions and enhance the capacity of countries to develop climate-resilient post-harvest food handling infrastructure.

## Harvesting and Post Harvesting Definition

For the RE-GAIN programme, the key value chain stages considered are shown in Figure 1-2.

![Figure 1-2 Strategic value chain stages included in the RE-GAIN Programme](image-link)

The harvesting process within this RE-GAIN Programme proposal is defined as the interval between the culmination of agricultural production, marked by the crop reaching its maturity, and the initiation of post-harvest treatment. This process encompasses the identification of the optimal harvesting time and is further delineated into four distinct stages:

1. Removal of contaminated seeds, heads or cobs of matured crops at harvest
2. Reaping, which involves cutting, pulling, or gathering the mature crops.
3. Threshing, the process of separating the grain from the rest of the plant.
4. Cleaning, such as winnowing, to remove chaff and other impurities.
5. Hauling, which entails the transportation of the harvested produce to storage or processing facilities.

The post-harvest handling and storage stage commences once the crop exits the field and is typically conducted on the farm. This stage encompasses several key operations, including:

1. Threshing, which can be performed manually or with mechanical threshing machines.
2. Drying, utilizing cribs, tarpaulins, and similar methods.
3. Cleaning and sorting, such as through winnowing, to remove impurities.
4. On-farm storage, which includes the use of granaries, hermetic bags, ordinary bags, stacks, metal silos, and plastic silos.
5. In some instances, primary processing activities, such as grinding, hulling, pounding, milling, drying, and sieving, are also conducted during this stage.

The processing, transportation, and logistics stage involves farmers selling their harvested crops either directly to traders, who collect the produce from the farm, or to collection centres and processors. These market participants then undertake the tasks of product accumulation, initial processing, quality control, grading, packaging, and transportation to wholesale buyers.

## 1.3 REASONING FOR REQUESTED FUNDING

Africa's food insecurity challenge has been exacerbated by climate change. Sub-Saharan Africa stands at a crossroads with an unprecedented opportunity for food systems transformation, driven by the demands of a rapidly growing population of 1.5 billion and the pressures of a changing climate (World Bank, 2023) (Worldometer, n.d.). The continent faces significant development challenges including food insecurity, resource degradation, poverty, gender inequality, and social exclusion. The vicious cycle of poverty and environmental degradation in Africa is evident in low crop productivity, deforestation, land degradation, conflict, migration, and vulnerability to climate shocks, which perpetuate persistent food insecurity and poverty. The effects of climate change are expected to be severe in Africa, where the capacity to adapt and respond to a changing climate is weak.

The impacts of climate change have increased over the past decades in Africa, manifesting in more frequent, intense, and prolonged extreme weather events, such as floods, droughts, heatwaves, locust outbreaks, desertification, and sandstorms. These extreme weather events have resulted in increased temperatures and humidity, shifts in precipitation patterns, water stress, and soil erosion. Most African countries already face recurrent droughts that affect growing seasons, often leading to short growing periods reducing the viability of farming in marginal agricultural areas. Projected reductions in crop yields in some countries could reach as much as 50% by 2030, and crop net revenues may fall by up to 90% by 2100, with smallholder farmers being the most affected (IPCC, 2018).

Therefore, the RE-GAIN programme aims to enhance the climate resilience and adaptive capacity of smallholders by promoting the widespread adoption of FL-RS in seven African countries. According to the World Bank estimates, a one percent reduction in post-harvest losses in Sub-Saharan Africa could lead to economic gains of $40 million each year, and most of the benefits would go directly to smallholder farmers (World Bank, 2011). Moreover, food loss and waste are the result of an extremely inefficient use of resources and account for about 3.3 gigatonnes of greenhouse gas emissions globally (FAO, 2013). Large amounts of water and fertilizer also go into the production of food that never reaches human mouths. Recovering the food that is lost during harvest and post-harvest handling can help close that calorie gap in Africa while strengthening livelihoods and improving food security—without imposing any additional environmental cost. Therefore, facilitated by the Green Climate Fund (GCF) investment, RE-GAIN will roll out a suite of physical interventions alongside capacity building and enhanced financial and market access. Not only will this benefit the respective countries as a whole, but it also has the potential to benefit the region and the wider planet.

## 1.4 PROGRAMME GOAL STATEMENT

IF the capacity of the target countries and communities to respond to climate-triggered food losses is strengthened through improved and inclusive access to financing, promotion of context-specific and gender-responsive innovations to reduce food losses, and better enabling conditions for public and private investments, THEN smallholder farmers will have enhanced food security and livelihood resilience, BECAUSE the widespread use of food loss-reduction technologies will reduce food loss and reduce the carbon footprint of food systems, while increasing household income and building the resilience of smallholder farmers, MSMEs, and rural communities to climate shocks.

## 2 Programme Overview

The RE-GAIN programme is designed to address food loss in Africa through an integrated approach, taking into account the growing need for climate adaptation in this space and the potential for mitigation of reducing food losses. The RE-GAIN programme’s holistic approach to these growing challenges includes strengthening extension services, particularly as it portrays to climate information, demand-side interventions, supply-side development, financial mechanisms, and creating an enabling environment. Below is a comprehensive summary of the programme’s three components. Further information on the programme design and process can be uncovered on the Feasibility Study (Annex 2) and the full Funding Proposal.

### 2.1 COMPONENT 1: FOOD LOSS-REDUCTION SOLUTIONS (FL-RS) DEMAND-SIDE DEVELOPMENT

This component focuses on increasing the demand for and adoption of FL-RS among smallholder farmers to reduce post-harvest food losses, enhance food security, and build resilience to climate change. The activities are centered around raising awareness, providing training, and improving market linkages.

#### 2.1.1 Output 1.1: Support to Smallholder Farmers

- **Awareness-Raising Campaigns**: Targeted campaigns educate farmers about the impacts of climate change on post-harvest losses and the benefits of adopting climate-resilient FL-RS.
- **Training Programs through Extension Services**: Farmers receive training on best practices for harvesting, post-harvest handling, storage techniques, and the use of FL-RS. Training is tailored to local contexts and emphasizes gender responsiveness and youth engagement.
- **Demonstrations of FL-RS**: Practical demonstrations showcase the effectiveness of FL-RS, helping farmers understand and adopt these solutions in their local environments.
- **Technical Assistance to Food Processors**: Assistance is provided to facilitate a shift to whole-grain flour production, which reduces food waste and improves profitability.

#### 2.1.2 Output 1.2: Improved Market Linkages

- **Technical Assistance for Value Chain Structuring**: The programme helps establish more structured relationships between farmers, processors, and buyers, ensuring better quality compliance and reduced post-harvest losses.
- **Linkages with Institutional Markets**: Farmers are connected to institutional markets like school feeding programs, which provide reliable demand for high-quality produce, thereby encouraging the adoption of FL-RS.

#### 2.1.3 Outcome for Component 1

The main outcome of component 1 is increased adoption of FL-RS by smallholder farmers.

### 2.2 COMPONENT 2: FL-RS SUPPLY-SIDE DEVELOPMENT

This component complements the first by improving the availability and affordability of FL-RS through support for local businesses and the introduction of innovative financial mechanisms to stimulate supply of FL-RS and to improve the affordability of physical FL-RS solutions that can improve climate resilience for smallholder farmers and micro, small, and medium enterprises (MSMEs).

#### 2.2.1 Output 2.1: Business Development Support for FL-RS Providers

- **Training for MSMEs and Cooperatives**: Local MSMEs and cooperatives, especially those led by women and youth, are trained to develop sustainable FL-RS service provision operations, with lease-to-own as an example. This includes technical training on equipment use and business management.
- **Market Intelligence and Networking**: AGRA supports the development of market intelligence and networks among local manufacturers, importers, and agro-dealers to boost the FL-RS market.

#### 2.2.2 Output 2.2: Financial Mechanisms and Physical Solutions to Support the Adoption of FL-RS

To stimulate supply and increase the affordability of FL-RS that can increase smallholder farmers and community-led organisations’ climate resilience, the following physical FL-RS solutions have been selected given their impact on food loss and its climate-impacted causes:

- **Hermetic Bags, Metal and Plastic Silos, Tarpaulins, and Plastic Sheets**: These are essential physical solutions aimed at reducing post-harvest losses. They protect crops from pests, moisture, and contamination, thus improving storage and prolonging the shelf life of produce.
- **Harvesting Machinery and Mechanical Threshers/Shellers**: These tools, including solar-powered options, are designed to reduce labor intensity and improve the efficiency of harvesting and post-harvest processing, minimizing losses due to improper handling.
- **Moisture Meters and Storage Protectants**: These devices help maintain optimal conditions for stored crops, preventing spoilage and contamination. Moisture meters are crucial for monitoring and managing crop moisture levels, which is vital in preventing mold growth and toxin production.
- **Communal Storage Structures and Warehouses**: These facilities offer shared storage solutions that help smallholder farmers and aggregators store their produce in controlled environments, reducing the risk of losses due to poor individual storage conditions.

To increase the affordability of these solutions in the market, the following financial models will be leveraged:

Model 1 promotes the local supply of FL-RS interventions by using conditional procurements to effectively subsidise interventions at smallholder farmer level (referred to hereafter as ‘smart-subsidies’). At its base, the smart-subsidy model enables agro-dealers to provide FL-RS to smallholder farmers at a discounted rate by using GCF funds to procure one item for every two items procured and sold by an agro-dealer. The subsidy is passed down to the smallholder farmers as a discount on the purchase price.

The interventions are typically focused on smallholder farmers, with lower individual ticket sizes to the end-users — i.e. for FL-RS such as tarpaulins and plastic sheets, metal and plastic silos, and hermetic bags. The flow of funds will be facilitated through a trust/escrow account at a local financial institution (FI) and released once proof of offtake by eligible smallholder farmers is provided.

## Women in Agriculture

The Malawi economy is highly dependent on agriculture, contributing over a quarter to the country's GDP, and employing approximately 80% of the population with the majority being women. However, the sector's contribution to GDP is hampered by the large gender productivity gap. On average, plots managed by women are 25% less productive. This is largely attributed to lower levels of inputs (including seeds and fertiliser), reduced access to labour, technology and knowledge as well as constraints on crop choice (The World Bank, 2022).

A recent study in Malawi, looking at Maize fields, found that female farmers were much more likely to use poor quality seeds, reduced amounts of fertiliser and farm on lower quality soil compared to their male counterparts. Additionally, the study found that when accounting for the quality and quantity of these inputs, there was no gender gap in agricultural yield (William J. Burke, 2021). Additionally, extension services are critical for ensuring women have equitable access to agricultural inputs and receive fair returns from those inputs. However, research indicates that female farmers and female-headed households in Malawi are less likely to receive agricultural advice than men. Additionally, the rate of female-headed households receiving advice decreased between 2016 and 2018. Older women heading households face a particular disadvantage, being the least likely to receive agricultural advice (Ragasa, 2019). This is further compounded by the fact that most women plot managers are widowed, divorced, or separated, leading to fewer household members contributing to agricultural labour. Consequently, women often rely on female family labour, including themselves and their children as they are rarely able to afford to hire male labour. When they do, it's often less productive due to affordability issues, cultural bias, or women's competing domestic responsibilities (The World Bank, 2022).

#### Snapshot of Findings from AGRA’s Gender Gap Analysis in Malawi’s National Seed System

AGRA’s deep dive into the gendered differences in Malawi’s National Seed System was conducted using the SeedSAT. It highlights areas for improvement, requiring effort by both the government and private sector actors – for example (AGRA, 2022):

- There is no evidence of breeding, variety release, and maintenance activities aimed at promoting gender equality and including women and youth. Additionally, although smallholder farmers, especially women, have extensive knowledge of seed varieties, seeds are still exchanged in agrarian communities marked by gender inequalities, unequal land distribution, and social stigma.
- Trained extension officers are largely men, reinforcing the perception that women are not farmers and should not be consulted on agricultural decisions.
- Women's participation in farmer organisations, such as labour-saving cooperatives, producers' organisations, and marketing groups, is limited. This limitation negatively impacts their access to product markets and, more importantly, their potential for resource allocation.

This is echoed throughout, highlighting the need to ensure climate change adaptation measures are planned with a gender lens and tracked to account for gender and other vulnerabilities.

Malawi has not submitted a National Adaptation Plan or a Climate Change Gender Action Plan (ccGAP) thus far. However, it does have a National Adaptation Plan Framework under its Ministry of Natural Resources, Energy and Mining Environmental Affairs Department which includes gender as one of its guiding principles. Within this, it calls for a gender-responsive approach to the NAP process including ensuring adaptation actions consider women’s access and impact alongside the use of sex-disaggregated data and inputs from gender experts for planning purposes (Government of Malawi, 2020).

## TANZANIA

### National Overview: Status of Gender Equality

Tanzania has demonstrated a strong commitment to promoting gender equality through its participation in various international frameworks and agreements. The country is a signatory to key international instruments such as the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW), which it ratified in 1985, reaffirming its pledge to eliminate gender discrimination and promote women’s rights. Tanzania is also a member of the Beijing Declaration and Platform for Action (1995), where it committed to taking concrete steps to empower women and promote gender equality in areas such as education, health, and economic participation. Additionally, Tanzania actively engages with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), specifically Goal 5, which aims to achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls by 2030. The country is also part of the African Union's Agenda 2063, which emphasizes the importance of gender equality for sustainable development on the continent (UN Women, 2023).

#### Tanzania’s National Gender Legislation

Beginning in 1992, the Government of Tanzania has consistently pushed for the integration of gender equality into its ministries and policies, starting with the creation of the Ministry of Community Development, Women’s Affairs and Children (now known as The Ministry of Community Development, Gender, Women and Special Groups) (The World Bank, 2022). A suite of policies and commitments followed this, aimed at increasing women's participation in society and protecting their rights. This includes the 1999 Land and Village Land Acts offer equal land ownership rights to men and women, The 1971 Law of Marriage Act grants women equal property rights within marriage, including the right to contract, sue, and be sued. However, it controversially allows girls aged 14-15 to marry with parental or court consent, despite a 2019 court ruling mandating the marriage age for both genders to be 18. (UN Women, 2023). More recently, gender considerations have been integrated into the Tanzania Development Vision 2025, which commits to redressing gender imbalances and empowering women (The United Republic of Tanzania, 1999).

Due to these efforts, Tanzania is ranked above regional averages for the World Bank’s Women, Business and the Law (WBL) Index and the World Economic Forum’s Global Gender Gap Index (GGGI). Under the WBL Index, Tanzania scored 81.3/100 and received a perfect score for workplace, pay and mobility (World Bank, 2023). Its main areas for improvement include parenthood and assets, where there is no paid leave of at least 14 weeks available to mothers, and both sons and daughters as well as male and female surviving spouses do not have equal rights to inherit assets (World Bank, 2023). However, for the GGGI, Tanzania is ranked 54 out of 146 countries, down six places from last year (WEF, 2024). This drop is largely due to a decrease in its subindex scores for economic participation and opportunity, as well as educational attainment.

#### Barriers to Effective Implementation of Policies, Legislature and Commitments

Whilst the areas for improvement differ between the GGGI and WBL Indexes, they both fundamentally indicate a need to improve the opportunities available to women and girls from education through to work, including the impacts on marriage and parenthood. Many of the legal frameworks perpetuate gender disparities or have significant gaps. One such example is the Law of Marriage Act, which permits marriage for girls as young as 15, whilst the Law of the Child Act does not address the legal age of marriage or prohibit child marriage. Progress was made when Tanzania's high court ruled in 2016 that marriage of girls under 18 is unconstitutional, a finding reaffirmed by the Court of Appeal in 2019. (The World Bank, 2022) However, it is crucial to align statutory laws with this ruling to ensure consistency and clarity. With over one-third of women in Tanzania married before turning 18, and over half married before turning 20, these rates are more than five times higher than those for men (The World Bank, 2022). Further barriers to successful enforcement include the limited representation of women in decision-making roles which hinder their participation in governance. Limited access to resources and institutional bias, along with violence and harassment within governance structures, further discourage women's involvement, undermining their confidence and opportunities for leadership. This is then subsequently reflected through the ongoing systemic challenges, including discriminatory legal frameworks and sociocultural barriers rooted in traditional norms (Lulu, 2024).

## Women in Agriculture

Across Tanzania, agriculture employs a significant portion of the workforce, accounting for 65% of the overall labour force. In total, 67% of women, compared with 64% of men (International Labour Organization, 2022) are engaged in the agricultural workforce. Despite women making up the majority, female farmers appear to experience a reduction in both quantity and quality of yields compared with their male counterparts. This appears to be because of reduced access to male farm labour and decreased returns from both labour and non-labour inputs, including pesticides and organic fertilizers (Vanya Slavchevska, 2015).

Female plot managers, often widowed, divorced, or separated, lack male household labour and rely more on female family labour, which tends to be less productive. In instances where they can afford to hire male labourers, they tend to be less productive under female management, possibly due to lower-quality labour affordability or cultural biases against working for female managers (The World Bank, 2015). Additionally, women's unequal return from non-labour inputs (i.e. pesticides and fertilizers) is likely related to either using lower quality products, or insufficient knowledge on how and when to apply them (The World Bank, 2022).

Due to the significant role agriculture plays in the Tanzanian economy, in April 2022, President Samia Suluhu announced “Agenda 10/30”, aimed at bolstering agricultural output growth by 10% by 2030. Amongst other measures, the centre of this strategy is the expansion of labour and land, with over 3 million workers expected to be added to the sector by 2025, made up mostly of women and youth. It also aims to ensure each farmer is allocated a minimum of 10 acres of land (UNDP, 2023).

## Climate Change and Gender Equality

Tanzania submitted its first Nationally Determined Contributions (NDC) in 2018 and has since submitted an updated version in 2021. In its most recent NDC, under adaptation measures to be pursued, there is a brief mention of gender mainstreaming, highlighting the need for enhanced gender equity in climate change adaptation actions as well as promoting measures to address the negative impacts of climate change on women. Beyond this, there is no further mention of gender issues including anything related to mainstreaming or equality (The United Republic of Tanzania Vice President's Office, 2021).

Tanzania has not submitted a National Adaptation Plan (NAP), though it does have a National Adaptation Programme of Action (NAPA) which makes no mention of gender equality or mainstreaming (United Republic of Tanzania, 2007). It does however have a Climate Change Gender Action Plan (ccGAP), which was issued in 2013. It acknowledges the disproportionate impact climate change will have on women, as well as their role as agents of change. Within this, agriculture was identified as a priority sector, due to its contribution to the Tanzanian economy and employment prospects. As an objective, it largely promotes the need for greater integration and consideration of women in agricultural activities, outlining several broad-scope actions by which to do so (United Republic of Tanzania Vice Presidents Office, 2013).

## 4 RE-GAIN Programme-Level Gender Analysis

Building on the country-level analyses above, this Chapter describes RE-GAIN’s approach to understanding the programme-specific gender dynamics within the seven countries, the way in which these dynamics influence key gender domains, and the impact that these domains may have on programme activities. The Chapter then describes the findings from applying this stakeholder-driven approach, serving as a basis for developing the Programme’s Gender & Inclusion Action Plan (Chapter 5).

### 4.1 Approach

National and local stakeholder workshops were facilitated in each of the seven RE-GAIN countries, serving as key informants of this programme design process. The first round of workshop discussions (compromising 14 workshops) focussed on the pressing climate challenges faced during harvesting, post-harvesting and processing, transportation and logistics processes – considering the specific regions and crops selected per country – and potential solutions that could be provided by the RE-GAIN programme to overcome these. These workshops were attended by a range of stakeholders, with on average 50% representation of male and female participants.

Building on these workshops, a second round of engagements was facilitated at national and local levels (comprising 14 workshops in total), to garner feedback and recommendations on the solutions and barriers to accessing them, with a particular focus on the gender, environmental and broader social considerations that may influence the sustainability and impact of the proposed solutions on different sub-groups of the target communities. A core focus of these workshops therefore explored:

- The environmental and social risks and impacts of the implementation of solutions, informing the development of Annex 6.
- Potential gender and youth-specific barriers that may impact women and youth’s adoption of solutions and identifying opportunities to overcome these, as a guide to determining key recommendations and actions proposed in this Annex 8.

The dedicated gender and inclusion session was structured in three parts:

- The first focussed on describing the roles that women play in harvest and post-harvest value chains – and whether these differ for male counterparts, and youth.
- The second element of discussion considered various domains that influence women, men and youth engagement in agriculture, and specifically the harvest and post-harvest activities for the select country crops. These domains included:
  - Practices and participation;
  - Knowledge, beliefs, and perceptions;
  - Access to assets;
  - Legal rights and status;
  - Gender roles, responsibilities and time use; and
  - Power and decision-making.
- The session concluded with group discussions on specific opportunities and barriers that women, men or youth may experience in adopting the identified solutions.

As with the first round of workshops, there was – on average – gender parity in participation in both the national and local workshops, with a diverse range of stakeholders invited, including ministry representatives, development agencies, farmer associations, the private sector and individual farmers. The workshop notes from the gender sessions are contained in APPENDIX A of this document.

### 4.2 Findings

As with many of the findings presented in Chapter 2 on the disparities (and underlying drivers) between women and men within the context of agriculture and climate change – many clear, common and consistent themes on gender and inclusion emerge from the fourteen workshop reports. This sheds light on the fact that, while the seven RE-GAIN countries differ in terms of gender equality considerations across key sectors and markers / indicators of progress, they experience many similarities in terms of the gender dynamics that influence the involvement of women and youth in agricultural value chains. Given the inherent linkages between the domains listed below, many findings are interlinked or cut across multiple domains.

**Gender Domains: The Underlying Drivers that Influence the Roles, Voice, Choice and Control that Women, Men and Youth Have in Agricultural Value Chains**

Patterns in the practices of women, men and youth involved in the agricultural sector are largely reflective of how these groups are viewed more broadly in terms of their contribution to, and position within, society as a whole. During the workshops, most participants coupled discussions on “practices” with the typically assigned “roles and responsibilities” of these different groups. The graphic below presents a snapshot of the most common delineation of roles, indicating differences and areas of overlap in the responsibilities of women, men and youth at various stages along agricultural value chains. For example, while all groups are involved in land preparation activities, the way in which these activities are assigned reflects the underlying patriarchal power dynamic that governs how land is used; men take a lead role and operate at the meta-scale of land preparation activities while women and youth take on supportive, more menial tasks. This delineation is similarly echoed across the post-harvest value chain stages and thus of significance to the planning and design of the RE-GAIN programme, which focuses on these latter stages.

The narrative that follows provides more depth into the implications of these roles in terms of other key gender domains.

Socio-cultural and gender relations were noted as the significant underlying reasons for food value chain inefficiencies, which in turn directly cause food loss. The different productive and social roles of men and women, as prescribed by socio-cultural structures affect their access to and control over assets, knowledge and services, and their participation in productive activities and decision-making. This impacts the efficiency of the food value chain and hence is an underlying reason for food loss. **Practices & Participation**

- Women typically participate in more labour-intensive, menial / tedious tasks like planting, winnowing, weeding, and threshing. This stems from perceptions that women are more patient than men, and also inexperienced / incapable of using machinery, pesticides etc. Often, these tasks are also delineated to specific crop types (for example, beans, grains and teff as well as subsistence fruit and vegetables grown for household consumption) and require traditional methods, while men have full control and responsibility over more profitable “cash crops” including transplanting and harvesting as well as decision-making on use of fertilisers, pricing and marketing of crops, and use of mechanised equipment. Women’s involvement in post-harvest activities is often unpaid and includes processing and food preparation. Typically, a portion of the food prepared during these post-harvest processes is retained for household consumption. Women thus place importance on their involvement in these activities as it provides assurance that certain produce is ringfenced for their families prior to sending goods to the market. Women’s access to and participation in markets is significantly lower than men, which affects their ability to sell produce, earn a fair income, and control the use of these proceeds.

- Both women and men attend agricultural-related groups or meetings, and at times women’s level of participation exceeds that of men. Where women’s participation has been higher than that of men, this has been due to intentional efforts by development programmes to reach more women.
