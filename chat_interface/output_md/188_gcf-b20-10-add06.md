### A.2. PROJECT / PROGRAMME EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (MAX 300 WORDS)
1. El Salvador, located in the dry corridor of Central America, is highly vulnerable to climate risks. Projected increases in rainfall variability, temperature, and extreme rainfall events threaten food and water security and livelihoods of family farmers on deforested and degraded lands. Projections estimate water availability reductions of 35%-63%; El Salvador will be most affected. Rising temperatures may reduce crop yields by 30% by 2050, mainly due to drought. Sorghum and maize yields could decrease by up to 20% and dry beans by 50% by 2065, impacting food security, especially in the dry corridor.
2. The project aims to improve resilience of vulnerable family farmers through an integrated landscape approach, promoting on-farm measures for agricultural resilience, household and community water supply systems, and maintaining environmental services through improved production systems and ecosystem restoration.
3. The project will benefit approximately 225,000 people in 50,000 family farms, increase resilience of agricultural systems over 56,600 ha, and restore 17,333 ha of degraded ecosystems, benefiting the target population and others downstream.
4. By scaling up adaptation measures, the project aims for a paradigm shift in agroecosystem-based climate change adaptation, moving away from narrow resource management and smallholder support to sustainable pathways focusing on agroecosystem-based adaptation, diversification, farmer-led technology, community-based governance, and ecosystem restoration.
5. Project outcomes will be sustained through financially viable agroecosystem-based adaptation measures, participation and ownership in adaptation measures, mainstreaming into producer support programs, and effective dissemination of benefits to encourage a favorable environment for sustainability and scaling up.

# DETAILED PROJECT / PROGRAMME DESCRIPTION

## Project Overview

The Salvadorian Institute for Agrarian Transformation (ISTA) delivered 73,266 land certificates between 2009 and 2017 and aims to deliver another 18,000 by the end of 2019. This has increased farmers’ security of occupancy and their confidence in investing in sustainable land management and adaptation measures.

Despite progress, El Salvador still requires support to tackle climate change issues. The project will address this by:

- Implementing restoration/reforestation activities in 17,333ha of degraded ecosystems.
- Promoting resilience of rural livelihoods and family-based farming systems over 56,600 ha.
- Generating mitigation benefits by increasing carbon capture and storage.

The project aligns with national plans and strategies supporting landscape and ecosystem restoration approaches.

## Existing Measures and Strategies

Measures and strategies for adaptation and resilience exist but have not been sufficiently extended in rural areas. The project will upscale models tested through national strategies and initiatives, including:

- **SCCF/GEFTF Project**: Focused on reducing vulnerability and increasing adaptive capacity in micro-watersheds.
- **National Program for the Restoration of Ecosystems and Rural Landscapes (PREP)**: Promotes restoration and conservation of ecosystems and climate-resilient agriculture.
- **Sustainable Agriculture on Hillside Zones Project**: Emphasizes practical training and gender considerations.
- **Central American Agro-Chains**: Aims to improve food security by linking producers to formal markets.
- **El Salvador Environmental Program, PAES**: Focuses on learning and collaboration between project staff and beneficiaries.

The project supports international commitments, including several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).

## Project/Programme Objective against Baseline

The project aims to restore and reforest degraded ecosystems and improve the resilience of livelihoods in El Salvador’s dry corridor through adaptive agroecosystem management.

### Target Locations and Beneficiaries

The Dry Corridor of El Salvador has a population of 2.2 million, with 1.9 million vulnerable to climate change. The project targets localities at highest risk, selected through a multi-step process:

1. **Ranking of Municipalities**: Based on drought exposure and poverty levels.
2. **Defining Intervention Areas**: Based on hydrographic regions with high-vulnerability municipalities.
3. **Selecting Target Areas**: Focused on socioeconomic and environmental vulnerability.
4. **Defining Beneficiaries**: Small-scale family farmers at risk of extreme food insecurity.

The project will target around 50,000 households (225,000 people), prioritizing female-headed households and indigenous populations.

### Criteria for Selection

| Criterion | Level | Weighting | Reasoning |
|-----------|-------|-----------|-----------|
| Drought risk | Municipal | Severe = 2, Moderate = 1 | High exposure to drought increases livelihood collapse risk |
| Levels of extreme poverty | Municipal | Severe = 2, High = 1 | Limited financial resources to buffer against climate impacts |
| Malnutrition | Municipal | Severe = 2, High = 1 | High malnutrition levels increase risk under climate change |

### Project Beneficiaries

The project will focus on family farmers reliant on family labor, with limited access to resources for adaptation. The MAG Paquete Agrícola program targets similar beneficiaries.

| Characteristics | Beneficiary Group | Total Numbers in Target Areas |
|-----------------|-------------------|-------------------------------|
| Hill farmers without livestock or irrigation | I | 32,500 |
| Hill farmers with livestock | II | 6,000 |
| Flat land farmers with irrigation | III | 3,500 |
| Flat land farmers with livestock | IV | 8,000 |

### Baseline Scenario

Without the project, family farmers will face livelihood collapse and extreme food insecurity due to climate change. Historical and projected climate changes have significant implications for agriculture and food security.

### Climate Change Observations and Projections

Significant trends in precipitation and temperature have been observed, with projections indicating further increases in temperature and changes in precipitation patterns. These changes will affect water availability and agricultural productivity, increasing the risk of food insecurity.

### Key Barriers

1. Limited awareness and capacities among farmers regarding agroecosystem management options.
2. Continued application of practices that exacerbate vulnerability due to lack of knowledge and resources.

Farmers lack access to diverse genetic material and financial resources for adaptation strategies, relying heavily on government support.

---

## DETAILED PROJECT / PROGRAMME DESCRIPTION

### 2. Limited capacities for restoring ecosystems and ecosystem service flows in the wider landscape

The importance of maintaining adequate vegetation around water sources and recharge areas is recognized, but measures to restore degraded vegetation are seldom taken due to financial and technical limitations. Active restoration requires investment in nurseries, seed, materials, and labor. Barriers include limited technical knowledge and organizational difficulties, especially for community-based nurseries. Awareness of alternative restoration approaches, like assisted natural regeneration, is also lacking.

### 3. Inadequate conditions for the adaptive and collaborative management of responses to climate change

Government personnel have limited access to information on climate risks and adaptation options. The Meteorological Service collects data, but reliable guidance on climatic trends is missing. Key agencies like MAG/CENTA and MARN have differing views on agriculture, affecting policy integration. The government lacks resources to meet climate change challenges and coordinate responses effectively. Incentive mechanisms do not include adaptation measures, and agrarian reform initiatives lack provisions for tenure security in vulnerable areas.

### Adaptation (with project) Scenario

The project proposes an agroecosystem-based approach to increase resilience, including:

- Promotion of agroforestry practices
- Community-based environmental governance
- Restoration of ecosystem services
- Promotion of climate-resilient crop varieties
- Integration of climate risk management into poverty alleviation and food security initiatives
- Emphasis on adaptive management and stakeholder involvement

# Detailed Project / Programme Description

## Rooftop Rainwater Collection Systems

1. **Rooftop Capture Area**: The system functions well with various roof types, including corrugated iron and clay tiles.
2. **Water Conduction System**: Utilizes gutters and tubing, ideally PVC, which is light, durable, and cost-effective.
3. **Tank for Interception**: Diverts initial contaminated runoff, allowing cleaner water to enter the collection tank.
4. **Reservoir**: Household systems use high-density polypropylene tanks; community systems use underground reinforced concrete tanks.
5. **Cabin for Water Purifying System**: Includes an electric pump, filters, UV light, and ozone generator powered by solar panels.

143. The GCF project will strengthen the resilience of family farmers to climate change impacts through developing capacities for climate resilient agricultural, water management, and restoration practices, resulting in improved food and water security and more sustainable and resilient livelihoods.

145. Overcome institutional and technical barriers, as well as socioeconomic constraints. The Second Communication on Climate Change of El Salvador notes limited effectiveness of some Government and civil society initiatives due to:
- Difficulty in mainstreaming policies and regulatory frameworks on climate change mitigation and adaptation in sectoral ministries and territorial governments.
- Government programs that are often sub-optimal, disconnected, under-financed, and geographically scattered.
- Limited institutional and organizational capacities in territories due to lack of investment in solutions to common problems, mainly natural resources management and land use.
- Weak governance to manage and implement adaptation and mitigation actions in territories.

146. Reach the most vulnerable. Vulnerable groups such as women, youth, and indigenous peoples are particularly affected. 147. Support transformation in agricultural practices and ecosystem health at scale. It will build on lessons from the SCCF/GEF project, scaling these up to landscape level, supporting wider ecosystem health and sustainability. Investments in information management, capacity building, awareness development, and mainstreaming adaptation into policy, regulatory, and planning instruments will support scaling up to national level.

148. Droughts linked to El Nino events in 2014-2015 caused considerable damage to agriculture as farmers had limited access to knowledge, inputs, and support services to manage climate risks. GCF support will also mobilize investments at the small farmer level, who will co-invest in needed agricultural inputs.

150. Strengthen institutional and technical capacity and governance to address climate change. GCF funds will support technical operations of MARN and MAG, improve coordination processes, and encourage joint programs between the two Ministries. GCF support will improve strategic and operational planning to orient investments toward programs that mainstream climate change in existing and future government programs, focusing on climate resilient agricultural measures and landscape management, and improved extension services at the local level. GCF support will build capacities and knowledge of community extension workers, knowledge that will remain with communities after the project ends. The introduction of climate resilient agricultural measures and improved management of land and natural resources will generate resilience to climate change for the most vulnerable.
