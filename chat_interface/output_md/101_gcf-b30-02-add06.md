# PROJECT/PROGRAMME INFORMATION

## B.1. Climate context

### Context

The Dry Corridor of Central America consists of semi-arid and sub-humid ecosystems that cover most of the Pacific slope of Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador, and Nicaragua, as well as smaller areas of Costa Rica and Panama. Similar climatic conditions are found in the arid and semi-arid areas in the west of the Dominican Republic. The Dry Corridor is the most densely populated region of Central America, and the Arid Zones are home to a significant proportion of the Dominican Republic's population — 12% of the total, amounting to ~1.2 million people. In addition, Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador, and Nicaragua have comparatively small Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita, while Costa Rica, Panama, and the Dominican Republic are comparatively wealthy and have relatively low poverty levels. However, all these countries have high income inequality, with instances of extreme poverty — particularly in rural areas.

![Figure 1](#)

Across the region, poverty in rural areas is significantly higher than in urban areas. Nearly 60% of people in the Dry Corridor live in poverty, while the Arid Zones of the Dominican Republic have the highest poverty rate in the country. A further example is that in Costa Rica double the number of people in rural areas are living in extreme poverty compared with in urban areas. Similarly, in Panama, prevalence of extreme poverty is 27% in rural areas compared with only 4% in urban areas. Most rural populations in the Dry Corridor and Arid Zones depend on agriculture for their livelihoods, practicing both subsistence and commercial farming, and farming both crops and livestock. In addition to agriculture, plantation forestry supports the livelihoods of some local communities.

### Climate rationale

The Dry Corridor and Arid Zones are among the world's most vulnerable tropical regions to the impacts of climate change, with Honduras, Nicaragua, Guatemala, the Dominican Republic, and El Salvador recognized as being among the fifteen most vulnerable countries in the world to extreme climate events. The term "Dry Corridor" emerged in the last three decades to describe the increasing frequency and intensity of droughts in the region. These droughts are linked to El Niño events, which are occurring more frequently and intensely as a result of climate change, and have had severe impacts on agriculture and food security in the region. Since agriculture and forestry are greatly affected by climatic conditions, the livelihoods of rural people in the Dry Corridor and Arid Zones are extremely sensitive to the impacts of climate change. For example, during the 2015 drought, over three million people experienced acute food insecurity. These impacts are exacerbated by poor land management practices — which result in extensive environmental degradation — and widespread poverty. Furthermore, these communities have limited access to financial resources for the implementation of adaptation interventions. Without urgent adaptation measures, the vulnerability of the region's population to climate change impacts will increase. Further details of climate change risks and impacts for the Dry Corridor and Arid Zones are described below.

### Climate change risks

Past climate trends and future projections show that the region's climate is changing in several ways, including: i) temperatures increasing across the region; and ii) shifting rainfall patterns. In most parts of the Dry Corridor and Arid Zones, rain seasons are shortening and the intensity of midsummer droughts is increasing. Droughts that extend over a year or more are also becoming increasingly frequent and severe, mainly because of the increasing frequency and intensity of El Niño events. Concurrently, extreme rainfall events are increasing in frequency and severity because of changes in La Niña. Country-specific observed and predicted changes in temperature and rainfall are presented in Section 3 of Annex 2: Feasibility Study.

Overall, temperatures across Central America are anticipated to continue to increase in future. By the end of the century, temperatures are projected to increase by 3–3.5°C under a medium emissions scenario (RCP4.5) and by as much as 6–7°C under a high emissions scenario (RCP8.5) relative to the baseline of 1961–1990. While the trend of increasing temperatures is consistent across Central America and the Caribbean, the magnitude of change is expected to vary among countries and between geographical areas within countries.

![Figure 2](#)

Mean annual rainfall is projected to decrease across much of Central America by 2070 for both medium (RCP4.5) and high (RCP8.5) emissions scenarios. In addition to the predicted future trends in mean annual temperature and rainfall, extreme temperatures are predicted to increase in the coming decades. From 2021–2050, daytime high temperatures (TXx) and night-time high temperatures (TNx) are projected to increase everywhere in the region. Rates of warming are expected to be strongest over the northern portion of the Dry Corridor, particularly over the eastern mountain slopes in Guatemala, Honduras, and Nicaragua. Similarly, temperatures of cold days are predicted to increase across the region. The rates of these increases are projected to be strongest over the Pacific coasts of Nicaragua, El Salvador, Honduras, and Guatemala. In addition — and although rainfall in the Dry Corridor and Arid Zones is expected to decrease by 11% on average by the end of the century — extreme rainfall is predicted to increase significantly across parts of the region by mid-century. The number of intense rainfall events and extremely intense rainfall events occurring each year is predicted to increase.

![Figure 3](#)

Recent evidence and model simulations predict that the risk of drought will increase with climate change, as more intense and prolonged El Niño events occur across the region. Indeed, if average global temperatures reach 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels, extreme El Niño events are likely to happen twice as often — approximately every 10 years as opposed to every 20 years. Both droughts and dry spells will increase in the Dry Corridor and Arid Zones as a result of climate change. Droughts are years or seasons when rainfall is below average, while dry spells are defined as large number of consecutive days when minimal rain falls. From 2021–2050, it is projected that parts of the Dry Corridor and Arid Zone will experience an increase in dry spells. While the number of dry spells is projected to decrease in parts of Panama, Costa Rica, Nicaragua, and Honduras, it is expected to increase over the Pacific basin in Nicaragua and El Salvador, as well as in Guatemala.

![Figure 4](#)

In addition, annual mean water excess is projected to decrease across most parts of the Dry Corridor. Projections for drought events in the region show an increase in drought length, intensity, and frequency under both a medium emissions scenario (RCP4.5) and high emissions scenario (RCP8.5), for both short-term and long-term droughts. These increases are projected to be moderate in early decades of the century (2011–2040), then become more pronounced at the mid-century (2041–2070) before becoming extreme at the end of the century (2071–2100). During dry spells and droughts, there is a pronounced risk of the spread of aridity in parts of the Dry Corridor and Arid Zone as the availability of water is reduced, which could have lasting impacts on the local environment. On an annual scale, the region currently experiences a rainy season with two peaks separated by a dry period of weeks to over a month in duration, termed the mid-summer drought (MSD). Projections for MSD trends under a high emissions scenario (RCP8.5) show an increase in duration for the MSD period, which is projected to increase by an average of over a week. Additionally, the MSD minimum precipitation is projected to decrease by an average of over 26%.

![Figure 5](#)

### Climate change impacts

Climate change is already having severe impacts on communities in the Dry Corridor and Arid Zones. Indeed, its impact on agriculture is commonly cited as a major driver of migration out of the region to North America, especially from Honduras, Guatemala, and El Salvador. In 2014, for example, severe drought across the region resulted in an estimated US$465 million worth of agricultural losses, including basic grains, coffee, and livestock. In Honduras, Guatemala, and El Salvador alone, a total ~2.5 million people experienced food insecurity as a result of approximately 75% loss of maize and bean crops. In 2016, 50–90% of crop loss resulted in 3.5 million people requiring humanitarian assistance across the region. This trend is continuing, with subsistence farmers in the Dry Corridor of Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, and Nicaragua having lost more than half of their maize and bean crops in 2018, with 2.2 million people affected. These recent droughts have been unprecedented in their severity and the magnitude of their impacts. Such intense droughts are expected to increase in frequency under future climate conditions and, in the absence of adaptation interventions, people living in the Dry Corridor and Arid Zones will continue to experience adverse impacts of climate change.

Future predicted impacts of climate change include decreased harvests of maize, beans, coffee, and other important crops, leading to increased poverty, food insecurity, and migration from rural areas. Reductions in coffee yields have been predicted to be between 7% in 2020 and 39% in 2100 across all Central American countries under climate change conditions, while a similar study predicted reductions in maize yields of between 4% in 2020 and 18% in 2100. These reductions will result from direct climate impacts (e.g. droughts and floods) as well as indirect impacts on crops (e.g. declines in crop pollinators). Climate change will also impact the land area suitable for growing specific crops, with declining areas expected for coffee and beans across the region. For example, El Salvador and Costa Rica are expected to experience decreases in land suitability for growing coffee of 40% or more. Similarly, modeling of maize indicates that land suitability is likely to decrease by up to 34% in El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, and Nicaragua. Although some cooler highland areas that are currently unsuitable for these crops may become more suitable as temperature increases, desire to shift crops into these areas will likely catalyze forest clearing in critical water recharge zones of river catchments thereby reducing aquifer recharge. This impact, coupled with an increase in extreme rainfall events, will increase the likelihood and severity of flooding. Climate change will likely also increase the frequency of wildfires, causing further forest degradation and threatening farming, forest-based livelihoods, and fuelwood supply.

The climate change impacts described above are exacerbated by the baseline problems of forest loss and land degradation, which result in decreased water supply and food security. Without comprehensive adaptation interventions, agricultural losses will continue to increase, along with ecosystem loss, water shortages, poverty, and food insecurity. This will ultimately increase the exposure and sensitivity of communities in the Dry Corridor and Arid Zones who are already extremely vulnerable to climate change.

The programme will target seven catchments for interventions — one in each of the participating countries. In these catchments, the application of the Soil and Water Assessment Tool (SWAT) allows for the analysis of potential impacts of climate change in the programme’s target areas. The availability of historical observed meteorological data and site-specific climate change projections are very limited. As observed in the following figure, there are only 24 meteorological stations within the limits of the Dry Corridor. Only for three of the countries, there were stations within the prioritized pilot sites and just one of them (Honduras) had complete observed rainfall and temperature data for a period of over 30 years with less than 15% of discontinuity.

![Figure 7a](#)

Input data: Data from Choluteca-baja are measurement stations that were verified locally and statistically. The regional data set for the rainfall and temperature that was used in the Dominican Republic and Costa Rica, was complemented and used as input was the CFSR obtained by the SWAT modelling online data system. The Global Weather Data (SWAT) is a product from the National Center for Environmental Prediction (NCEP) Climate Forecast System Reanalysis (CFSR). These have been continuously run over the 36-year period of 1979 through 2014, and continuously updated in real time. The CFSR was designed and executed as a global, high resolution, coupled atmosphere-ocean-land surface-sea ice system to provide the best estimate of the state of these coupled domains. These results have been used in several publications and evaluated with good validation results in different climates.

The results from the analysis undertaken in three basins in the Dry Corridor indicate that the foreseen impact will be severe in the programme areas, with a total predicted decrease in average rainfall of 11%, which is equivalent to a decrease of 655 mm/year under RCP 4.5 for 2050 and reaching up to 15% under RCP 8.5 for 2050. This decrease will also likely result in a significant delay in the timing of peak annual rainfall, with a shift from the month of May to the month of June, with important implications for agricultural activities. Additionally, climate change projections also predict substantial changes in the main hydrological variables in the programme areas, with a significant decrease between 4.1% and 5.7% in soil humidity and 18% to 25% in percolation for the Choluteca basin. These variables are critical for ecosystem maintenance and also for the sustainability of the agricultural activity and soil stability. Results from the hydrological model are included in the Feasibility Study in Annex 2 and Annex 31 of this proposal.

# GREEN CLIMATE FUND FUNDING PROPOSAL V.2.1

## Lending and Guarantee Facilities’ Financing Scheme

By providing a blended financing mechanism composed of concessional finance from GCF in combination with a market rate senior debt as co-financing from CABEI (and an associated guarantee facility), incentives are created for financial institutions on different levels to engage in EbA finance. Intermediary Financial Institutions in CABEI's network will manage the EbA lending facility to local partner financial institutions such as cooperatives, small banks, NGOs, rural community banks, among others - Partner Financial Institutions. PFIs will on-lend to end beneficiaries, targeting community-level investment, rural MSMEs, and water- and resource-efficient technologies.

Partner Financial institutions will access the EbA Lending facility at concessional below-market rates for direct on-lending to final customers for EbA investments. The facility aims to invest in initiatives that improve the climate resilience of rural communities by implementing integrated catchment management, improving hydrological flow, and reducing demand for scarce water resources.

Eligibility criteria for PFIs to access the EbA lending facility are outlined in Section 6.7.1 of Annex 23 and in section B4. Technical Assistance in EbA finance methodologies will be a condition precedent to gain access to the EbA Lending facility and Guarantee facility.

## Guarantee Facility

The Guarantee Facility will be managed as a specific window under a recently established Guarantee fund managed by CABEI. It will secure PFI’s on-lending and mobilize additional lending by mitigating against credit risks associated with commercial EbA Finance. The guarantee facility will issue capped partial credit risk guarantees to IFIs for their on-lending to PFI portfolios. The EbA Guarantee will be established as a liquid facility managed directly by CABEI with a US$30.0 million guarantee funded by GCF.

## Technical Assistance (TA) Facility

The financial facilities will be accompanied by a TA facility to develop capacity within municipalities and PFIs to monitor and assess the implementation of EbA and water/resource-efficient investment projects. FAO will be the Executing Entity for this TA Facility and will receive technical support from UNEP.

## Implementation Phases

The deployment of the loan facility will follow a phased approach to ensure cohesive and substantive impact. The three implementation phases are:

1. **Launching the loan facility**: Initially deployed through local PFIs in the Tempisque basin.
2. **Replicating the loan facility**: Loans will be disbursed within the initially prioritized municipalities and other surrounding municipalities.
3. **Scale up of the loan facility**: The loan facility will be expanded geographically to cover additional municipalities within the Dry Corridor.

## Demand Analysis

The potential of the lending facility was modeled at the regional and national levels. REDCAMIF provides a centralized database with data on its members’ performance, representing 130 microfinance institutions. The microfinance sector’s involvement in inclusive agricultural finance is a good proxy for its ability to serve EbA finance.

The independent TAP agrees that any intervention should first be validated against a site-specific catchment plan that, for each catchment, explains its specific climate challenges; how the challenges are affecting ecosystem service provision; how this makes communities vulnerable; how these vulnerabilities to hazards can be mapped spatially; and what interventions are needed to maintain or restore these specific ecosystem services, to reduce vulnerability. The validation of the demonstration interventions should confirm the expected adaptation impact, underpinned by a climate rationale for each catchment, and accompanied by a clear EbA plan for each catchment. Only by having a robust validation process can the independent TAP be certain of the truly “indicative” nature of these interventions.

EbA loan programme – The funding proposal envisages that GCF funding would also support a lending mechanism for EbA as well as water- and resource-efficient technologies in all seven countries. This would be accomplished by providing a blended financing mechanism composed of concessional finance from GCF in combination with market rate debt as co-financing from CABEI. The blended rate is intended to create incentives for regional and local financial institutions to engage in EbA finance. IFIs in CABEI’s established network would manage the EbA lending facility via direct on-lending to local PFIs such as cooperatives, small banks, non-government organizations, rural community banks, among others. PFIs in turn would on-lend to end beneficiaries. The goal is for investments to be targeted across the entire Dry Corridor/Arid Zone regions of the seven countries (not just the seven catchments receiving grant support) towards community-level investment, rural micro, small and medium-sized enterprises or other institutions that can foster EbA, as well as water- and energy-efficient technologies, for end beneficiaries across different sectors. The loan programme is not limited to the catchment areas in the grant portion of the funding proposal and does not rely on the grant-funded interventions for its success. In response to a question from the independent TAP on the link between the catchment-specific grants and the wider loans, the AE noted that the deployment of the loan facility will follow a phased approach starting first in a select group of five municipalities in the identified catchments and expanding from there geographically to cover additional municipalities. According to the AE, this phased approach will also allow for more time to strengthen capacities of the participating financial institutions during the initial stages and to promote the financial mechanism with communities and producers. The additional municipalities for the loan expansion are to be selected based on proper vulnerability assessments and climate impact projections as well as the analysis of demand and the potential for replication with the participating financial institutions to ensure economies of scale in the learning process and the internal processes already developed in the initial municipalities.

### 1.3 Paradigm shift potential Scale: Medium

Corporate or large agricultural producers in the Dry Corridor region, where they exist, usually apply climate risk management strategies. According to the funding proposal, PFIs across the region already have lending programmes for more sophisticated customers, including funding for interventions that the funding proposal categorizes as EbA. However, this is regularly not the case for small and medium-sized farming units. Increased resilience of such farming units could play a role in mitigating agricultural losses due to climate impacts, along with biodiversity loss, water shortages, poverty and food insecurity.

The proposed programme intends to catalyse a paradigm shift in the regional approach to catchment and water demand management, as well as managing climate change-intensified hazards such as drought, heatwaves, flood and hurricanes, in the Dry Corridor of Central America and the arid zones of the Dominican Republic. This shift will see governments promoting large-scale EbA investments as well as increased private sector participation, including by commercial farmers and entrepreneurs in rural communities who would then have access to the financial resources and technical skills required to implement impactful adaptation interventions.

It is the view of the independent TAP that a lending programme could offer a building block for future expansion if (i) new and additional demand for EbA finance can be confirmed; (ii) there is a path to financial sustainability for this expanded lending sector; and (iii) the links between the interventions financed through the lending programme and the proposed EbA interventions and the expected climate impacts are clear. The paradigm shift nature of the proposal is further enhanced by building the technical and institutional capacity of PFIs and non-regulated financial institutions to finance and re-finance EbA and water-efficient technologies by the proposed financial instruments.

In the view of the independent TAP, the highest potential for paradigm shift comes from the ability to contribute to improving the regulatory framework and policies that could catalyse EbA demand and acceptance. The funding proposal envisages enhancing the local evidence base for EbA through the creation of a regional knowledge hub, through which tailored knowledge products will be created and disseminated. Additionally, the monitoring and evaluation of project activities will support national and local level decision-makers to implement policies and incentives for EbA. Decision-makers within local government institutions will be trained to assess and update existing regulatory frameworks and policies to facilitate the mainstreaming of EbA, including making provision for incentives programmes targeting the adoption of EbA practices. By providing technical support to local governments for the adoption and implementation of climate-resilient land management practices, the project will help to mainstream climate adaptation into local-level development planning.

Comprehensiveness: The funding proposal intends to create an enabling environment that capitalizes on the scalability of EbA, focusing on three core areas of support: financial, policy and technical. If successful, the programme will diversify financial systems in the region to create an enabling environment for investment in EbA and water-efficient technologies. This will be based on the establishment of several financial instruments with various entry points to make climate finance more accessible to vulnerable communities. The enabling environment for investment will be further enhanced by building the technical and institutional capacity of PFIs and non-regulated financial institutions to finance and re-finance EbA and water-efficient technologies through all three of the proposed financial instruments.

The independent TAP is of the opinion that the overall approach of the programme is comprehensive in marrying capacity-building, technical support and finance. However, in the absence of further preparatory work, the paradigm shift potential would be significantly limited for the grant facility by the large degree of uncertainty remaining about whether the grants and loans will in fact reduce the vulnerability of Dry Corridor farming communities to climate change impacts, given the gaps in the climate rationale and EbA plans for catchments. For this reason, the endorsement of the grant facility by the independent TAP is contingent upon the review and acceptance of the condition around the completion of a validation process, and binding agreement that any intervention should first be validated against a site-specific catchment plan prior to funding. For the loan facility, which is applied beyond the seven catchments, in the wider Dry Corridor/Arid Zone in the seven countries, it is even more difficult to show that the disparate loans covering a wide range of interventions in varying geographies add up to effective climate change adaptation. This is offset, however, by the paradigm shift involved in developing the capacity of the IFIs and PFIs to tailor their loan portfolio to effectively managing climate risk. For this reason, the independent TAP considers the paradigm shift potential to be Medium.

### 1.5 Needs of the recipient Scale: High

From an overall perspective, the Latin America and the Caribbean region — under which the seven beneficiary countries fall — has achieved an average annual economic growth rate of 3.2 per cent over the last ten years. However, this growth rate varies substantially between the countries and even within countries between urban and rural communities. Rural communities have little or no access to adaptation finance, which limits their adaptive capacity. By improving access to financial resources for adaptation interventions, the adaptive capacity of rural communities in the Dry Corridor will be increased. With increased financial and technical capacity, communities will be able to withstand the negative impacts of severe droughts, flood and severe storms.

Owing to the different socioeconomic, development and environmental contexts of the different participating countries, their respective needs for climate finance differ. With regards to the loan and guarantee financial mechanisms being proposed by the AE, the participating countries will likely have different existing capacities for the uptake of EbA finance, meaning that some will be able to utilize higher levels of finance than others. There will also be different levels of demand for EbA finance in each country. To that end, the on-the-ground interventions to be implemented in the target municipalities in each country were selected on the basis of extensive stakeholder engagement, availability of area for implementation and appropriateness of interventions.

At the local level, vulnerable rural communities do not have the financial resources, knowledge base or technical capacity to (i) develop, implement and maintain EbA and other adaptation measures; (ii) co-ordinate the frequent, cross-sectoral, multi-stakeholder engagement that is necessary for EbA and other adaptation interventions; and (iii) capitalize on EbA interventions by generating new private income streams from the ecosystem goods and services produced by restored climate-resilient ecosystems.

The national budgets of Dry Corridor countries are limited, and poverty eradication and sustainable development have been prioritized for funding over climate change action. There is a high dependency on non-reimbursable international support to meet climate change goals across the region where the finance available is far below what is required to meet adaptation needs. The added burden of the COVID-19 pandemic on the economies of these countries will leave even fewer funds available for climate action in the participating countries. The proposed programme will provide access to technical and financial resources, knowledge products and government support structures that will enable rural communities to adapt effectively to climate change in the Dry Corridor. For all these reasons, the independent TAP considers the needs of the recipient to be High.

The social, political, economic and environmental conditions of the countries occupying the dry corridor, especially the countries of Central America, remain a trigger for the extreme poverty experienced by a part of their population, especially the rural and indigenous population. The focus of the gender action plan under this activity will be to ensure equal participation and needs consideration when implementing EbA activities in the rural communities of the target areas.

#### Specific Activities Include:
- At the landscape and farm levels, ensure that women specific needs are considered in the decision-making processes for establishing the exact areas of intervention.
- At the farm level, ensure that the beneficiaries of the implementation of EbA measures include farms owned or managed by women.
- Guarantee that women are hired for the on-the-ground implementation of EbA pilot measures.
- Develop a gender-sensitive monitoring & evaluation plan for the EbA pilot activities.

#### Indicators:
- % of women producers, representatives or organizations that participate in the decision-making process for selecting exact areas of intervention at the landscape and farm levels.
- % of farms benefited by the EbA pilot measures at the farm level that are owned or managed by women.
- % of women that are hired for the on-the-ground implementation of EbA pilot activities.
- Number of monitoring & evaluation plans that are drafted considering the needs expressed by women and that measure the differentiated impacts for both women and men.

#### Targets:
- At least 30% of the participants in the decision-making processes for selecting exact areas of intervention at the landscape and farm levels are women.
- At least 10% of the farms benefited by the EbA pilot measures at the farm level are owned or managed by women.
- At least 30% of the workforce hired for the on-the-ground implementation of EbA pilot activities are women.

### Activity 2.2. Implement Demonstration Water- and Resource-Efficient Technologies
